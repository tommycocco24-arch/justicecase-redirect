Black-Letter Rule

Even where minimum contacts may exist, a state court's exercise of personal jurisdiction violates due process if it is unreasonable under the World-Wide Volkswagen fairness factors. When the defendant is foreign, its burden weighs heavily and courts must also weigh the interests of other nations and federal foreign-relations policy. No majority agreed on the stream-of-commerce test. The four-Justice plurality held that placing a product into the stream of commerce, even with awareness that it will reach the forum, is not purposeful availment without "additional conduct" aimed at the forum State.

Facts

  • Accident: Gary Zurcher lost control of his Honda motorcycle in Solano County, California. He was severely injured and his wife, a passenger, was killed. He alleged that a sudden loss of air and an explosion in the rear tire caused the crash, and that the tire, tube, and sealant were defective.
  • Parties: Zurcher sued Cheng Shin, the Taiwanese tube manufacturer, among others. Cheng Shin cross-claimed for indemnity against Asahi, the Japanese maker of the tube's valve assembly.
  • Settlement: Zurcher's claims settled and were dismissed. Only Cheng Shin's indemnity claim against Asahi remained.
  • Asahi's sales: Asahi made valve assemblies in Japan and sold them to Cheng Shin and other tire makers. Sales to Cheng Shin took place in Taiwan, and goods shipped from Japan to Taiwan. Cheng Shin bought between 100,000 and 500,000 Asahi assemblies a year from 1978 to 1982. Those sales were 1.24% of Asahi's income in 1981 and 0.44% in 1982.
  • California market: Cheng Shin alleged that about 20% of its U.S. sales were in California. In one Solano County store, 21 of about 115 tubes had Asahi valve stems, and 12 of those 21 were in Cheng Shin tubes.
  • Awareness: A Cheng Shin manager swore that Asahi knew its valves would end up throughout the United States and in California. Asahi's president swore that Asahi never contemplated being sued in California.
  • No forum presence: Asahi had no offices, agents, employees, or property in California. It did not advertise or solicit business there. It did not create, control, or employ the distribution system that brought its valves into California. No contract between Asahi and Cheng Shin was in the record.
  • Long-arm statute: California's statute reaches to the limits of the state and federal constitutions.

Procedural Posture

The Superior Court denied Asahi's motion to quash service. The Court of Appeal issued a writ ordering the motion granted. The California Supreme Court reversed, finding jurisdiction based on Asahi's intentional placement of components into the stream of commerce combined with its awareness that some would reach California. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does the Due Process Clause permit a California court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a foreign component manufacturer on an indemnity claim by a foreign co-defendant, where the manufacturer knew its components would reach California in the stream of commerce?

Holding

No. Exercising jurisdiction over Asahi would offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice" given the severe burden on the alien defendant and the slight interests of the plaintiff and California, so the judgment was reversed and remanded (Justice O'Connor; unanimous as to Part I; eight Justices joined Part II-B on reasonableness; Parts II-A and III were a four-Justice plurality of O'Connor, the Chief Justice, Powell, and Scalia).

Reasoning

  • Framework (Part II-A, plurality): Under Burger King and International Shoe, the "constitutional touchstone" is whether the defendant purposefully established minimum contacts with the forum. Per Hanson v. Denckla, contacts must arise from the defendant's own act of purposeful availment and create a "substantial connection" (McGee) with the forum.
  • *Reading World-Wide Volkswagen (plurality):* That case rejected jurisdiction based on a consumer's unilateral act of bringing a product into the forum. It said foreseeability is not "wholly irrelevant," and that jurisdiction is proper over a corporation that delivers products into the stream of commerce "with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State." Lower courts split on what that means.
  • Choosing a side of the split (plurality): The plurality rejected the view of Bean Dredging, Hedrick, and the California Supreme Court that awareness is enough. It adopted the view reflected in the Court of Appeal and Humble v. Toyota that something more is required.
  • "Stream of commerce plus" test (plurality): Placing a product in the stream of commerce, "without more," is not purposeful direction toward the forum. Awareness that the stream "may or will" carry the product into the forum does not change that. Examples of additional conduct showing intent to serve the forum market:
  • Designing the product for the forum market.
  • Advertising in the forum.
  • Establishing channels for regular advice to customers in the forum.
  • Marketing through a distributor that agreed to serve as sales agent in the forum.
  • Application (plurality): Even assuming Asahi knew its valves would end up in California, it did none of these things. It had no forum presence and no control over distribution, and no evidence showed it designed its product for California.
  • Reasonableness factors (Part II-B, majority): Drawing from World-Wide Volkswagen, a court must weigh:
  • The burden on the defendant.
  • The forum State's interests.
  • The plaintiff's interest in obtaining relief.
  • The interstate judicial system's interest in efficient resolution of controversies.
  • The shared interest of the several States in furthering fundamental substantive social policies.
  • Burden (majority): The burden was severe. Asahi had to travel from Japan and submit to a foreign legal system. The "unique burdens" of defending in a foreign legal system deserve "significant weight."
  • Plaintiff's interest (majority): Slight. Cheng Shin is Taiwanese, the underlying transaction occurred in Taiwan, and Cheng Shin did not show that California was more convenient than Taiwan or Japan.
  • Forum's interest (majority): Considerably diminished because the plaintiff is not a California resident. The California Supreme Court defined the State's consumer-safety interest too broadly. This dispute concerns indemnification, not safety standards. It was also unclear that California law would govern (citing Shutts and Allstate v. Hague). Safety deterrence will still reach Asahi through its customers, who remain subject to California tort law.
  • International factor (majority): The "several States" factor becomes the procedural and substantive interests of other nations plus the federal government's foreign-relations interests. Courts should be unwilling to find serious burdens on alien defendants outweighed by minimal plaintiff or forum interests. The Court quoted Justice Harlan's dissent in United States v. First National City Bank that "great care and reserve" is needed when extending personal jurisdiction internationally.
  • Independence of II-B: The majority found jurisdiction unreasonable "even apart from the question of the placement of goods in the stream of commerce."

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Brennan (joined by White, Marshall, Blackmun), concurring in part and in the judgment: He joined Parts I and II-B but rejected II-A. The stream of commerce means the "regular and anticipated flow" of products from manufacture to retail sale. A participant aware that its final product is marketed in the forum cannot be surprised by suit there, and it benefits economically and legally from forum sales. No additional conduct is required. II-A is a "marked retreat" from World-Wide Volkswagen, which distinguished goods reaching a State through distribution chains from goods a consumer carries there, and which cited Gray v. American Radiator approvingly. Most circuits had followed his view. He would find minimum contacts here, but agreed this is one of the "rare cases" where fairness defeats jurisdiction despite purposeful forum activity (Burger King).
  • Justice Stevens (joined by White, Blackmun), concurring in part and in the judgment: He joined Parts I and II-B but not II-A, for two reasons. First, II-A was unnecessary because the reasonableness finding alone requires reversal, and Burger King shows a minimum-contacts analysis is not always needed. Second, even if a test were stated, II-A misapplied it. No "unwavering line" separates awareness from purposeful availment. The question depends on "the volume, the value, and the hazardous character of the components." He would usually find that a regular course of dealing delivering over 100,000 units a year for several years is purposeful availment, even for a standard product sold worldwide.

Notes

  • Split on stream of commerce: No test commanded a majority. Four Justices adopted "stream of commerce plus" (O'Connor). Four rejected it in favor of awareness-based stream of commerce (Brennan). Stevens offered a volume/value/hazard approach. White and Blackmun joined both concurrences.
  • Narrow majority holding: The only majority ruling on due process is Part II-B. Jurisdiction over Asahi was unreasonable on these facts given the international context, the alien defendant's heavy burden, and the slight interests of a foreign plaintiff and the forum.
  • Expressly left open: Whether Congress could, consistent with Fifth Amendment due process, authorize federal-court jurisdiction over alien defendants based on aggregate national contacts instead of contacts with the State where the court sits.
  • Relied on: International Shoe and Milliken v. Meyer (fair play and substantial justice); Burger King (minimum contacts touchstone; purposeful availment; fairness can defeat jurisdiction); Hanson v. Denckla (purposeful availment); McGee (substantial connection); Keeton v. Hustler (purposeful direction); World-Wide Volkswagen (foreseeability; stream-of-commerce language; reasonableness factors); Shutts and Allstate v. Hague (choice of law doubts); United States v. First National City Bank (Harlan, J., dissenting).
  • Lower-court split as described: The plurality rejected Bean Dredging (CA5) and Hedrick v. Daiko Shoji (CA9). It aligned with Humble v. Toyota (CA8), Hutson v. Fehr Bros. (CA8), and Max Daetwyler (CA3). It cited Hicks v. Kawasaki and Rockwell International with "cf." on distribution control and product design.
  • Overruled: None.
