Black-Letter Rule

To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion under Rule 8(a)(2), a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face, meaning factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable. Courts apply a two-pronged approach. First, disregard legal conclusions and threadbare recitals of the elements, which get no assumption of truth. Second, assume the well-pleaded factual allegations are true and ask whether they plausibly give rise to an entitlement to relief. The Twombly standard governs all civil actions.

Facts

  • Parties: Javaid Iqbal, a Pakistani citizen and Muslim, sued 34 current and former federal officials and 19 "John Doe" corrections officers. Only former Attorney General John Ashcroft and FBI Director Robert Mueller (petitioners) were before the Court.
  • Detention: After September 11, Iqbal was arrested on criminal fraud charges, designated "of high interest" to the investigation, and placed in the ADMAX SHU at the MDC in Brooklyn: lockdown 23 hours a day, the remaining hour in handcuffs and leg irons with a four-officer escort.
  • Later events: Iqbal pleaded guilty, served his sentence, and was removed to Pakistan. He then filed a Bivens action in the Eastern District of New York.
  • Allegations against others: Jailors allegedly beat him, subjected him to serial strip and body-cavity searches, and refused to let him pray. These claims were not before the Court.
  • Allegations against petitioners: The FBI, "under the direction of" Mueller, arrested and detained thousands of Arab Muslim men (¶ 47). Ashcroft and Mueller approved the policy of holding post-September-11 detainees in highly restrictive conditions until "cleared" by the FBI (¶ 69). Both "knew of, condoned, and willfully and maliciously agreed to subject" Iqbal to harsh conditions "solely on account of" religion, race, and/or national origin (¶ 96). Ashcroft was the "principal architect" of the policy and Mueller was "instrumental" in it (¶¶ 10-11).
  • Scope of claim: The complaint did not challenge the arrest or confinement in the general population. It challenged treatment in the ADMAX SHU.

Procedural Posture

The District Court, applying Conley v. Gibson's no-set-of-facts test, denied petitioners' motion to dismiss on qualified immunity grounds. On interlocutory appeal, the Second Circuit applied a "flexible plausibility standard" under Twombly and affirmed, with Judge Cabranes concurring and urging Supreme Court review. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Did Iqbal's complaint plead sufficient factual matter, taken as true, to state a claim under Rule 8(a)(2) that Ashcroft and Mueller purposefully discriminated against him on account of race, religion, or national origin?

Holding

No. The complaint's allegations of discriminatory purpose are conclusory and not entitled to the assumption of truth, and the remaining factual allegations do not plausibly show that petitioners adopted the detention policy for a discriminatory purpose. Reversed and remanded, with the Court of Appeals to decide in the first instance whether to remand to the District Court so Iqbal can seek leave to amend. (Kennedy, J., for the Court; Souter, Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer, JJ., dissenting.)

Reasoning

  • Appellate jurisdiction (threshold). Subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be waived, so the Court addressed it.
  • 28 U.S.C. § 1291 allows appeals from "final decisions." Under the collateral-order doctrine (Cohen), a narrow class of prejudgment orders qualifies.
  • A denial of qualified immunity that "turns on an issue of law" is immediately appealable (Mitchell). That includes denials at the motion-to-dismiss stage (Behrens).
  • Iqbal argued the court could review only whether the alleged wrong violated clearly established law, and not the sufficiency of the pleadings. The Court rejected this, relying on Hartman and Wilkie. Pleading sufficiency is "inextricably intertwined with" (Swint) and "directly implicated by" the immunity defense.
  • Johnson v. Jones is distinguished. Johnson barred appeal of a summary-judgment order resting on a fact-based dispute over a vast record. Reviewing a complaint's four corners is an abstract legal question within appellate competence.
  • Elements of the claim.
  • Bivens is the federal analog to § 1983. Implied actions are disfavored. The Court assumed without deciding that a First Amendment free-exercise claim is actionable under Bivens, because petitioners did not press the point.
  • Vicarious liability does not apply in Bivens or § 1983 suits (Monell, Dunlop, Robertson). A plaintiff must plead that each official, through the official's own individual actions, violated the Constitution.
  • Invidious discrimination under the First and Fifth Amendments requires discriminatory purpose (Lukumi, Washington v. Davis). Purpose means acting "because of," not merely "in spite of," adverse effects on a group (Feeney).
  • "Supervisory liability" is a "misnomer." A supervisor's knowledge of and acquiescence in a subordinate's discriminatory purpose is insufficient. Supervisors, like subordinates, must themselves act with discriminatory purpose.
  • Pleading standard (Rule 8(a)(2)).
  • Rule 8 does not require "detailed factual allegations." It demands more than "labels and conclusions," a "formulaic recitation of the elements," or "naked assertion[s]" devoid of "further factual enhancement."
  • Plausibility is not a "probability requirement." It asks for more than a "sheer possibility." Facts "merely consistent with" liability fall short.
  • Plausibility is a "context-specific task" drawing on "judicial experience and common sense."
  • Rule 8 departed from code pleading, but it "does not unlock the doors of discovery for a plaintiff armed with nothing more than conclusions."
  • Twombly illustrates the two prongs. The bare allegation of conspiracy was a legal conclusion. Parallel conduct was consistent with conspiracy but was more likely explained by lawful market behavior.
  • Application, prong one. The ¶ 96 "knew of, condoned, and willfully and maliciously agreed" allegation and the "principal architect" and "instrumental" labels are a formulaic recitation of the Feeney purpose element. They are disregarded because they are conclusory. The Court expressly said it was not rejecting them as unrealistic or fanciful.
  • Application, prong two.
  • The ¶ 47 arrests: an "obvious alternative explanation" exists. The attackers were Arab Muslim members of al Qaeda, so a legitimate investigative policy would produce a disparate, incidental impact on Arab Muslims. Discrimination is not plausible.
  • The ¶ 69 detention policy: even if the arrests were plausibly discriminatory, the claim targets the ADMAX SHU policy. That allegation suggests only that officials sought to hold suspected terrorists in secure conditions until cleared. Iqbal did not argue that motive is unconstitutional.
  • Unlike Twombly, there is no respondeat superior to bind petitioners. The complaint has no factual allegation plausibly suggesting their own discriminatory state of mind.
  • Iqbal's three counterarguments, all rejected.
  • Twombly is limited to antitrust. No. Twombly interpreted Rule 8, which governs "all civil actions" under Rule 1.
  • Pleading should be relaxed because discovery can be cabined. No. Pleading sufficiency does not turn on discovery controls (Twombly). This matters especially for qualified immunity, which aims to spare officials "disruptive discovery" (Siegert). The costs of diversion are magnified during a national security emergency. Deferring discovery for petitioners still burdens them, because they would need to participate as discovery proceeds against others.
  • Rule 9(b) allows intent to be alleged "generally." No. "Generally" is relative to Rule 9(b)'s particularity requirement for fraud or mistake. Rule 9 excuses a heightened standard but leaves Rule 8 operative.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Souter, J., dissenting (joined by Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer, JJ.):
  • Supervisory liability. Petitioners conceded that knowledge plus deliberate indifference would support liability. The Court should not override a party's concession, especially without briefing on a question that has divided the circuits. The majority treats the choice as respondeat superior versus nothing. That is a false dichotomy given the spectrum of circuit tests: knowledge and acquiescence, facilitate or condone, reckless supervision, gross negligence. The majority effectively eliminates Bivens supervisory liability, though its ruling on that point does not drive the outcome. Overriding the concession is unfair to Iqbal.
  • Pleading. Twombly requires taking allegations as true however skeptical the judge, except for fantastic claims such as "little green men." Souter agrees that ¶¶ 47 and 69 alone do not state a claim. The majority errs by reading ¶¶ 10, 11, and 96 in isolation. Read with the specific allegations that named FBI officials implemented a discriminatory "high interest" policy (¶¶ 47-53), they are not conclusory and give fair notice. The majority also has no principled basis for crediting ¶ 69 as factual while treating ¶ 96 as conclusory.
  • Breyer, J., dissenting: Joins Souter in full. Agrees that unwarranted litigation should not interfere with government work. Argues that trial courts have case-management tools for that purpose, such as starting discovery with lower-level defendants before reaching high-level officials. Neither the briefs nor the majority shows those tools are inadequate. Would affirm.

Notes

  • Limits of the holding:
  • The Court decided only the sufficiency of the complaint against Ashcroft and Mueller. It expressed no opinion on the claims against the other defendants, whose alleged misconduct it called "serious."
  • The Court assumed without deciding that the First Amendment claim is actionable under Bivens.
  • The Court did not decide whether Iqbal may amend. It left to the Court of Appeals, in the first instance, whether to remand for leave to amend.
  • Scope of the pleading rule: Twombly's plausibility standard applies to "all civil actions," including discrimination suits. It is not limited to antitrust.
  • Conley: The majority recounts that the District Court applied Conley's no-set-of-facts test, and that the Second Circuit acknowledged Twombly had "retired" that test. The Iqbal majority applies Twombly and does not purport to overrule anything itself.
  • Relied on:
  • Pleading: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, Papasan v. Allain, Copperweld.
  • Appealability: Cohen, Mitchell v. Forsyth, Behrens v. Pelletier, Harlow v. Fitzgerald, Hartman v. Moore, Wilkie v. Robbins, Swint, Arbaugh, Cotton.
  • Bivens and liability: Malesko, Davis v. Passman, Bush v. Lucas, Monell, Dunlop v. Munroe, Robertson v. Sichel.
  • Discriminatory purpose: Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye, Washington v. Davis, Personnel Administrator v. Feeney.
  • Litigation burdens: Siegert v. Gilley (Kennedy, J., concurring in judgment).
  • Distinguished:
  • Johnson v. Jones: fact-based summary-judgment order, not appealable; abstract pleading question, appealable.
  • Twombly facts: corporate respondeat superior was available there and is not here.
  • Rule 9(b): "Generally" excuses particularity for states of mind. It does not license conclusory pleading under Rule 8.
