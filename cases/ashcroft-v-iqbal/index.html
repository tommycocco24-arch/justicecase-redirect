Black-Letter Rule

To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face. This means factual content that lets the court reasonably infer the defendant is liable, which is more than a sheer possibility of unlawful conduct. Courts apply Twombly's two-pronged approach in all civil actions: (1) set aside conclusory allegations, which get no presumption of truth, and (2) ask whether the remaining well-pleaded facts plausibly give rise to an entitlement to relief.

Facts

  • Parties: Javaid Iqbal, a Pakistani citizen and Muslim, sued former Attorney General John Ashcroft and FBI Director Robert Mueller, along with 34 current and former federal officials and 19 "John Doe" corrections officers.
  • Detention: After 9/11, the FBI arrested Iqbal on identity-document fraud and conspiracy charges. He was designated "of high interest" to the investigation and held in the ADMAX SHU at the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn. Detainees there were locked down 23 hours a day.
  • Claims against others: The complaint alleged that jailors beat him, subjected him to unjustified strip and body-cavity searches, and interfered with prayer. Those defendants were not before the Court.
  • Claims against petitioners: The complaint alleged that Ashcroft and Mueller "knew of, condoned, and willfully and maliciously agreed" to subject him to harsh confinement "solely on account of" religion, race, or national origin. It named Ashcroft the "principal architect" and Mueller "instrumental" in the policy.
  • Factual allegations: The complaint alleged that the FBI, under Mueller's direction, arrested and detained thousands of Arab Muslim men. It also alleged that Ashcroft and Mueller approved holding post-9/11 detainees in highly restrictive conditions until the FBI "cleared" them.
  • Scope: The complaint did not challenge Iqbal's arrest or his confinement in the general population. It challenged only his treatment in the ADMAX SHU.

Procedural Posture

Iqbal filed a Bivens action in the E.D.N.Y. The district court applied Conley v. Gibson's no-set-of-facts test and denied petitioners' motion to dismiss on qualified immunity grounds. On interlocutory appeal, the Second Circuit applied a "flexible plausibility standard" from the newly decided Twombly and affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Did Iqbal's complaint plead sufficient factual matter, taken as true, to state a plausible claim that Ashcroft and Mueller purposefully discriminated against him on account of race, religion, or national origin in violation of clearly established constitutional rights?

Holding

No. Under Twombly's plausibility standard, which governs all civil actions under Rule 8, the complaint's allegations against petitioners were conclusory, and the remaining facts did not plausibly show discriminatory purpose. The judgment was reversed and remanded, with the Second Circuit to decide in the first instance whether to remand so Iqbal could seek leave to amend. Justice Kennedy wrote for the Court, and four Justices dissented.

Reasoning

  • Appellate jurisdiction (collateral-order doctrine):
  • Under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and Cohen, a denial of qualified immunity that turns on an issue of law is immediately appealable (Mitchell v. Forsyth; Behrens).
  • Qualified immunity is both a defense to liability and an entitlement not to face the burdens of litigation.
  • Whether a complaint sufficiently pleads a clearly established violation is "inextricably intertwined with" the immunity defense (Swint; Hartman; Wilkie).
  • **Johnson v. Jones distinguished:**
  • Johnson held unappealable a summary-judgment order resting on "fact-based" questions that required review of a vast pretrial record.
  • Judging pleading sufficiency is an "abstract" legal question. It is confined to the four corners of the complaint and is within an appellate court's core competency.
  • Substantive elements come first:
  • As in Twombly, the Court first identified what must be pleaded.
  • Bivens is the federal analog to § 1983. Neither allows vicarious liability (Monell; Dunlop; Robertson).
  • A plaintiff must therefore plead that each official, through the official's own individual actions, violated the Constitution.
  • Discriminatory purpose required:
  • First and Fifth Amendment discrimination claims require purpose (Lukumi; Washington v. Davis).
  • Purpose means acting "because of," not merely "in spite of," adverse effects on a group (Feeney).
  • Iqbal's knowledge-and-acquiescence theory of "supervisory liability" was rejected. The term is a "misnomer" without vicarious liability. A supervisor, like a subordinate, needs purpose, and knowledge does not suffice.
  • *Rule 8(a)(2) standard, drawn from Twombly:*
  • Rule 8 does not require detailed factual allegations.
  • It does demand more than "labels and conclusions," a "formulaic recitation of the elements," or "naked assertion[s]" devoid of "further factual enhancement."
  • Plausibility is less than probability and more than possibility.
  • Facts "merely consistent with" liability fall short.
  • Plausibility is a context-specific inquiry that draws on judicial experience and common sense.
  • Two-pronged approach:
  • Prong one: identify and disregard conclusions not entitled to the assumption of truth.
  • Prong two: assume the truth of well-pleaded facts and ask whether they plausibly give rise to relief.
  • In Twombly, the bare allegation of "conspiracy" was a conclusion. The well-pleaded parallel conduct was more likely explained by lawful market behavior.
  • Prong one applied:
  • The "knew of, condoned, and willfully and maliciously agreed," "principal architect," and "instrumental" allegations were a formulaic recitation of the elements of the claim.
  • The Court rejected them for being conclusory. It expressly did not reject them as unrealistic or fanciful.
  • Prong two applied:
  • The allegations of mass arrests of Arab Muslim men and of approval of restrictive confinement were consistent with purposeful discrimination.
  • The "obvious alternative explanation" was a legitimate policy aimed at people suspected of links to attacks carried out by Arab Muslim al Qaeda members, producing a disparate, incidental impact.
  • Even a plausible inference of discriminatory arrest would not help, because Iqbal challenged only the ADMAX SHU confinement policy. Nothing plausibly suggested petitioners adopted that policy for discriminatory reasons.
  • The complaint suggested only that top officials sought to keep suspected terrorists in secure conditions until cleared.
  • Respondent's three arguments rejected:
  • Twombly is not limited to antitrust. It interpreted Rule 8, which governs "all civil actions" under Rule 1.
  • Careful case management or cabined discovery does not relax the pleading standard (Twombly). This holds especially where qualified immunity aims to spare officials "disruptive discovery" (Siegert). Deferring discovery for petitioners would not work because they would still have to take part to protect their position. A complaint deficient under Rule 8 earns no discovery, "cabined or otherwise."
  • Rule 9(b) lets intent be alleged "generally." "Generally" is relative to Rule 9(b)'s particularity requirement for fraud or mistake. It excuses a heightened standard and does not license evading Rule 8 with conclusions.
  • Policy:
  • Litigation diverts officials' time from governing.
  • These costs are magnified for high-level officials responding to a national security emergency.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Souter, dissenting (joined by Justices Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer):
  • On supervisory liability: Petitioners conceded they would be liable if they had actual knowledge of subordinates' discrimination and were deliberately indifferent to it. The majority overrode that concession without briefing and "eliminat[ed] Bivens supervisory liability entirely." Souter argued this was unnecessary to the result and unfair to Iqbal, who had relied on the concession. He also argued the majority assumed a false choice between respondeat superior and no supervisory liability, ignoring a spectrum of circuit tests.
  • On pleading: Under the concession, the complaint satisfied Rule 8(a)(2). Twombly does not let judges disbelieve factual allegations unless they are fantastic, such as "little green men." The majority erred by reading the "knew of, condoned" allegations in isolation. Read with the specific allegations that named FBI officials implemented a discriminatory policy, they were not conclusory and gave fair notice. The majority also treated ¶ 69 (approval of restrictive conditions) as factual, and Souter saw no principled basis for treating the parallel discrimination allegations as conclusory.
  • Justice Breyer, dissenting:
  • Joined Souter and wrote separately. He agreed that unwarranted litigation should not interfere with government work.
  • He argued that need does not justify the majority's reading of Twombly and Rule 8. Trial courts have other tools, such as sequencing discovery to begin with lower-level defendants.
  • Neither the briefs nor the majority showed those tools to be inadequate.
  • He would affirm.

Notes

  • Limits:
  • The holding addresses only the claims against Ashcroft and Mueller.
  • The Court expressed no opinion on the sufficiency of the complaint against the other defendants. It acknowledged that those allegations describe "serious official misconduct."
  • Left open:
  • The Court assumed without deciding that a First Amendment religious-discrimination claim is actionable under Bivens. It noted it has not found a Free Exercise damages remedy and declined to extend Bivens in Bush v. Lucas.
  • Whether Iqbal may amend was left to the Second Circuit.
  • Not a credibility test: The Court disclaimed rejecting allegations as implausible because they were fanciful. Conclusory form is the problem.
  • **Conley v. Gibson:* The opinion describes Twombly as having "retired" Conley*'s no-set-of-facts test, which the district court had applied.
  • Relied on:
  • Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly (plausibility, two-prong illustration, case management no answer)
  • Papasan v. Allain
  • Cohen, Mitchell v. Forsyth, Behrens, Hartman, Wilkie, Swint (collateral-order appealability)
  • Harlow v. Fitzgerald (qualified immunity)
  • Malesko, Davis v. Passman, Bush v. Lucas (Bivens scope)
  • Monell, Dunlop, Robertson (no vicarious liability)
  • Lukumi, Washington v. Davis, Feeney (discriminatory purpose)
  • Siegert v. Gilley (avoiding disruptive discovery)
  • Wright & Miller (Rule 9(b))
  • Distinguished:
  • Johnson v. Jones: fact-based summary-judgment order versus an abstract pleading question.
  • Twombly's facts: there respondeat superior could bind the corporate defendant. Here each petitioner had to act with discriminatory purpose personally.
  • Rejected the Second Circuit's view: The Second Circuit read Twombly as a "flexible" standard requiring amplification only in some contexts. The Court rejected that reading, though it agreed plausibility is context-specific.
  • Rule 9(b) interplay: Rule 9(b) allows intent to be alleged "generally." Rule 8's plausibility requirement still applies to those allegations.
