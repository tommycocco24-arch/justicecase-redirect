Black-Letter Rule

Under the First Amendment, a state may not impose blanket suppression of attorney advertising, and it may not prevent a lawyer from publishing a truthful newspaper advertisement about the availability and terms (including prices) of routine legal services. The state keeps the power to restrain false, deceptive, or misleading advertising, to impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions, and to suppress advertising of illegal transactions. A disciplinary rule adopted and actively enforced by a state supreme court as sovereign is state action exempt from the Sherman Act under Parker v. Brown.

Facts

  • Parties: Appellants Bates and O'Steen were Arizona-licensed attorneys and therefore members of the appellee, the State Bar of Arizona, an integrated bar created under the direction and control of the Arizona Supreme Court.
  • The practice: In 1974 they opened a "legal clinic" in Phoenix to serve people of moderate income who did not qualify for government legal aid. They took only routine matters, such as uncontested divorces, uncontested adoptions, simple personal bankruptcies, and name changes. They kept costs down with paralegals, automatic typewriting equipment, and standardized forms and procedures. Low per-case returns meant they depended on volume.
  • The ad: After two years, they concluded the practice could not survive unless low-cost availability, and fees in particular, were advertised. They ran a newspaper ad in the Arizona Republic offering "legal services at very reasonable fees" and listing fees for specific services (e.g., uncontested divorce, $175 plus a $20 filing fee).
  • The rule: Disciplinary Rule 2-101(B), incorporated in Arizona Supreme Court Rule 29(a) and derived from the ABA Code of Professional Responsibility, barred lawyers from publicizing themselves through newspaper ads and other commercial publicity. Appellants conceded the ad clearly violated it.
  • Record on price: Appellee's counsel said uncontested divorces in the area ran $150 to $300. The State Bar's own Legal Services Program set the rate at $250 and had participating attorneys perform similar services at standardized rates.

Procedural Posture

After a complaint by the State Bar president, a local administrative committee recommended suspensions of at least six months, and the Board of Governors recommended one-week suspensions. The Arizona Supreme Court rejected the Sherman Act and First Amendment challenges, but reduced the sanction to censure because the ad was a good-faith test of the rule; the U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Does Arizona's disciplinary rule barring attorney advertising violate the Sherman Act or the First Amendment as applied to appellants' truthful newspaper advertisement of prices for routine legal services?

Holding

Yes on the First Amendment, no on the Sherman Act: the rule is exempt from the Sherman Act under Parker v. Brown because it is the affirmative command of the state supreme court acting as sovereign, but its application to appellants' truthful newspaper ad for routine legal services violates the First Amendment; the judgment was affirmed in part and reversed in part (opinion by Justice Blackmun).

Reasoning

Sherman Act (Part II)

  • Parker v. Brown held that the Sherman Act does not reach restraints imposed by a state "as sovereign" as an act of government.
  • _Goldfarb_ distinguished. There the state had not required the bar associations' minimum-fee schedule. Here the restraint is the affirmative command of the Arizona Supreme Court, the ultimate body wielding the state's power over law practice. It is therefore "compelled by direction of the State acting as a sovereign."
  • _Cantor_ distinguished on three grounds:
  • Defendant's identity. Cantor involved a private utility. Here the claim runs against the state; the Arizona Supreme Court is the real party in interest, and the State Bar acts as its agent under continuous supervision.
  • State regulatory interest. The state in Cantor had no independent interest in the light-bulb market. Regulating lawyers is at the core of the state's power to protect the public, and controls on attorney solicitation and advertising have long been subject to state oversight.
  • Clear articulation and active supervision. The Cantor program originated with the utility and the commission merely acquiesced. Here the rules clearly articulate state policy and are subject to pointed re-examination by the policymaker in enforcement proceedings.
  • Goldfarb itself disclaimed any intent to diminish state authority to regulate professions. Allowing this Sherman Act challenge would have that effect.

First Amendment (Part III)

  • Starting point: _Virginia Pharmacy_. That case protected price advertising for prescription drugs, emphasizing the consumer's and society's interest in the free flow of commercial information and rejecting paternalistic justifications that rest on keeping people ignorant. The Court said its conclusion here "might be said to flow a fortiori" from that case. It still took up the lawyer-specific arguments because Virginia Pharmacy had reserved judgment on other professions.
  • Narrowed question. The Court set aside quality claims, in-person solicitation, and bare directory information (name, address, hours). The "heart of the dispute" is price advertising for routine services.
  • Six justifications, each rejected:
  • Professionalism. The link between advertising and eroding professionalism is "severely strained." Clients know lawyers earn a living, and the ABA's own EC 2-19 calls for early fee agreements. Bankers and engineers advertise without loss of dignity. The ban began as a rule of etiquette, and "habit and tradition" cannot answer a constitutional challenge.
  • Inherently misleading. Only routine services lend themselves to fixed-price ads. Lack of standardization is refuted by the Bar's own standardized-rate program and former fee schedules. Clients can identify the service they want at the level of generality ads provide. Incomplete information beats none, and the remedy is more disclosure.
  • Administration of justice. The Court rejected the idea that it is always better to suffer a wrong silently. The bar conceded the middle 70% of the population is underserved, and advertising can address fear of cost and difficulty finding a lawyer.
  • Economic effects. Arguments about overhead and entry barriers do not distinguish lawyers and are not relevant to the First Amendment. Advertising may lower prices, and the ban entrenches established lawyers who rely on community contacts.
  • Quality of service. A lawyer inclined to cut quality will do so regardless of the ad rule. Standardized fees do not mean undesirably standardized service, and clinics may reduce error.
  • Enforcement difficulty. Most lawyers will keep their oaths, and honest lawyers have an interest in weeding out abusers.
  • No overbreadth. Overbreadth exists to prevent chilling fragile speech. Commercial speech is hardy because it is tied to profit, and the advertiser can readily judge its own speech's truth. As "strong medicine," overbreadth does not apply to professional advertising. Appellants therefore had to show their own ad was protected.
  • This ad was not misleading.
  • "Legal clinic" would be readily understood, and the Bar itself had a committee exploring the concept.
  • "Very reasonable fees" was accurate given the $150 to $300 range and the Bar program's $250 rate.
  • Failing to disclose that a client could change a name without a lawyer was not shown to be misleading, since most legal services can lawfully be self-performed.

Permissible regulation (Part IV)

  • False, deceptive, or misleading ads may be restrained. The leeway given untruthful speech elsewhere has little force in commercial speech.
  • Because the public lacks sophistication about legal services, misstatements tolerable in other ads may be inappropriate in legal ads.
  • Quality claims may be so likely to mislead as to warrant restriction, and similar objections may justify limits on in-person solicitation.
  • Warnings or disclaimers may be required even for ads like this one.
  • Reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions are allowed, ads for illegal transactions may be suppressed, and broadcast advertising warrants special consideration.
  • In a footnote answering the dissents, the Court said the bar may define the services that must be included in an advertised package, such as an uncontested divorce.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Chief Justice Burger (concurring in part, dissenting in part): He joined Part II and generally agreed with Justice Powell.
  • Virginia Pharmacy rested on standardized, prepackaged drugs. Legal services rarely if ever can be standardized, and clients rarely know in advance what they need. Price ads can therefore be "a trap for the unwary."
  • The Court never defines "routine" services, and existing disciplinary machinery is inadequate to police lawyer advertising.
  • He would let the organized bar experiment with publishing probable fee ranges for narrowly defined services, with a statement that actual fees vary.
  • Justice Powell, joined by Justice Stewart (concurring in part, dissenting in part): He joined Part II and dissented on the First Amendment.
  • This case presents the very question Virginia Pharmacy reserved. Professional services carry a vastly greater potential for deception and are far harder to regulate than tangible products.
  • The routine/unique line is unworkable. An "uncontested divorce" can involve alimony, custody, support, and property issues, and the diagnostic and advisory function is the most important part of the service.
  • "Very reasonable" is opinion, not verifiable fact. Reasonableness turns on factors like those in DR 2-106(B), not just market averages.
  • The bar cannot police such ads effectively. Constitutionalizing the issue cuts off state experimentation.
  • He agreed ads for initial consultation fees are not inherently misleading and would also allow hourly-rate ads with a disclosure that total cost depends on hours spent.
  • He highlighted the Court's reservations (quality claims, time/place/manner, disclaimers) as possible limits, and argued time, place, and manner limits should reach further for professions than for products.
  • Justice Rehnquist (dissenting in part): He joined Parts I and II and dissented from Part III and the judgment.
  • Largely for his Virginia Pharmacy dissent's reasons, he would hold that commercial advertising is outside the First Amendment, relying on Valentine v. Chrestensen.
  • He agreed with Powell's critique but would not join Powell's suggestion that some legal advertising is protected. The Valentine line was sound and workable, and he refused to take "even one step down the 'slippery slope.'"

Notes

  • Narrow holding as stated: The constitutional issue was "only whether the State may prevent the publication in a newspaper of appellants' truthful advertisement concerning the availability and terms of routine legal services." The Court held the "present application" of the rule unconstitutional.
  • Expressly left open:
  • Advertising claims about the quality of legal services.
  • In-person solicitation (hospital room, accident site, runners).
  • The special problems of electronic broadcast advertising.
  • Whether warnings or disclaimers may be required even for ads like this one.
  • Many questions about the line between deceptive and nondeceptive ads, with the bar expected to play a "special role."
  • Audience matters: A footnote says whether an ad is misleading depends on the legal sophistication of its audience, so different degrees of regulation may be appropriate in different areas.
  • Overbreadth: The Court declined to apply First Amendment overbreadth analysis to professional advertising. A challenger must show that their own ad is protected.
  • Precedents relied on:
  • Virginia Pharmacy Board v. Virginia Consumer Council (commercial speech protection; anti-paternalism; "commonsense differences" for commercial speech).
  • Bigelow v. Virginia.
  • Parker v. Brown (state-action exemption).
  • Broadrick v. Oklahoma (overbreadth as "strong medicine").
  • NAACP v. Button and the union legal-services cases, which protect access to the courts and undercut the view of lawsuits as an evil in themselves.
  • Precedents distinguished:
  • Goldfarb v. Virginia State Bar: the state did not compel the fee schedule there.
  • Cantor v. Detroit Edison Co.: private defendant, no state regulatory interest, and state acquiescence only.
  • Williamson v. Lee Optical, Semler v. Dental Examiners, and Head v. New Mexico Board: the Court notes none resolved a First Amendment issue. Those cases upheld professional advertising limits against due process, equal protection, and commerce challenges.
  • Overruling noted: The Court cites Cohen v. Hurley for the state's interest in regulating lawyers. A footnote observes it was overruled in other respects by Spevack v. Klein. Bates itself overrules nothing.
  • Claims not before the Court: Equal protection, vagueness, and due process challenges raised below were not pressed.
  • Sanction: The Arizona Supreme Court had reduced the penalty to censure. Justice Rehnquist stayed the censure pending the Court's decision.
