Black-Letter Rule

A state may not impose a blanket ban on lawyer advertising. Under the First Amendment, a state may not discipline a lawyer for a truthful newspaper advertisement of the availability and prices of routine legal services. It may still restrain false, deceptive, or misleading advertising, impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions, and suppress advertising of illegal transactions. A state supreme court's disciplinary rule restricting lawyer advertising is immune from Sherman Act attack under the Parker v. Brown state-action exemption, because the restraint is the State's own clearly articulated and actively supervised command.

Facts

  • Parties: Appellants Bates and O'Steen were licensed Arizona attorneys and, as such, members of appellee State Bar of Arizona, an integrated bar.
  • The practice: After working at legal aid, they opened a "legal clinic" in Phoenix in 1974. It served moderate-income clients who did not qualify for government legal aid. They took only routine matters (uncontested divorces, uncontested adoptions, simple personal bankruptcies, name changes), kept costs down with paralegals, standardized forms, and office procedures, and depended on high volume.
  • The ad: After two years they concluded the clinic could not survive without advertising fees. In 1976 they ran a newspaper ad offering "legal services at very reasonable fees" and listing prices, including an uncontested divorce for $175 plus a $20 filing fee.
  • The rule: Appellants conceded the ad violated DR 2-101(B), incorporated by Arizona Supreme Court Rule 29(a). The rule barred lawyers from publicizing themselves through newspaper ads and other commercial publicity. It derived from the ABA Code of Professional Responsibility.
  • State's own practices: The State Bar sponsored a Legal Services Program with standardized fees for similar services, including $250 for an uncontested divorce. Appellee's counsel said area uncontested divorces ran $150 to $300.

Procedural Posture

After a bar complaint, a hearing committee recommended suspensions of at least six months, and the Board of Governors recommended one-week suspensions. The Arizona Supreme Court rejected appellants' Sherman Act and First Amendment claims but reduced the sanction to censure because the ad was a good-faith test of the rule, and the U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Is Arizona's disciplinary rule banning lawyer advertising invalid under §§ 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act, or under the First Amendment as applied to appellants' truthful newspaper advertisement of prices for routine legal services?

Holding

No as to the Sherman Act, because the rule is state action exempt under Parker v. Brown; yes as to the First Amendment, because truthful advertising of the availability and terms of routine legal services may not be subjected to blanket suppression; affirmed in part and reversed in part (opinion by Justice Blackmun).

Reasoning

Sherman Act (Part II)

  • Parker v. Brown held the Sherman Act does not reach restraints a State imposes "as sovereign." The Court applied it here.
  • Goldfarb is distinguishable. There, the State did not require the bar associations' minimum-fee schedule. Here, the restraint is the "affirmative command" of the Arizona Supreme Court, the ultimate body wielding the State's power over the practice of law. It is "compelled by direction of the State acting as a sovereign."
  • Cantor is distinguishable on three grounds:
  • Defendant's identity. Cantor involved a private utility. Here the claim runs against the State. The Arizona Supreme Court is the real party in interest, and the State Bar acts as its agent under continuous supervision.
  • State regulatory interest. In Cantor the State had no independent interest in the light-bulb market. Regulating lawyers is at the core of state power, and advertising and solicitation have long been under state oversight.
  • Source and supervision of the policy. The Cantor program was instigated by the utility with mere state acquiescence. Here the disciplinary rules are a "clear articulation" of state policy, and the policymaker actively reexamines them in enforcement proceedings.
  • Allowing the Sherman Act challenge would diminish state authority to regulate professions, which Goldfarb expressly disclaimed.

First Amendment (Part III)

  • Starting point. Virginia Pharmacy protected commercial speech proposing a mundane transaction. It stressed the listener's and society's interest in the free flow of information and rejected "highly paternalistic" bans that rest on keeping consumers ignorant. The Court said the result here "might be said to flow a fortiori" from Virginia Pharmacy. It still addressed the lawyer-specific arguments, since Virginia Pharmacy had reserved the question of other professions.
  • Narrow framing. The Court set aside three matters: quality claims, in-person solicitation, and basic directory information. The question was price advertising for routine services.
  • Six justifications rejected:
  • Professionalism. The link to eroding professionalism is "severely strained." Clients know lawyers charge fees. The ABA requires early fee disclosure (EC 2-19), so condemning the same disclosure before the client arrives is inconsistent. The ban began as etiquette, and "habit and tradition" cannot answer a constitutional challenge.
  • Inherently misleading. Only routine services lend themselves to fixed-price ads. The State Bar's own fixed-rate program refutes the claim that no standardization is possible. Clients can identify the service they want at the level of generality ads provide. Incomplete information beats none, and the preferred remedy is more disclosure.
  • Administration of justice. It is not always better to suffer a wrong silently. The bar itself conceded that the middle 70% of the population is underserved, and advertising helps address fear of cost and difficulty finding a lawyer.
  • Economic effects. These arguments do not distinguish lawyers and are not relevant to the First Amendment. The ban insulates lawyers from price competition and entrenches established lawyers who rely on community contacts.
  • Quality of service. A lawyer inclined to do shoddy work will do so regardless of advertising rules. Standardized clinics may even reduce error.
  • Enforcement difficulty. It is incongruous to praise lawyers' integrity and then predict they will mislead. Most will stay honest and help weed out abusers.
  • No overbreadth. Overbreadth is "strong medicine" (Broadrick). Its anti-chilling rationale applies weakly to commercial speech, which is hardy because it is tied to profit and is verifiable by the speaker. The Court therefore declined to apply overbreadth and examined appellants' own ad.
  • The ad as applied. The State Bar called three features misleading, and the Court rejected each:
  • The public would understand "legal clinic."
  • "Very reasonable" was accurate given the $150 to $300 local range and the Bar's own $250 rate.
  • Failing to say a client could change a name without a lawyer was not shown to mislead on this record. Most legal services can be self-performed (Faretta).

Permissible regulation (Part IV)

  • States may restrain false, deceptive, or misleading ads. Commercial speech gets less leeway for falsity because it is calculated and the advertiser knows the product.
  • Because the public lacks sophistication about legal services, misstatements tolerable in other ads may be inappropriate in legal ads.
  • Quality claims may be so likely to mislead as to warrant restriction. Similar objections might justify restraints on in-person solicitation.
  • States may require warnings or disclaimers and impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions. They may suppress ads for illegal transactions (Pittsburgh Press). Broadcast advertising warrants special consideration.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Chief Justice Burger (concurring in part, dissenting in part): Agreed with Part II and generally with Powell. Read Virginia Pharmacy as resting on standardized, prepackaged drugs. Argued legal services can rarely if ever be standardized, so price ads give an inaccurate picture and become "a trap for the unwary." Faulted the majority for never defining "routine" and for relying on disciplinary machinery he called wholly inadequate. Called the decision a "great leap." Preferred letting the organized bar experiment with programs announcing probable fee ranges for narrowly defined services, with disclaimers that actual fees vary and a grievance procedure.
  • Justice Powell, joined by Justice Stewart (concurring in part, dissenting in part): Joined Part II. Dissented on the First Amendment for two reasons. First, legal services cannot be sorted into "routine" and "unique." Even an "uncontested" divorce can involve custody, support, and property issues, and the lawyer's diagnostic and advisory work is the most valuable part. Second, price ads for services cannot be tested empirically, so policing is impractical among roughly 400,000 lawyers. Argued "very reasonable" is opinion rather than verifiable fact, and the reasonableness of a fee turns on the DR 2-106(B) factors, not market averages. Would permit advertising of initial-consultation fees and hourly rates with a disclosure that total charges depend on hours spent. Emphasized the majority's reservations (quality claims, time/place/manner, disclaimers) as possible limits. Argued time/place/manner rules should reach further for professional services. Invoked federalism and state experimentation.
  • Justice Rehnquist (dissenting in part): Joined Parts I and II. Dissented from Part III and the judgment. Relied on Valentine v. Chrestensen and his Virginia Pharmacy dissent. Would hold that commercial advertising, however truthful, is outside the First Amendment. Agreed with Powell that the majority gives little guidance. Refused to join Powell's implication that some lawyer advertising is protected, because once Valentine is abandoned, case-by-case line-drawing is unavoidable.

Notes

  • Narrow holding. The Court ruled only that a State may not prevent newspaper publication of appellants' truthful ad about the availability and terms of routine legal services.
  • Expressly left open:
  • Advertising claims about the quality of legal services.
  • In-person solicitation (hospital room, accident site, runners).
  • Special problems of electronic broadcast advertising.
  • Where the line between deceptive and nondeceptive ads falls. The Court said "many of the problems" remain and the bar has a "special role."
  • Whether warnings or disclaimers may be required even for ads like this one.
  • Misleadingness is audience-dependent. The legal sophistication of the audience matters, so different degrees of regulation may suit different areas. The bar may also define what an advertised package (e.g., uncontested divorce) must include.
  • Overbreadth. The doctrine does not apply to professional commercial advertising. Challengers must show their own speech is protected.
  • Relied on: Parker v. Brown (state-action exemption). Virginia Pharmacy (commercial speech protection, anti-paternalism, falsity may be regulated, time/place/manner). Bigelow v. Virginia. Broadrick v. Oklahoma (overbreadth as "strong medicine"). Goldfarb's statement of the State's strong interest in regulating lawyers. Pittsburgh Press (ads for illegal transactions). NAACP v. Button and the union legal-services cases (access to courts is not an evil).
  • Distinguished: Goldfarb (anticompetitive conduct not compelled by the State). Cantor (private defendant, no state regulatory interest, private instigation). On Williamson v. Lee Optical, Semler, and Head: the Court noted these upheld professional advertising restrictions against due process, equal protection, and commerce challenges and did not resolve any First Amendment issue.
  • Overruled: None. The Court noted Cohen v. Hurley, cited for state interest, had been overruled in other respects by Spevack v. Klein.
  • PR doctrine tie-ins: The Court leaned on the ABA Code. EC 2-19 requires early fee agreements. EC 2-1 says the bar should facilitate intelligent selection of lawyers. DR 2-102(A)(6) already permitted directory listings, including initial-consultation fees.
