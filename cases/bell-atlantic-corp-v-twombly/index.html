Black-Letter Rule

Under Rule 8(a)(2), a complaint survives a Rule 12(b)(6) motion only if it pleads enough factual matter, taken as true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face. Labels, conclusions, and a formulaic recitation of the elements do not count. For a Sherman Act § 1 claim, allegations of parallel conduct plus a bare assertion of conspiracy fail; the parallel conduct must be placed in a context that raises a suggestion of a preceding agreement.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiffs Twombly and Marcus represent a putative class of local telephone and high-speed Internet subscribers. Defendants are the four ILECs ("Baby Bells") formed from the 1984 AT&T divestiture, allegedly controlling 90 percent or more of local telephone service in the 48 contiguous States.
  • Regulatory backdrop: The Telecommunications Act of 1996 ended the ILECs' regional monopolies and required them to share their networks with competitors (CLECs). The ILECs vigorously litigated the scope of that obligation.
  • Theory one: The ILECs engaged in parallel conduct to inhibit CLECs, including unfair access agreements, inferior connections, overcharging, and sabotaging CLECs' customer billing. Plaintiffs alleged that one ILEC's failure to resist would have shown how well CLECs could compete elsewhere.
  • Theory two: The ILECs agreed not to compete in each other's territories. Plaintiffs inferred this from their common failure to pursue attractive opportunities in neighboring markets and from Qwest CEO Notebaert's statement that such competition "might be a good way to turn a quick dollar but that doesn't make it right."
  • Ultimate allegation: "Upon information and belief," and "in light of" the parallel conduct, the ILECs entered into a "contract, combination or conspiracy" and agreed not to compete with one another.
  • Scope: The complaint alleged no specific time, place, or person involved in any agreement, only a span beginning in 1996 and continuing to the present.

Procedural Posture

The S.D.N.Y. dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to plead facts tending to exclude independent self-interested conduct. The Second Circuit reversed, holding that plus factors need not be pleaded and that dismissal requires concluding there is "no set of facts" that would show collusion; the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does a § 1 complaint survive a motion to dismiss when it alleges parallel anticompetitive conduct by competitors, absent factual context suggesting agreement as distinct from identical, independent action?

Holding

No. A § 1 complaint must allege enough factual matter, taken as true, to plausibly suggest an agreement, and allegations of parallel conduct with a conclusory assertion of conspiracy do not meet that standard; Justice Souter wrote for the Court, which reversed the Second Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion.

Reasoning

  • Substantive antitrust law sets the target. Section 1 reaches only restraints effected by a contract, combination, or conspiracy. The crucial question is whether conduct stems from independent decision or from agreement, tacit or express (Theatre Enterprises; Copperweld). Parallel conduct, even conscious parallelism, is not itself unlawful (Brooke Group).
  • Parallel conduct is ambiguous. It is consistent with conspiracy and equally consistent with rational, unilateral business strategy driven by common perceptions of the market. The Court has guarded against false inferences at trial (Theatre Enterprises), in proof generally (Monsanto), and at summary judgment (Matsushita). This case asks the antecedent pleading question.
  • The pleading standard, broken into parts:
  • Rule 8(a)(2) requires a "showing" of entitlement to relief, giving fair notice of the claim and the grounds on which it rests.
  • Detailed factual allegations are not required.
  • Labels, conclusions, and a formulaic recitation of elements are insufficient. Courts need not accept legal conclusions couched as factual allegations (Papasan).
  • Factual allegations must raise a right to relief above the speculative level.
  • All factual allegations are assumed true, even if doubtful in fact. A judge's disbelief is no ground for dismissal (Neitzke), and a complaint may proceed even if recovery seems remote and unlikely (Scheuer).
  • Plausibility is not a probability requirement. It calls for enough fact to raise a reasonable expectation that discovery will reveal evidence of illegal agreement.
  • Two lines to cross (fn. 5). A complaint must cross from conclusory to factual and from factually neutral to factually suggestive. Parallel conduct alone, like a naked assertion of conspiracy, "stops short of the line between possibility and plausibility."
  • Policy: discovery costs. Antitrust discovery is expensive, and the threat of discovery expense pushes defendants to settle weak cases (Dura; Blue Chip Stamps; Associated General Contractors). Deficient claims should be exposed at the point of minimum expenditure. Case management, summary judgment, and jury instructions come too late to solve the problem, and judicial supervision of discovery has had modest success (Easterbrook).
  • *Retiring Conley's "no set of facts."* Read literally, the phrase would let a wholly conclusory complaint survive whenever some undisclosed facts might later support recovery. Judges and commentators had long refused to read it that way. The Court held the phrase "has earned its retirement" and reread it as describing how an adequately stated claim may be supported at proof, not the minimum standard for a complaint's survival.
  • Application to theory one. Each ILEC had a powerful, independent incentive to resist being forced to subsidize competitors. Resisting competition is routine market conduct. If parallel resistance sufficed, pleading a § 1 violation against almost any group of competitors would be a sure thing. The "one success would reveal the others" premise does not answer the point that each ILEC would resist regardless of the others.
  • Application to theory two. An obvious alternative explanation exists: former government-sanctioned monopolists were sitting tight, expecting neighbors to do the same. The complaint did not allege that CLEC competition was more lucrative than other opportunities, and it described the near-insurmountable barriers CLECs faced. Congress's disappointed expectation of ILEC cross-entry does not make conspiracy plausible.
  • Conclusory agreement allegations disregarded. The few statements alleging agreement were, on fair reading, legal conclusions resting on the parallel-conduct allegations.
  • Notebaert statement. The District Court could take judicial notice of the full articles quoted in the complaint (Fed. R. Evid. 201), which showed he called CLEC entry an unsustainable economic model (fn. 13).
  • **Swierkiewicz distinguished.** That case struck down a heightened pleading requirement demanding specific facts beyond those needed to state a claim. Here the Court required no heightened fact pleading of specifics, only enough facts to state a plausible claim.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Stevens, J., dissenting, joined by Ginsburg, J., except as to Part IV. Rule 8 was drafted to abandon code pleading's fact/conclusion distinction, and the majority revives it. The allegation that defendants agreed not to compete is a factual allegation that must be accepted as true; "defendants entered into a contract" is less of a legal conclusion than Form 9's "defendant negligently drove." Conley's "no set of facts" language was a pleading standard, cited approvingly in 16 opinions and adopted by 26 States and D.C.; its retirement belongs to the rulemaking process, and no party asked for it. Matsushita is a summary judgment rule, and Swierkiewicz shows that an evidentiary burden does not become a pleading burden. Leatherman and Swierkiewicz hold that discovery costs are managed through case management and summary judgment. In antitrust cases, where proof lies with the alleged conspirators, early dismissal should be granted sparingly. The Notebaert statement and trade-association contacts support a plausible inference of agreement, and the court should have construed the ambiguous statement in plaintiffs' favor. Stevens would have required an answer and limited discovery, such as depositions of Notebaert and an executive from each defendant. Part IV (Stevens alone) argues the decision contradicts the intent behind the Sherman Act, the 1996 Act, and the Federal Rules, and is driven by distrust of trial judges' ability to control discovery.

Notes

  • Framing of the holding. The Court stated the holding as an application of Rule 8's general standards "to a § 1 claim." The dissent observed that whether the test will benefit all civil defendants or only antitrust defendants "is a question that the future will answer." The majority did not address that question.
  • Not a heightened standard (fn. 14). The Court expressly disclaimed applying any heightened pleading standard or broadening Rule 9, which can be done only by amending the Rules (Swierkiewicz; Leatherman). Rule 9(b)-(c) particularity remains a separate requirement.
  • Not probability. Plausibility does not ask whether proof is likely. A well-pleaded complaint proceeds even if a judge thinks proof improbable.
  • What would suffice (fn. 4). The parties agreed that "complex and historically unprecedented changes in pricing structure made at the very same time by multiple competitors, and made for no other discernible reason" would support a plausible inference of conspiracy. Commentators describe parallel behavior unlikely to result from chance, independent responses to common stimuli, or mere interdependence.
  • Context matters. In a traditionally unregulated industry with low entry barriers, sparse competition among firms dominating separate regions "could very well signify illegal agreement." The regulatory monopoly history supplied the obvious alternative explanation here.
  • Notice point (fn. 10). Even taken alone, the agreement allegations likely gave inadequate notice: no identified ILEC, employee, time, or place, in contrast to Form 9.
  • **Conley v. Gibson:* "No set of facts" language retired and recast as a gloss on how an adequately stated claim may be supported. The Court said its analysis comports with post-Conley statements and that Conley* itself properly found the complaint there adequate.
  • Relied on: Theatre Enterprises, Monsanto, Matsushita, Brooke Group, Copperweld (substantive § 1 law); Papasan (legal conclusions); Scheuer, Neitzke, Swierkiewicz (allegations taken as true); Dura, Blue Chip Stamps, Associated General Contractors (discovery cost, specificity before massive litigation); DM Research (1st Cir.), Car Carriers (7th Cir.).
  • Distinguished: Swierkiewicz (heightened specific-fact pleading, as opposed to plausibility).
  • Rejected: The Second Circuit's reliance on Nagler v. Admiral Corp. for the proposition that pleading parallel conduct alone states a § 1 claim (fn. 7).
  • Acknowledged with caution: Poller's counsel to be careful before dismissing antitrust complaints in advance of discovery.
  • Disposition: Reversed and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion.
