Black-Letter Rule

Under Rule 8(a)(2), a complaint survives a Rule 12(b)(6) motion only if it pleads enough factual matter, taken as true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face. Labels, conclusions, and a formulaic recitation of the elements will not do. For a Sherman Act § 1 claim, allegations of parallel conduct must be placed in a context that suggests a preceding agreement, since parallel conduct alone is equally consistent with independent action.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiffs represent a putative class of local telephone and high-speed Internet subscribers. Defendants are the four ILECs (Baby Bells) that remained after the 1984 AT&T divestiture and later mergers. Together they allegedly control 90 percent or more of the local telephone market in the 48 contiguous states.
  • Regulatory backdrop: The Telecommunications Act of 1996 ended the ILECs' regional monopolies. It required them to share their networks with competitive local exchange carriers (CLECs) at wholesale rates. The ILECs vigorously litigated the scope of that sharing duty.
  • Theory one (keeping CLECs out): The complaint alleges that the ILECs engaged in parallel conduct to inhibit CLECs. The alleged conduct included unfair access agreements, inferior connections, overcharging, and billing designed to sabotage CLEC customer relations. It also alleges that collusion was needed because one CLEC success would have shown how CLECs could succeed elsewhere.
  • Theory two (not competing with each other): The complaint alleges that the ILECs failed to pursue attractive opportunities in one another's contiguous markets. It quotes Qwest CEO Richard Notebaert saying that competing in another ILEC's territory "might be a good way to turn a quick dollar but that doesn't make it right."
  • Ultimate allegation: "Upon information and belief," and "in light of" the parallel conduct and noncompetition, the ILECs "have entered into a contract, combination or conspiracy" and "have agreed not to compete with one another."
  • Scale: The suit covered at least 90 percent of subscribers in the continental U.S. It was brought against the country's largest telecom firms and alleged unspecified violations over seven years.

Procedural Posture

The Southern District of New York dismissed for failure to state a claim. It held that parallel conduct alone does not state a § 1 claim. The Second Circuit reversed. It held that plus factors need not be pleaded and that dismissal required a conclusion that "no set of facts" could show collusion. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does a § 1 complaint survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when it alleges parallel anticompetitive conduct and a bare assertion of conspiracy, without factual context suggesting agreement as distinct from identical, independent action?

Holding

No. A § 1 complaint must plead enough facts to make an agreement plausible, and allegations of parallel conduct plus a conclusory assertion of conspiracy do not cross the line from conceivable to plausible. The Second Circuit's judgment was reversed and the case remanded (Souter, J., for the Court).

Reasoning

  • Substantive baseline: Section 1 reaches only restraints effected by a contract, combination, or conspiracy (Copperweld). The crucial question is whether conduct stems from independent decision or from agreement, tacit or express (Theatre Enterprises). Even conscious parallelism is "not in itself unlawful" (Brooke Group).
  • Hedging against false inferences at later stages: The Court has already guarded against inferring conspiracy from parallel conduct at several points. Parallel conduct alone does not support a directed verdict (Theatre Enterprises). Proof must tend to exclude independent action (Monsanto). At summary judgment, the evidence must tend to rule out independent action (Matsushita). This case asks the antecedent pleading question.
  • The Rule 8(a)(2) standard, broken into parts:
  • Detailed factual allegations are not required.
  • The complaint must show the "grounds" of entitlement to relief. Labels, conclusions, and a formulaic recitation of the elements are insufficient. Courts need not accept "a legal conclusion couched as a factual allegation" (Papasan).
  • Factual allegations must raise a right to relief "above the speculative level."
  • All factual allegations are assumed true, even if doubtful in fact (Swierkiewicz, Neitzke, Scheuer).
  • The facts must state a claim that is plausible on its face. Plausibility is no probability requirement. It calls for "enough fact to raise a reasonable expectation that discovery will reveal evidence of illegal agreement." A well-pleaded complaint may proceed even if proof seems improbable and recovery "very remote and unlikely."
  • Applied to § 1: The complaint needs allegations "plausibly suggesting (not merely consistent with) agreement." Parallel conduct stays "in neutral territory" without a further circumstance pointing toward a meeting of the minds. Footnote 5 identifies two lines a complaint must cross. One runs from conclusory to factual. The other runs from factually neutral to factually suggestive.
  • Conley's "no set of facts" language retired: Read literally, the phrase would let a wholly conclusory complaint survive whenever undisclosed facts might later support recovery ("Mr. Micawber's optimism would be enough"). Judges and commentators had long questioned it. The Court read Conley in light of the concrete allegations that case had before it. It recast the phrase as a description of what a plaintiff may prove once a claim is adequately stated, and held that it does not set the minimum standard for a complaint's survival. The phrase "has earned its retirement."
  • Policy: discovery cost and settlement pressure: Antitrust discovery is expensive. Groundless claims carry "in terrorem" settlement value (Dura, Blue Chip Stamps). A deficiency should be exposed "at the point of minimum expenditure." District courts must be able to insist on some specificity before a massive factual controversy proceeds (Associated General Contractors). The Court rejected case management, summary judgment, and jury instructions as adequate checks. It reasoned that judicial supervision of discovery has had modest success and that the cost threat pushes defendants to settle before those stages (Easterbrook).
  • Application to theory one: Resisting CLECs was the natural, unilateral reaction of each ILEC protecting its turf. The 1996 Act forced the ILECs to subsidize rivals, so each had a powerful independent incentive. If parallel resistance to competition implied conspiracy, pleading § 1 against nearly any group of competitors "would be a sure thing." The "revealing success" argument still fails because each ILEC would resist regardless of what the others did.
  • Application to theory two: Sparse competition among dominant firms in separate regions might signal agreement in a traditionally unregulated, low-barrier industry. Here an "obvious alternative explanation" exists. Former government-sanctioned monopolists were "sitting tight, expecting their neighbors to do the same thing." The complaint itself describes near-insurmountable barriers to CLEC profitability. It does not allege that CLEC entry was more lucrative than other opportunities. Firms do not enter every profitable market. Congress's disappointed expectations do not make conspiracy plausible.
  • Conclusory agreement allegations disregarded: The few direct statements of agreement are legal conclusions resting on the parallel-conduct allegations. The "nub" of the complaint is parallel behavior.
  • Notebaert statement (fn. 13): The District Court could take notice of the full published articles quoted in the complaint (Fed. R. Evid. 201). In those articles Notebaert called CLEC entry not "a sustainable economic model" and said the regulatory environment was too unstable.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Stevens, J., dissenting, joined by Ginsburg, J., except as to Part IV: Parallel conduct was never the real issue, since Theatre Enterprises settled long ago that it is admissible but not itself illegal. The complaint expressly alleges agreement, which must be taken as true, and the defendants never even answered. History of Rule 8: the drafters deliberately abandoned code pleading's facts-versus-conclusions distinction, and Form 9's "defendant negligently drove" would have been a conclusion under the codes. "Defendants entered into a contract" is no more a legal conclusion. Conley's "no set of facts" rule set the minimum for a complaint's survival. Its sources (Leimer, Continental Collieries, Dioguardi) support that reading, and no prior opinion of the Court questioned it. Petitioners did not ask to retire it, and changes of that order belong to the Rules Enabling Act process. Swierkiewicz and Leatherman teach that a heightened summary-judgment burden (Matsushita) does not become a heightened pleading burden, and that discovery abuse is handled through discovery control and summary judgment. Antitrust is a poor vehicle because proof lies with the alleged conspirators (Poller, Hospital Building) and Congress encouraged private suits through treble damages. On the merits, the Notebaert quote and the trade-association contacts support a plausible inference of agreement, and the ambiguous quote had to be read in plaintiffs' favor. District courts have ample tools: Rules 12(e), 7(a), 16, 23, 26(c), and 11. Part IV, which Ginsburg did not join, argues that the decision follows no congressional command, rests on distrust of trial judges' ability to control discovery, and leaves open whether the new test helps only antitrust defendants or all civil defendants.

Notes

  • Not heightened pleading: The Court disclaims any heightened standard and any expansion of Rule 9 (fn. 14). It says Rule 9 can be broadened only by amending the Rules (Swierkiewicz, quoting Leatherman). The complaint failed "in toto" to make entitlement to relief plausible. Lack of particularity was not the defect.
  • No probability requirement: Plausibility asks for a reasonable expectation that discovery will reveal evidence. Courts still may not dismiss because a judge disbelieves the allegations or thinks recovery unlikely.
  • What would suffice (fn. 4): The parties agreed that "complex and historically unprecedented changes in pricing structure made at the very same time by multiple competitors, and made for no other discernible reason" would support a plausible inference. Commentators describe parallel behavior unlikely to result from chance, common stimuli, or mere interdependence.
  • Notice gap (fn. 10): Even taken alone, the agreement allegations named no specific time, place, or person, and did not say which ILECs or employees agreed. The Court contrasted Form 9, which gives date, place, and act.
  • Context matters: The Court tied its analysis to the telecom industry's monopoly history. It noted that sparse regional competition could signal agreement in a traditionally unregulated, low-barrier industry.
  • Open question: The majority does not expressly say whether its standard reaches beyond § 1 claims. The dissent flags the question and says "the future will answer."
  • Retired: Conley v. Gibson's "no set of facts" language. Conley's fair-notice requirement and its result are left intact.
  • Distinguished: Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. There the Second Circuit demanded specific facts beyond those needed to state a claim, which amounted to heightened pleading. Here the Court asks only for facts making the claim plausible.
  • Rejected reliance: Nagler v. Admiral Corp. (2d Cir.). The Second Circuit had cited it for the view that parallel conduct alone suffices to plead § 1. The Court said Nagler misread Theatre Enterprises and was overtaken by Monsanto and Matsushita (fn. 7).
  • Relied on: Theatre Enterprises, Monsanto, Matsushita, Copperweld, Brooke Group (substantive § 1 law); Papasan, Scheuer, Neitzke (12(b)(6) basics); Dura Pharmaceuticals and Blue Chip Stamps ("reasonably founded hope" and in terrorem settlement value); Associated General Contractors (power to insist on specificity; no assuming unpleaded facts); Wilson v. Schnettler; Wright & Miller; Areeda & Hovenkamp; Car Carriers and DM Research (circuit criticism of conclusory pleading).
  • Acknowledged: Poller's caution about dismissing antitrust complaints before discovery, which the Court balanced against discovery costs.
