Black-Letter Rule

To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, a complaint must plead "enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face." Labels, conclusions, and a formulaic recitation of the elements will not do, and factual allegations, taken as true, must raise a right to relief above the speculative level. For a Sherman Act § 1 claim, allegations of parallel conduct must be placed in a context that raises a suggestion of a preceding agreement; parallel conduct plus a bare assertion of conspiracy is insufficient.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiffs Twombly and Marcus represent a putative class of subscribers of local telephone and/or high-speed Internet service from February 1996 to the present. Defendants are four Incumbent Local Exchange Carriers (ILECs), the successors to the Baby Bells, which allegedly control 90 percent or more of the local telephone market in the 48 contiguous States.
  • Regulatory background: The Telecommunications Act of 1996 ended the ILECs' approved regional monopolies and required them to share their networks with competitive local exchange carriers (CLECs). The ILECs vigorously litigated the scope of that sharing obligation.
  • First theory: The ILECs engaged in parallel conduct in their own territories to inhibit CLECs: unfair access agreements, inferior connections, overcharging, and billing designed to sabotage CLECs' customer relations. The complaint said collusion was needed because one CLEC's success anywhere would have revealed how CLECs could succeed elsewhere.
  • Second theory: The ILECs agreed not to compete in each other's territories. Plaintiffs inferred this from the ILECs' common failure to pursue "attractive business opportunities" in neighboring markets and from Qwest CEO Richard Notebaert's statement that competing in another ILEC's territory "might be a good way to turn a quick dollar but that doesn't make it right."
  • The agreement allegation: The complaint alleged "upon information and belief" that, "in light of" the parallel conduct and lack of competition, the ILECs entered into a "contract, combination or conspiracy" and agreed not to compete. It named no specific time, place, or person involved.
  • Discovery stakes: The Court stressed that the putative class covered at least 90 percent of subscribers in the continental United States, against America's largest telecom firms, over a seven-year period.

Procedural Posture

The Southern District of New York dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6) because parallel conduct alone does not state a § 1 claim. The Second Circuit reversed, holding that "plus factors" need not be pleaded and that dismissal required a conclusion that "no set of facts" could show collusion. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does a § 1 complaint state a claim when it alleges parallel anticompetitive conduct and a conclusory assertion of conspiracy, without factual context suggesting agreement as distinct from identical, independent action?

Holding

No. Under Rule 8(a)(2), a § 1 complaint must plead enough factual matter, taken as true, to plausibly suggest that an agreement was made. Because these allegations did not cross "the line from conceivable to plausible," the complaint "must be dismissed." The Second Circuit's judgment was reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion (Souter, J., for the Court; Stevens, J., dissenting, joined by Ginsburg, J., except as to Part IV).

Reasoning

  • Substantive backdrop (§ 1 requires agreement): Section 1 reaches only restraints effected by a contract, combination, or conspiracy (Copperweld). The crucial question is whether conduct stems from independent decision or from agreement, tacit or express (Theatre Enterprises). Parallel conduct, even conscious parallelism, is "not in itself unlawful" (Brooke Group).
  • Ambiguity of parallel conduct: Parallel behavior is consistent with conspiracy and equally consistent with rational, unilateral business strategy. The Court already guards against false inferences at later stages: no directed verdict on parallelism alone (Theatre Enterprises), proof must tend to exclude independent action (Monsanto), and summary-judgment evidence must tend to rule out independent action (Matsushita). This case asks the antecedent pleading question.
  • The pleading standard, broken into parts:
  • Rule 8(a)(2) requires a "showing" of entitlement to relief that gives fair notice of the claim and the grounds on which it rests.
  • Detailed factual allegations are not required.
  • Labels, conclusions, and formulaic recitations of elements are insufficient; courts need not accept "a legal conclusion couched as a factual allegation" (Papasan).
  • Factual allegations, assumed true even if doubtful, must raise the right to relief above the speculative level.
  • Plausibility is not probability. It calls for enough fact to raise a reasonable expectation that discovery will reveal evidence of illegal agreement. A complaint may proceed even if a judge thinks proof improbable and recovery "very remote and unlikely" (Scheuer).
  • Applied to § 1: Allegations must plausibly suggest agreement. Mere consistency with agreement is not enough. Parallel conduct without more "stays in neutral territory"; it gets close to stating a claim but stops short of "the line between possibility and plausibility." In footnote 5 the Court described two borders a complaint must cross: from conclusory to factual (DM Research), and from factually neutral to factually suggestive.
  • Policy: discovery costs: Antitrust discovery is expensive, and the threat of it can push defendants to settle "anemic cases" (Dura, quoting Blue Chip Stamps: the "in terrorem increment of the settlement value"). A district court "must retain the power to insist upon some specificity in pleading" before letting a massive controversy proceed (Associated General Contractors). The Court rejected the dissent's reliance on case management, summary judgment, and jury instructions, citing Judge Easterbrook's view that judicial control of discovery abuse has been modest.
  • *Retiring Conley's "no set of facts": Read literally, Conley*'s statement that a complaint survives unless "the plaintiff can prove no set of facts" would let a wholly conclusory complaint survive on the bare possibility of later-discovered facts ("Mr. Micawber's optimism would be enough"). Courts and commentators had long declined to read it literally. The phrase "has earned its retirement." Properly understood, it describes how an adequately stated claim may be supported once pleaded. It does not set the minimum standard for a complaint's survival.
  • Application to the complaint:
  • The few statements alleging agreement are legal conclusions resting on the parallel-conduct allegations, so the complaint's "nub" is parallel behavior.
  • Resisting CLECs: Each ILEC had a powerful independent incentive to resist subsidizing competitors. Resisting competition is routine market conduct. If that sufficed, pleading § 1 against almost any group of competitors "would be a sure thing." The "one CLEC's success would reveal" theory fails because each ILEC would resist regardless of the others.
  • Not entering each other's territories: There was an "obvious alternative explanation." Former government-sanctioned monopolists were "sitting tight, expecting their neighbors to do the same thing." The complaint did not allege that CLEC entry was more lucrative than other opportunities, and its own allegations showed CLECs faced near-insurmountable barriers. Firms do not enter every potentially profitable market.
  • Notebaert statement: The district court could take notice of the full articles cited in the complaint, which quoted Notebaert calling the CLEC model unsustainable and the regulatory environment unstable (fn. 13, citing FRE 201).
  • **Swierkiewicz distinguished:** That case rejected a heightened requirement that a Title VII plaintiff plead specific facts establishing a prima facie case. Here the Court required no "heightened fact pleading of specifics," only enough facts to make the claim plausible.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Stevens, J., dissenting (joined by Ginsburg, J., except Part IV):
  • Settled law misapplied: If the question were only whether parallel conduct alone states a § 1 claim, Theatre Enterprises answered it. The complaint alleged an actual agreement, which must be taken as true, and dismissal came before defendants even answered or denied it.
  • Rule 8 history: The Rules' drafters deliberately abandoned the code-pleading distinction among facts, evidence, and conclusions. Form 9 ("defendant negligently drove") shows the notice-pleading model. "Defendants entered into a contract" is no more a legal conclusion than "negligently drove."
  • **Conley defended:* Read with its source cases (Leimer, Continental Collieries, Dioguardi), Conley* set the minimum pleading standard and permits dismissal only when further proceedings would be futile. It had been cited in 16 opinions and separate writings without criticism, and 26 States and D.C. use it. Neither petitioners nor their amici asked to retire it. Changes of that order belong to the rulemaking process (28 U.S.C. §§ 2072-2074).
  • Precedent: Scheuer, Leatherman, and Swierkiewicz reject heightening pleading burdens to address litigation costs. Matsushita's summary-judgment burden should not migrate to the pleading stage. Antitrust dismissals before discovery should be granted "very sparingly" (Hospital Building Co., Poller). Treble damages show Congress favored private enforcement.
  • Plausibility on these facts: Notebaert's statement and the trade-association allegations support a plausible inference of agreement, and the ambiguous statement had to be construed in plaintiffs' favor. Using FRE 201 to draw inferences for defendants is ironic. Lack of specificity is remedied by a Rule 12(e) motion, which petitioners never filed; they conceded the complaint was "quite specific."
  • Case management: Rules 7(a), 11, 12(e), 16, 23, and 26 give trial judges tools to control discovery. Stevens would not have allowed massive discovery here but would have required an answer and some depositions.
  • Part IV (Ginsburg did not join): The intent behind the Sherman Act, the 1996 Act, and the Federal Rules all point the other way. The decision rests on protecting wealthy antitrust defendants from discovery and on distrust of trial judges. Whether the new test will benefit all civil defendants "is a question that the future will answer."

Notes

  • Scope as the opinion frames it: The holding is stated as an application of Rule 8's general standards "to a § 1 claim." The plausibility discussion and the retirement of Conley are framed in terms of Rule 8(a)(2) itself. The dissent's Part IV flags, without resolving, whether the test reaches beyond antitrust.
  • Not a heightened standard: The Court disclaimed any heightened pleading requirement and any broadening of Rule 9, which can be changed only by amending the Rules (fn. 14, citing Swierkiewicz and Leatherman). The complaint failed "in toto" to render entitlement to relief plausible. Insufficient particularity was not the defect.
  • Not a probability test: A savvy judge's doubt about proof is not grounds for dismissal.
  • What would suffice: Footnote 4 notes the parties agreed that "complex and historically unprecedented changes in pricing structure made at the very same time by multiple competitors, and made for no other discernible reason" would support a plausible inference. The Court also observed that in a traditionally unregulated industry with low entry barriers, sparse competition among dominant firms in separate regions "could very well signify illegal agreement."
  • Notice point (fn. 10): Even read as a direct allegation of agreement, the complaint named no time, place, or person, which the Court contrasted with Form 9.
  • Precedent retired: Conley v. Gibson's "no set of facts" language. The Court did not overrule Conley's fair-notice holding and reinterpreted the passage as describing the breadth of proof available once a claim is adequately stated.
  • Precedent rejected: Nagler v. Admiral Corp. (2d Cir.), which treated parallel conduct alone as enough to plead § 1, cited Theatre Enterprises without explanation.
  • Precedent distinguished: Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A. (heightened prima facie pleading rejected; that holding is consistent with plausibility).
  • Precedent relied on:
  • Substantive antitrust: Theatre Enterprises, Monsanto, Matsushita, Copperweld, Brooke Group.
  • Pleading: Papasan (legal conclusions not accepted as true), Scheuer and Neitzke (allegations taken as true), Associated General Contractors (court may insist on some specificity), Dura and Blue Chip Stamps ("reasonably founded hope" from discovery), Wilson v. Schnettler.
  • Lower courts: Car Carriers and DM Research.
  • Precedent acknowledged: Poller, for caution before dismissing antitrust complaints before discovery.
