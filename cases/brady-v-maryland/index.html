Black-Letter Rule

The prosecution violates due process when it suppresses evidence favorable to an accused, after the accused has requested it, if that evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment. The prosecution's good faith or bad faith is irrelevant. The remedy may be limited to a new trial on punishment where, under state law, the suppressed evidence could not have affected the verdict on guilt.

Facts

  • Parties: Petitioner Brady and a companion, Boblit, were each convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to death. They were tried separately, and Brady was tried first.
  • The crime: Murder committed during a robbery. Under Maryland law the punishment was life imprisonment or death. The jury could limit the sentence to life by adding the words "without capital punishment."
  • Brady's defense: Brady testified and admitted taking part in the crime. He claimed Boblit did the actual killing. In summation, his counsel conceded first-degree murder and asked only for a verdict "without capital punishment."
  • The request: Before trial, Brady's counsel asked the prosecution to let him examine Boblit's extrajudicial statements.
  • The suppression: The prosecution showed counsel several statements. It withheld one dated July 9, 1958, in which Boblit admitted the actual homicide. Brady did not learn of it until after he had been tried, convicted, and sentenced, and after his conviction was affirmed.
  • No guile: The Maryland Court of Appeals found that the withholding was not "the result of guile."
  • Maryland jury rule: The Maryland Constitution makes juries in criminal cases "the Judges of Law, as well as of fact."

Procedural Posture

The Maryland Court of Appeals affirmed Brady's conviction. The trial court denied his motion for a new trial, his appeal was dismissed without prejudice to post-conviction relief, and the trial court then dismissed his post-conviction petition. On appeal, the Court of Appeals held that the suppression denied due process and remanded for a retrial on punishment only, leaving guilt untouched. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Was Brady denied a federal constitutional right when the Maryland Court of Appeals limited his new trial to the question of punishment rather than ordering a retrial on guilt as well?

Holding

No. Suppression of requested evidence favorable to the accused violates due process when the evidence is material to guilt or punishment, regardless of the prosecution's good or bad faith, but limiting the new trial to punishment did not violate due process or equal protection because under Maryland law the court rules on admissibility on the issue of guilt, and the Court of Appeals had effectively ruled the confession could not reduce Brady's offense below first-degree murder. Affirmed. Opinion of the Court by Justice Douglas, announced by Justice Brennan. The opinion text does not state the vote.

Reasoning

  • Agreement with the state court: The Court agreed with the Maryland Court of Appeals that suppressing Boblit's confession violated the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. It endorsed the two Third Circuit decisions the state court relied on, United States ex rel. Almeida v. Baldi and United States ex rel. Thompson v. Dye, as stating "the correct constitutional rule."
  • *Building on Mooney v. Holohan: The Court called its ruling "an extension of" Mooney. Mooney* held that a State violates due process when it obtains a conviction through deliberate deception of court and jury by presenting testimony known to be perjured.
  • **Pyle v. Kansas:* Pyle stated the rule more broadly. It said allegations of knowingly used perjured testimony and "deliberate suppression" of favorable evidence charged a constitutional deprivation. The Third Circuit in Baldi read Pyle* to mean that suppression of favorable evidence alone was enough to deny due process.
  • **Napue v. Illinois:* Napue extended Mooney to cases where the State, without soliciting false evidence, lets it go uncorrected. The Court also cited Alcorta v. Texas and Wilde v. Wyoming, and compared the dissent in Burley v. Mayo*.
  • Purpose of the rule: The Mooney principle aims at "avoidance of an unfair trial to the accused." It does not exist to punish society for a prosecutor's misdeeds. That purpose explains why good faith does not matter.
  • The prosecutor's role: "Society wins not only when the guilty are convicted but when criminal trials are fair." The Court quoted the Department of Justice inscription: "The United States wins its point whenever justice is done its citizens in the courts." A prosecutor who withholds requested evidence that would tend to exculpate or reduce the penalty becomes "the architect of a proceeding that does not comport with standards of justice," even without guile.
  • Why punishment-only retrial was permissible: The Court acknowledged that if Maryland juries truly decided the admissibility of evidence on guilt, the question "would seem to be foreclosed" in Brady's favor. But Maryland's "Judges of Law" provision has exceptions. Under Giles v. State and a long line of Maryland cases (Wheeler v. State, Bell v. State, Beard v. State, Dick v. State; cf. Vogel v. State), trial courts pass on the admissibility of evidence on guilt or innocence.
  • Reading the state court's ruling: While conceding that federal courts walk "on treacherous ground" when exploring state law, the Court read the Court of Appeals' statement that nothing in the confession could have reduced Brady's offense below first-degree murder as a ruling on admissibility on the issue of guilt.
  • No right to a "sporting chance": A jury might have flouted a ruling that the confession was inadmissible on guilt. The Court refused to "raise that trial strategy to the dignity of a constitutional right." Depriving Brady of that chance through a bifurcated proceeding (cf. Williams v. New York) did not deny due process or equal protection.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice White (separate opinion, concurring in the equal protection disposition): The Court should not have reached the due process question. The Maryland court did not say whether it relied on the federal or state due process clause. The State did not cross-petition or challenge the due process ruling, and the equal protection question was a sufficient federal question by itself. The Court's due process discussion is therefore "wholly advisory." It also "goes substantially beyond the holding below." White would use narrower language and would not "cast in constitutional form a broad rule of criminal discovery." He would leave that task, at least for now, to rulemaking or legislation after full consideration by legislators, bench, and bar.
  • Justice Harlan, joined by Justice Black (dissenting): The only federal question is equal protection. The answer would be yes if Boblit's statement would have been admissible on guilt at the original trial. Harlan could not read the Court of Appeals' opinion as an admissibility ruling. It could as easily rest on that court's view of its remedial power under § 645G of the Maryland Post Conviction Procedure Act and Maryland Rule 870. The Maryland cases the majority cited say only that a jury may not "overrule" the trial court on admissibility, and they are unclear about what happens if a jury does. The trial court here charged the jury that it was the judge of "both the law and the facts." The State also conceded at oral argument that the statement would have been admissible for all purposes. Harlan noted that the Court of Appeals did not limit Day v. State, which ordered separate new trials on all issues for felony-murder codefendants who each blamed the other for the killing. Given the uncertainty, he would vacate and remand for reconsideration (cf. Minnesota v. National Tea Co.). He agreed with White that the Court need not decide the broad due process question.

Notes

  • Elements of the due process violation, as stated: (1) suppression by the prosecution; (2) of evidence favorable to the accused; (3) after a request by the accused; (4) where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment. Good faith or bad faith of the prosecution does not matter.
  • Materiality reaches punishment: Evidence that would only "reduce the penalty" counts. Here the confession bore on whether the jury would withhold the death penalty.
  • Undefined terms: The opinion does not define "material" or set a standard for measuring it.
  • The equal protection ruling rests on Maryland law: The Court said that in a jurisdiction where the jury was not the judge of the law, "a different question would be presented." It then resolved the case on its reading of Maryland's exceptions to the jury's role.
  • Professional Responsibility framing: The opinion casts the prosecutor's duty in terms of the fairness of the trial. A prosecutor who withholds requested favorable evidence becomes the "architect" of an unjust proceeding even when acting without guile. The opinion text also quotes Simon Sobeloff, as Solicitor General, describing the government lawyer as "an advocate for a client whose business is not merely to prevail in the instant case" and whose "chief business is not to achieve victory but to establish justice."
  • Finality: The opinion text includes a passage finding the judgment reviewable as a "final judgment" under 28 U.S.C. § 1257(3). It reasons that the general rule that final judgment in a criminal case means sentence (Berman v. United States) cannot apply here, because the claimed right to a new trial on guilt is independent of, and cannot be mooted by, a retrial on punishment.
  • Precedents relied on: Mooney v. Holohan (extended); Pyle v. Kansas; Napue v. Illinois (extended Mooney); Alcorta v. Texas; Wilde v. Wyoming; United States ex rel. Almeida v. Baldi and United States ex rel. Thompson v. Dye (approved as the correct rule); Giles v. State and earlier Maryland cases on the court's control of admissibility.
  • Compared: Burley v. Mayo (dissenting opinion); Williams v. New York (bifurcated proceedings).
  • Overruled: None in the opinion text.
