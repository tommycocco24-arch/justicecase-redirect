Black-Letter Rule

The prosecution's suppression of evidence favorable to an accused, after the accused requests it, violates the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment when the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment. The prosecution's good faith or bad faith is irrelevant.

Facts

  • Parties: Brady (petitioner) and his companion Boblit were each convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to death. They were tried separately, and Brady was tried first.
  • The crime: Murder committed during a robbery. In Maryland the punishment was life or death. The jury could limit the sentence to life by adding "without capital punishment."
  • Brady's defense: Brady testified that he took part in the crime but said Boblit did the actual killing. His counsel conceded first-degree murder in summation and asked only for a verdict "without capital punishment."
  • The request: Before trial, Brady's counsel asked to examine Boblit's extrajudicial statements. The prosecution showed him several of them.
  • The suppression: The prosecution withheld Boblit's July 9, 1958 statement, in which Boblit admitted the actual homicide. Brady learned of it only after he was tried, convicted, and sentenced, and after his conviction was affirmed.
  • Prosecutor's state of mind: The Maryland Court of Appeals found that the withholding was not "the result of guile."
  • Maryland jury rule: The state constitution makes criminal juries "the Judges of Law, as well as of fact." Maryland case law carves out exceptions, including that the trial court decides the admissibility of evidence on guilt or innocence.

Procedural Posture

The Maryland Court of Appeals affirmed Brady's conviction and death sentence. After the trial court denied post-conviction relief, the Court of Appeals held that the suppression denied Brady due process and remanded for a retrial on punishment only. Brady sought certiorari, arguing he was entitled to a new trial on guilt as well.

Issue

Was Brady denied a federal constitutional right when the Maryland Court of Appeals limited his new trial to the question of punishment after the prosecution suppressed Boblit's confession?

Holding

No. The suppression violated due process because the confession was material to punishment, but limiting the retrial to punishment violated neither due process nor equal protection, because under Maryland law the court decides admissibility on guilt and the Court of Appeals had effectively ruled the confession could not reduce Brady's offense below first-degree murder. Affirmed. Opinion by Justice Douglas, announced by Justice Brennan.

Reasoning

  • Due process violation. The Court agreed with the Maryland Court of Appeals that suppressing Boblit's confession violated the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. It endorsed the rule stated by the Third Circuit in United States ex rel. Almeida v. Baldi and United States ex rel. Thompson v. Dye as "the correct constitutional rule."
  • Line of precedent. The Court called its ruling "an extension of" Mooney v. Holohan, which held that a State violates due process by deliberately deceiving court and jury with testimony known to be perjured.
  • Pyle v. Kansas phrased the rule more broadly, covering knowing use of perjured testimony and "deliberate suppression" of favorable evidence.
  • Baldi read Pyle to mean suppression of favorable evidence was itself enough for a due process violation.
  • Napue v. Illinois extended Mooney to cases where the State, without soliciting false evidence, lets it go uncorrected.
  • The Court also cited Alcorta v. Texas and Wilde v. Wyoming, and compared the dissent in Burley v. Mayo.
  • The rule's elements. A due process violation occurs when:
  • the prosecution suppresses evidence,
  • the evidence is favorable to the accused,
  • the accused requested it, and
  • the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment.
  • The prosecution's good faith or bad faith does not matter.
  • Purpose of the rule (PR framing). The Mooney principle aims to avoid an unfair trial to the accused. Punishing society for a prosecutor's misdeeds is not its purpose. Society wins when trials are fair, as well as when the guilty are convicted. The Court quoted the Department of Justice inscription: "The United States wins its point whenever justice is done its citizens in the courts." A prosecutor who withholds requested evidence that would tend to exculpate or reduce the penalty becomes "an architect of a proceeding that does not comport with standards of justice," even without guile.
  • Scope of the retrial. The Court recognized that if the Maryland jury truly judged the law, the Court of Appeals could not say the confession could not have reduced Brady's offense. But Maryland precedent (Giles v. State, Wheeler v. State, Bell v. State, Beard v. State, Dick v. State; cf. Vogel v. State) holds that the court, not the jury, rules on the admissibility of evidence bearing on guilt or innocence.
  • Reading the state court. Acknowledging it "walk[s] on treacherous ground" when exploring state law, the Court read the Court of Appeals' statement that nothing in the confession "could have reduced" Brady's offense below first-degree murder as a ruling that the confession was inadmissible on guilt.
  • No "sporting" right. Brady's best argument was that a jury might have disregarded a judge's inadmissibility ruling. The Court called this "a sporting theory of justice." It refused to raise that trial strategy to a constitutional right. Depriving Brady of that chance through a bifurcated proceeding (cf. Williams v. New York) violated neither due process nor equal protection.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice White (separate opinion).
  • The Court should not have reached the due process question. The Maryland court did not say whether it relied on the federal or the state due process clause. The State filed no cross-petition and did not challenge the due process ruling. That makes the Court's due process discussion "wholly advisory."
  • The Court's due process "advice" goes well beyond the holding below. White would not cast "a broad rule of criminal discovery" in constitutional form. He would leave that task, for now, to the rule-making or legislative process.
  • White concurred in the Court's disposition of the equal protection argument.
  • Justice Harlan (dissent), joined by Justice Black.
  • The case presents only one federal question: whether limiting the new trial to punishment violated equal protection. The answer would be yes if the Boblit statement would have been admissible on guilt at the original trial.
  • The Court of Appeals' opinion can as easily be read as an exercise of its remedial power under § 645G of the Post Conviction Procedure Act and Maryland Rule 870, rather than as an admissibility ruling.
  • The Maryland cases the majority cites do not settle whether the statement was admissible on guilt. The trial court itself told the jury it was the final judge of law and fact. The State also conceded at oral argument that the statement would have been admissible for all purposes.
  • Harlan would vacate and remand for reconsideration under the equal protection principle (cf. Minnesota v. National Tea Co.).
  • He agreed with White that the broad due process question did not need to be decided.

Notes

  • Request requirement. As stated, the rule covers suppression of favorable evidence "upon request" by the accused. Here defense counsel specifically asked for Boblit's statements.
  • Materiality. Evidence is material if it bears on guilt or on punishment. Evidence material only to punishment requires only a new punishment proceeding.
  • Intent irrelevant. The rule applies "irrespective of the good faith or bad faith of the prosecution." This extends beyond the deliberate deception in Mooney.
  • Expressly left open. The Court said that in a jurisdiction where the jury does not judge the law, "a different question would be presented." It did not decide that question.
  • State-law dependence. The remedy ruling rests on the Court's reading of Maryland admissibility law and the Court of Appeals' opinion. The Court said state courts are the final expositors of state law.
  • Relied on:
  • Mooney v. Holohan, which the ruling extends.
  • Pyle v. Kansas and Napue v. Illinois, which broadened Mooney.
  • Alcorta v. Texas and Wilde v. Wyoming.
  • The Third Circuit's Almeida v. Baldi and Thompson v. Dye, which state "the correct constitutional rule."
  • Maryland's Giles v. State line on court control of admissibility.
  • Compared:
  • Burley v. Mayo (dissenting opinion), on the due process point.
  • Williams v. New York, on bifurcated trials.
  • Overruled: None.
  • Contested scope. White and Harlan (joined by Black) viewed the due process discussion as unnecessary to the decision, and White called it advisory.
