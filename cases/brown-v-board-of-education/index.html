Black-Letter Rule

In the field of public education, the doctrine of "separate but equal" has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal, so state laws requiring or permitting racial segregation in public schools deny Black children the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment, even when physical facilities and other "tangible" factors are equal.

Facts

  • Parties: Black minors, through legal representatives, in four consolidated cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware. The defendants were local school boards and officials.
  • Claim: The plaintiffs were denied admission to schools attended by white children under state laws requiring or permitting segregation by race. They alleged this denied them equal protection under the Fourteenth Amendment.
  • *Kansas (Brown):* A Kansas statute permitted, but did not require, cities over 15,000 to maintain separate schools. Topeka chose to segregate its elementary schools.
  • South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware: State constitutional and statutory provisions required school segregation.
  • Tangible equality: The courts below found the Black and white schools had been equalized, or were being equalized, in buildings, curricula, teacher qualifications and salaries, and other tangible factors.
  • Findings on harm: The Kansas court found that legally sanctioned segregation has a detrimental effect on Black children and tends to retard their educational and mental development. The Delaware Chancellor made a similar finding.

Procedural Posture

In Kansas, South Carolina, and Virginia, three-judge federal district courts denied relief under Plessy v. Ferguson's "separate but equal" doctrine, and the plaintiffs appealed directly to the Supreme Court. In Delaware, the state courts applied the same doctrine but ordered the plaintiffs admitted to the white schools because the Black schools were inferior, and the Supreme Court granted the defendants' petition for certiorari. The Court heard argument in the 1952 Term and reargument this Term on questions it propounded.

Issue

Does segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race, even though physical facilities and other "tangible" factors may be equal, deprive minority children of the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment?

Holding

Yes. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal, so segregation in public education denies equal protection under the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court (Chief Justice Warren delivering the opinion) announced this holding without entering final decrees and restored the cases to the docket for further argument on relief.

Reasoning

  • History is inconclusive. Reargument focused on the adoption of the Fourteenth Amendment in 1868. The Court found the history "inconclusive." The Amendment's proponents wanted to remove all legal distinctions, its opponents wanted it to have minimal effect, and what others intended "cannot be determined with any degree of certainty."
  • Education in 1868 was different. Free public schooling had not taken hold in the South. Education of Black children was almost nonexistent and in some states forbidden by law. Even in the North, curricula were rudimentary, terms were short, and compulsory attendance was virtually unknown. That explains why the Amendment's history says so little about public education.
  • Early precedent read the Amendment broadly. The first cases construing the Fourteenth Amendment read it as proscribing all state-imposed discrimination against Black people. "Separate but equal" did not appear in the Court until Plessy in 1896, which involved transportation.
  • Prior education cases left the doctrine untested. In Cumming and Gong Lum, the plaintiffs did not challenge the doctrine itself. In Gaines, Sipuel, Sweatt, and McLaurin, the Court found specific inequalities and never had to re-examine the doctrine. Sweatt expressly reserved whether Plessy applies to public education.
  • Tangible equality cannot decide these cases. Because the lower courts found tangible factors equalized or being equalized, the Court looked "to the effect of segregation itself on public education."
  • Education must be assessed as it exists today. The Court refused to "turn the clock back" to 1868 or 1896. Education is "perhaps the most important function of state and local governments" and "the very foundation of good citizenship." Where the state undertakes to provide it, the opportunity "is a right which must be made available to all on equal terms."
  • Intangibles matter, with added force for children. Sweatt and McLaurin relied on intangible factors at the graduate level. For grade and high school children, separation solely by race "generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone."
  • The lower court findings and modern authority confirm the harm. The Court quoted the Kansas court's finding on the detrimental effect of segregation, said it is "amply supported by modern authority" (citing social science literature in footnote 11), and rejected any contrary language in Plessy.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope of the rule: The holding is limited to "the field of public education." The Court did not address "separate but equal" in other contexts.
  • **Plessy treatment:* The Court held the doctrine has no place in public education and rejected "any language in Plessy v. Ferguson* contrary to" the finding that segregation harms Black children. The opinion does not use the word "overrule."
  • Due Process left open: Because the Court decided the case on equal protection, it found it unnecessary to decide whether segregation also violates the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Footnote 12 points to Bolling v. Sharpe on the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause.
  • Remedy deferred: Citing the class-action posture, the decision's wide applicability, and varied local conditions, the Court restored the cases to the docket. It requested further argument on Questions 4 and 5: whether a decree must order immediate admission or may allow "an effective gradual adjustment," and how detailed decrees should be framed. The U.S. Attorney General was again invited to participate. The Attorneys General of states requiring or permitting segregation in public education were not invited but would be permitted to appear as amici curiae upon request by September 15, 1954, with briefs due by October 1, 1954.
  • Precedent relied on: Slaughter-House Cases, Strauder v. West Virginia, Virginia v. Rives, and Ex parte Virginia (early broad reading of the Amendment); Sweatt v. Painter and McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents (intangible factors).
  • Precedent distinguished or described: Cumming v. County Board of Education and Gong Lum v. Rice (doctrine not challenged); Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada and Sipuel v. Oklahoma (relief granted on specific inequality without re-examining the doctrine); Sweatt (question reserved, and no finding of tangible equality there). The Court also cited Berea College v. Kentucky.
  • Origin of the doctrine: Footnote 6 traces "separate but equal" to Roberts v. City of Boston, a Massachusetts case on a state constitutional guarantee, and notes that school segregation was a nationwide problem.
