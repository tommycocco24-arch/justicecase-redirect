Black-Letter Rule

In the field of public education, the doctrine of "separate but equal" has no place, because separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. State-imposed segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race denies the minority children equal educational opportunities, and so deprives them of the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment, even where physical facilities and other "tangible" factors are equal.

Facts

  • Parties: Negro minors, through their legal representatives, in four consolidated cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware, sought admission to public schools on a nonsegregated basis.
  • Laws challenged: Each plaintiff was denied admission to schools attended by white children under laws requiring or permitting racial segregation. Kansas permitted, without requiring, cities over 15,000 to maintain separate schools, and Topeka chose to segregate its elementary schools. South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware required segregation by constitution and statute.
  • Tangible equality: The lower courts found that the Negro and white schools had been equalized, or were being equalized, in buildings, curricula, teacher qualifications and salaries, and other "tangible" factors. Virginia's Attorney General advised on reargument that its equalization program was complete.
  • Findings on segregation's effect: The Kansas court found that legally sanctioned segregation has a detrimental effect on Negro children, generates a sense of inferiority that affects motivation to learn, and tends to retard their educational and mental development. It ruled against the plaintiffs anyway. The Delaware Chancellor made a similar finding but did not rest the decision on it.

Procedural Posture

In Kansas, South Carolina, and Virginia, three-judge federal district courts denied relief under Plessy v. Ferguson's "separate but equal" doctrine, and the plaintiffs took direct appeals. In Delaware, the state courts adhered to Plessy but ordered the plaintiffs admitted to white schools because those schools were superior, and the Court granted the defendants' petition for certiorari. The cases were argued in the 1952 Term and reargued this Term on questions the Court propounded.

Issue

Does segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race, even where physical facilities and other "tangible" factors are equal, deprive minority children of equal educational opportunities and thus of the equal protection of the laws under the Fourteenth Amendment?

Holding

Yes: in the opinion by Chief Justice Warren, the Court held that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal and that the plaintiffs and others similarly situated are deprived of equal protection, and it restored the cases to the docket for further argument on the form of relief (the opinion text does not state the vote).

Reasoning

  • History is inconclusive. Reargument focused on the adoption of the Fourteenth Amendment in 1868. The Court found the sources "at best" inconclusive. Proponents wanted to remove all legal distinctions. Opponents wanted the narrowest effect. What others intended cannot be determined with certainty.
  • Public education in 1868 was undeveloped. In the South, free tax-supported schools had not taken hold, Negro education was almost nonexistent, and some states forbade it by law. In the North, curricula were rudimentary, terms were short, and compulsory attendance was virtually unknown. So the Amendment's history says little about public education.
  • Early precedent read the Amendment broadly. The first cases construing the Fourteenth Amendment read it as proscribing all state-imposed discrimination against the Negro race (Slaughter-House Cases, Strauder v. West Virginia, Virginia v. Rives, Ex parte Virginia). "Separate but equal" first appeared in Plessy in 1896, a transportation case.
  • Prior education cases did not decide the question. Cumming and Gong Lum did not challenge the doctrine itself. The graduate school cases (Gaines, Sipuel, Sweatt, McLaurin) found inequality in specific benefits and granted relief without re-examining the doctrine. Sweatt expressly reserved whether Plessy applies to public education.
  • Tangible comparison cannot decide these cases. Because the findings show equalized tangible factors, the Court looked to "the effect of segregation itself on public education."
  • Education must be judged as it exists today. The Court refused to "turn the clock back" to 1868 or 1896. Education is "perhaps the most important function of state and local governments," shown by compulsory attendance laws and large expenditures. It is the foundation of good citizenship and necessary to succeed in life. Where the state undertakes to provide it, education "is a right which must be made available to all on equal terms."
  • Intangible factors control. Sweatt relied on qualities "incapable of objective measurement." McLaurin relied on the ability to study, discuss, and exchange views with other students. These considerations apply "with added force" to grade and high school children.
  • Segregation generates a feeling of inferiority. Separating children solely by race "generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone." The Court adopted the Kansas finding, said it is "amply supported by modern authority" (citing social science studies in footnote 11), and rejected any contrary language in Plessy.
  • Equal Protection resolves the case. Having found an equal protection violation, the Court did not reach whether segregation also violates the Due Process Clause.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope: The holding is limited by its terms to "the field of public education." The Court did not address segregation in other settings, including transportation, the subject of Plessy.
  • *Treatment of Plessy: The Court held that "separate but equal" has no place in public education and rejected "any language in Plessy* contrary to" the finding that segregation harms Negro children. The opinion does not use the word "overrule."
  • Left open: Due Process. The Court found it unnecessary to decide whether school segregation violates the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Footnote 12 points to Bolling v. Sharpe on the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause.
  • Left open: Remedy. No decrees issued. Because these are class actions with wide applicability and varied local conditions, the cases were restored to the docket for argument on Questions 4 and 5: whether Negro children must be admitted forthwith to schools of their choice or whether the Court may permit "an effective gradual adjustment," and how decrees should be framed (detailed decrees, special master, or remand to the courts of first instance). The U.S. Attorney General and the Attorneys General of segregating states were invited to participate.
  • Relied on: Sweatt v. Painter and McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents for intangible factors. Strauder v. West Virginia and the Slaughter-House Cases for the early broad reading of the Amendment.
  • Distinguished: Cumming v. County Board of Education and Gong Lum v. Rice, where the doctrine itself was not challenged. Gaines, Sipuel, Sweatt, and McLaurin, where tangible inequality allowed relief without re-examining the doctrine. Sweatt also differs because here the findings show equalized tangible factors.
  • Origin of the doctrine: Footnote 6 traces "separate but equal" to Roberts v. City of Boston, a state constitutional case, and notes that school segregation was a nationwide problem, not merely a sectional one.
