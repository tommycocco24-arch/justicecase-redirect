Black-Letter Rule

Specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant satisfies due process when the defendant purposefully directed activities at forum residents or purposefully availed himself of the privilege of conducting activities in the forum, the claim arises out of or relates to those activities, and jurisdiction comports with fair play and substantial justice. A contract with a forum resident does not alone establish minimum contacts. Courts must weigh the prior negotiations, contemplated future consequences, contract terms, and actual course of dealing. Once purposeful minimum contacts exist, the defendant must present a compelling case that other considerations make jurisdiction unreasonable.

Facts

  • Parties: Burger King is a Florida corporation headquartered in Miami. Rudzewicz is a Michigan citizen and senior partner in a Detroit accounting firm.
  • The franchise: Rudzewicz and MacShara applied through Burger King's Birmingham, Michigan district office. The application went to Miami headquarters, which entered into the agreements. They took over a restaurant in Drayton Plains, Michigan, under a 20-year relationship. Rudzewicz personally obligated himself to payments exceeding $1 million.
  • Contract terms: The documents said the relationship was established in Miami, that the agreement was governed by Florida law, and that all fees and notices went to Miami headquarters. The agreement also stated that the choice-of-law clause "does not require that all suits concerning this Agreement be filed in Florida."
  • Negotiations and course of dealing: The franchisees negotiated with both the Michigan office and Miami headquarters over fees, building design, rent, and assignment of liabilities. They learned the district office had little decisionmaking authority. Miami granted limited concessions, including a $10,439 rent reduction for the third year.
  • Florida ties: Rudzewicz had no Florida office and apparently never visited the state. MacShara attended required management training in Miami. The franchisees bought $165,000 of equipment from a Burger King division in Miami.
  • Default and termination: After business declined, the franchisees fell behind on payments to Miami. Miami officials negotiated by mail and telephone, then terminated the franchise. The franchisees kept operating the restaurant as a Burger King.
  • Sophistication: The District Court found Rudzewicz and MacShara were "experienced and sophisticated businessmen" who did not act under economic duress. Rudzewicz had counsel throughout.

Procedural Posture

Burger King sued in the Southern District of Florida on diversity and trademark jurisdiction. The court denied the defendants' personal-jurisdiction motions under Florida's long-arm statute and, after a bench trial, entered judgment for Burger King. A divided Eleventh Circuit panel reversed on due process grounds. The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal as improperly brought under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(2), treated the jurisdictional statement as a cert petition, and granted it.

Issue

Does the Due Process Clause permit a Florida federal court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a Michigan franchisee for breach of a franchise agreement with a Miami-based franchisor, when the franchisee never physically entered Florida?

Holding

Yes. Because Rudzewicz established a substantial and continuing relationship with Burger King's Miami headquarters, received fair notice from the contract documents and the course of dealing that he might be sued in Florida, and failed to show that jurisdiction there would otherwise be fundamentally unfair, the exercise of jurisdiction did not offend due process. The judgment of the Court of Appeals was reversed and the case remanded (Justice Brennan for the Court; Justices Stevens and White dissenting; Justice Powell not participating).

Reasoning

  • Source of the protection: Due process protects an individual liberty interest in not being bound by judgments of a forum with which the defendant has no meaningful "contacts, ties, or relations." The requirement provides "fair warning" so defendants can structure their conduct with some assurance as to where they will be sued. A footnote ties this to individual liberty and distinguishes it from federalism concerns.
  • Specific jurisdiction test: Fair warning is satisfied where the defendant "purposefully directed" activities at forum residents (Keeton) and the litigation arises out of or relates to those activities (Helicopteros).
  • Why states may reach such defendants: States have a "manifest interest" in giving residents a convenient forum (McGee). Those who "purposefully derive benefit" from interstate activity should not use due process as a "territorial shield" against voluntarily assumed obligations (Kulko). Modern transportation and communication reduce the burden of defending away from home (McGee).
  • Purposeful availment controls: Foreseeability of causing injury in the forum is not enough (World-Wide Volkswagen). The relevant foreseeability is whether the defendant should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there. Following Hanson, the unilateral activity of others cannot create contacts. Contacts that are "random," "fortuitous," or "attenuated" do not count. Contacts that proximately result from the defendant's own actions creating a "substantial connection" do.
  • Presumption of reasonableness: A defendant who deliberately engaged in significant forum activities or created "continuing obligations" with forum residents (Travelers Health) has availed himself of the forum's benefits and protections. Requiring him to litigate there is presumptively reasonable.
  • Physical presence not required: Much commerce is conducted by mail and wire. The Court has consistently rejected the idea that absence of physical contacts defeats jurisdiction where efforts are purposefully directed at forum residents.
  • Fair play and substantial justice factors (from World-Wide Volkswagen), considered after minimum contacts are found:
  • Burden on the defendant
  • Forum State's interest in adjudicating the dispute
  • Plaintiff's interest in convenient and effective relief
  • Interstate judicial system's interest in efficient resolution of controversies
  • Shared interest of the several States in furthering fundamental substantive social policies
  • Sliding scale: These factors can sometimes support jurisdiction on a lesser showing of contacts (Keeton, Calder, McGee). In the other direction, a defendant with purposeful contacts must present a "compelling case" of unreasonableness. Many concerns can be handled through choice-of-law rules or a venue transfer. Jurisdiction still cannot make litigation "so gravely difficult and inconvenient" that a party is at a "severe disadvantage" (The Bremen).
  • Contracts as contacts: A contract with an out-of-state party alone cannot automatically establish minimum contacts. The Court rejected "mechanical" tests (International Shoe) and "conceptualistic" theories of place of contracting or performance (Hoopeston). Courts must examine four things:
  • Prior negotiations
  • Contemplated future consequences
  • Terms of the contract
  • Parties' actual course of dealing
  • Application to contacts: Rudzewicz deliberately "reach[ed] out beyond" Michigan to a Florida corporation for a long-term franchise. He accepted 20 years of exacting regulation from Miami. His nonpayment in Miami and continued unauthorized use of the trademarks caused foreseeable injury in Florida.
  • Rejecting the Eleventh Circuit's "Michigan office" view: The contract documents and course of dealing showed that Miami held decisionmaking authority. The district office mostly passed communications along to headquarters.
  • Choice-of-law clause: The Eleventh Circuit misread Hanson. Choice-of-law analysis looks at the whole transaction, while minimum contacts looks only at the defendant's purposeful connection. A choice-of-law clause standing alone is insufficient to confer jurisdiction. Combined with the 20-year relationship, it reinforced Rudzewicz's deliberate affiliation with Florida and the foreseeability of suit there.
  • Application to fairness factors: Florida had a legitimate interest. Rudzewicz did not show how Michigan's franchise law interest made Florida jurisdiction unconstitutional. The finding that he could not call Michigan witnesses had no record support, and inconvenience can usually be handled by a change of venue. A defendant cannot defeat jurisdiction simply because the adversary is wealthier.
  • Bargaining power: The District Court's findings of no misrepresentation, no duress, and sophisticated parties were not clearly erroneous under FRCP 52(a). Rudzewicz had counsel, negotiated for five months, and won concessions.
  • No talismanic formulas: The Court shared the Eleventh Circuit's worry about small consumers and default judgments. It held that the facts of each case must be weighed. Jurisdiction cannot rest on contract terms obtained through "fraud, undue influence, or overweening bargaining power" (The Bremen). Those dangers were absent here.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Stevens, joined by Justice White (dissent): Requiring a franchisee to defend in the franchisor's chosen forum is significantly unfair. Rudzewicz had no place of business, employees, or license in Florida and did all of his business in Michigan. His main contacts were with the Michigan office. The majority relies on boilerplate to find purposeful availment. Stevens would affirm and adopted Judge Vance's Eleventh Circuit opinion. That opinion said the Michigan office conducted negotiations and supervision, so Rudzewicz lacked notice of suit in Florida. It also said that the typical local franchisee is financially unprepared for distant litigation and that franchise contracts reflect a characteristic disparity in bargaining power.

Notes

  • No franchise presumption: The Court rejected Burger King's proposed rule or presumption that joining an interstate franchise means consent to jurisdiction at the franchisor's home. Some franchises may be primarily intrastate or have different decisionmaking structures.
  • Fact-specific approach: The Court acknowledged that its approach rules out clear-cut jurisdictional rules.
  • Left open, trademark claim: MacShara did not appeal, and Rudzewicz waived appeal of the trademark ruling. The Court did not decide how far Florida's tortious-act long-arm provision may reach out-of-state trademark infringement.
  • Left open, attribution: The Court did not decide the bounds of attributing MacShara's Miami training to Rudzewicz. It cited International Shoe and Calder on attribution where a party is a "primary participant" directing the activities. The training was not pivotal to the result.
  • Single acts: Even a single act can support jurisdiction if it creates a "substantial connection" (McGee). "Single or occasional acts" may not suffice if they create only an attenuated affiliation (International Shoe, World-Wide Volkswagen).
  • Specific vs. general jurisdiction: The Court distinguished specific jurisdiction from general jurisdiction over claims unrelated to forum contacts (Helicopteros, Perkins).
  • Consent: Personal jurisdiction is waivable. Freely negotiated forum-selection clauses that are not "unreasonable and unjust" can be enforced without offending due process (National Equipment Rental, The Bremen). The franchise agreement here contained a choice-of-law clause and no forum-selection clause.
  • Appellate jurisdiction: Appeal under § 1254(2) requires that the court of appeals squarely held a state statute unconstitutional on its face or as applied. A judgment resting on a stipulation that the statute applies does not qualify. Florida courts had read § 48.193(1)(g) to extend only to due process limits.
  • Relied on: International Shoe, McGee, Travelers Health, Hanson, World-Wide Volkswagen, Keeton, Calder, Helicopteros, Kulko, Hoopeston, The Bremen.
  • Distinguished as unilateral-activity cases: World-Wide Volkswagen (the customer drove the car to the forum), Kulko (the former spouse moved to the forum), and Hanson (the settlor exercised a power of appointment in the forum).
  • Overruled: None.
