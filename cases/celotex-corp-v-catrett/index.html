Black-Letter Rule

Under Rule 56(c), after adequate time for discovery and upon motion, summary judgment must be entered against a party who fails to make a showing sufficient to establish an element essential to its case on which it will bear the burden of proof at trial. A moving party who will not bear that burden need not produce affidavits or other evidence negating the opponent's claim. It may discharge its initial burden by "showing," meaning pointing out to the district court, that there is an absence of evidence to support the nonmoving party's case. The nonmoving party must then go beyond the pleadings and designate specific facts showing a genuine issue for trial.

Facts

  • Parties: Catrett, administratrix of her husband's estate, sued 15 asbestos manufacturers and distributors, including Celotex.
  • Claim: Wrongful death from asbestos exposure, sounding in negligence, breach of warranty, and strict liability. Filed September 1980; the husband died in 1979.
  • The motion: Celotex moved for summary judgment in September 1981. It argued Catrett had "failed to produce evidence" that any Celotex product proximately caused the injuries "within the jurisdictional limits" of the District Court. It noted that Catrett's interrogatory answers identified no witnesses who could testify to exposure to Celotex products. Celotex submitted no affidavits or evidence of its own negating exposure.
  • The response: Catrett produced three documents: the decedent's deposition transcript, a letter from an official of a former employer, and a letter from an insurance company to her attorney. All tended to show exposure to Celotex products in Chicago in 1970-1971.
  • Hearsay objection: Celotex argued the three documents were inadmissible hearsay and could not be considered.
  • Ruling below: The District Court granted the motion because there was "no showing that the plaintiff was exposed to the defendant Celotex's product in the District of Columbia or elsewhere within the statutory period."

Procedural Posture

The District Court granted summary judgment for Celotex, and a divided D.C. Circuit panel reversed, holding the motion "fatally defective" because Celotex offered no evidence supporting it (Bork, J., dissenting). The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a conflict with the Third Circuit.

Issue

Must a defendant moving for summary judgment on an issue on which the plaintiff bears the burden of proof at trial support its motion with affidavits or other evidence affirmatively negating the plaintiff's claim?

Holding

No. Rule 56 contains no express or implied requirement that the movant submit evidence negating the opponent's claim, and the movant may meet its initial burden by pointing out the absence of evidence supporting the nonmovant's case; the judgment of the Court of Appeals is reversed and the case remanded (Rehnquist, J., for a 5-4 Court).

Reasoning

  • Text of Rule 56(c): Summary judgment is proper when the record shows no genuine issue of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. A complete failure of proof on an essential element of the nonmovant's case "necessarily renders all other facts immaterial," so no genuine issue of material fact can exist. The movant is then entitled to judgment as a matter of law.
  • Directed verdict parallel: The summary judgment standard "mirrors the standard for a directed verdict" under Rule 50(a), quoting Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.
  • Movant's initial burden: The movant "always bears the initial responsibility" of informing the court of the basis for the motion and identifying the portions of the pleadings, depositions, interrogatory answers, admissions, and affidavits it believes show no genuine issue.
  • No affidavit requirement: Rule 56(c) refers to "the affidavits, if any." Rules 56(a) and (b) let claimants and defendants move "with or without supporting affidavits." The motion should be granted if whatever is before the court satisfies the Rule 56(c) standard.
  • Rule 56(e) "made and supported": Where the nonmovant bears the burden of proof on a dispositive issue, a motion resting solely on the pleadings, depositions, interrogatory answers, and admissions is "made and supported as provided in this rule." That triggers the nonmovant's duty to go beyond the pleadings and designate specific facts showing a genuine issue for trial.
  • Form of the nonmovant's evidence: The nonmovant need not produce evidence in a form admissible at trial and need not depose her own witnesses. She may oppose with any Rule 56(c) material except the pleadings themselves.
  • **Adickes explained:* The Adickes statement that the 1963 amendment did not modify the movant's burden "to show initially the absence of a genuine issue" is "accurate in a literal sense." The Court agreed the Adickes* motion should have been denied on that record. The language does not mean the movant must produce evidence negating an issue on which the nonmovant bears the burden.
  • Purpose of the 1963 amendment: The last two sentences of Rule 56(e) were added to stop parties from resisting a proper motion by relying on their pleadings. They were not meant to add to the movant's burden. The D.C. Circuit's reading would turn an amendment designed to facilitate summary judgment into one that makes it harder.
  • Sua sponte power: District courts may enter summary judgment sua sponte if the losing party had notice to come forward with all her evidence. It would "defy common sense" to bar the same result because the defendant asked for it.
  • Timing and Rule 56(f): The motion came a year after filing and after discovery, so Catrett was not "railroaded." Rule 56(f) handles premature motions by allowing denial or a continuance when the nonmovant has not had full discovery.
  • Policy: Summary judgment is "an integral part of the Federal Rules as a whole," which aim "to secure the just, speedy and inexpensive determination of every action" (Rule 1). Under notice pleading, the motion to dismiss seldom screens out factually insufficient claims, and summary judgment has taken its place. Rule 56 protects both the right to a jury trial on claims with factual support and the right of opponents to show before trial that claims lack a factual basis.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • White, J. (concurring): Agrees the movant need not always submit evidence or affidavits and may rely on depositions and interrogatory answers. Stresses that the movant must still discharge its burden: a conclusory assertion that the plaintiff has no evidence is not enough. A plaintiff need not depose her own witnesses or obtain their affidavits to defeat a motion asserting only that she has produced no support. It is the defendant's task to negate, if it can, the claimed basis for the suit. Celotex conceded that if Catrett named a witness, summary judgment requires Celotex to show that witness's possible testimony raises no genuine issue. Because the Court of Appeals did not reach whether Catrett revealed enough, he agrees to remand.
  • Brennan, J. (dissenting, joined by Burger, C.J., and Blackmun, J.): Does not disagree with the Court's legal analysis but says it fails to explain clearly what a movant must do. Divides the movant's burden into an initial burden of production, which can shift, and an ultimate burden of persuasion, which stays with the movant. Where the nonmovant bears the trial burden, the movant may (1) submit evidence negating an essential element, or (2) show the nonmovant's evidence is insufficient. Under the second route, the movant must affirmatively show the absence of evidence in the record and must attack any record evidence it overlooked or ignored. Celotex knew of the three documents and of Catrett's intent to call the former supervisor, Hoff, as a trial witness, yet did not attack that evidence. Celotex therefore failed its burden of production, and the case is "indistinguishable from Adickes." Remand is "a waste of time." In a footnote, notes the uncertainty created by reversing rather than vacating, and suggests that Justice White's view, as the fifth vote, would seem controlling (citing Marks v. United States).
  • Stevens, J. (dissenting): Celotex's motion and argument rested on lack of exposure within the District of Columbia. Catrett adequately showed exposure in Illinois, though possibly not in admissible form. Read in context, the District Court's grant was "palpably erroneous," and the bench remark "or elsewhere" neither validated it nor raised the question the Court decided. Would affirm on that narrow ground instead of engaging in an "abstract exercise in Rule construction." Calls the remand on "superior knowledge of local law" bewildering because no local-law question exists, and criticizes the Court's "increasing tendency to adopt a presumption of reversal."

Notes

  • Scope: The rule addresses issues on which the nonmoving party bears the burden of proof at trial.
  • Conditions built into the rule: Adequate time for discovery, a motion, and a failure of showing on an essential element on which the nonmovant bears the trial burden.
  • Left open on remand: Whether Catrett's showing was adequate, and whether that showing, if reduced to admissible evidence, would carry her burden of proof at trial. The Court sent both questions to the Court of Appeals to decide in the first instance.
  • Admissibility: The nonmovant's opposing materials need not be in admissible form. Pleadings alone are insufficient.
  • Premature motions: Rule 56(f) is the safeguard.
  • **Adickes v. S. H. Kress & Co. (explained):** Not overruled. The Court calls its burden language accurate "in a literal sense" and agrees its motion should have been denied, but rejects reading it to require the movant to produce negating evidence where the nonmovant bears the burden.
  • **Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc. (relied on):** Summary judgment standard mirrors the Rule 50(a) directed verdict standard.
  • Circuit split: The D.C. Circuit's view conflicted with the Third Circuit in In re Japanese Electronic Products (rev'd on other grounds sub nom. Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.). The Court notes the Fifth Circuit in Fontenot v. Upjohn Co. also rejected the D.C. Circuit's position.
  • Majority's response to Stevens (footnote): The District Court expressly said "in the District of Columbia or elsewhere," the Court assumes it meant what it said, and no judge below shared Stevens's reading.
  • Sources relied on: Bork's D.C. Circuit dissent and Wright, Miller & Kane on sua sponte summary judgment; Rule 1 and Schwarzer's article on summary judgment's role.
  • Disposition nuance: The Court reversed and remanded. Brennan flags tension between reversing and White's view that the Court of Appeals should reevaluate whether Celotex met its initial burden.
