Black-Letter Rule

Under Rule 56(c), after adequate time for discovery and upon motion, summary judgment must be entered against a party who fails to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an element essential to that party's case, and on which that party will bear the burden of proof at trial. When the nonmoving party bears the burden of proof at trial, the moving party need not produce affidavits or other evidence negating the opponent's claim. It may discharge its initial burden by "showing," meaning pointing out to the district court, that there is an absence of evidence to support the nonmoving party's case.

Facts

  • Parties: Catrett, administratrix of her husband's estate, sued 15 corporations, including Celotex, for wrongful death.
  • Claim: The complaint alleged the husband's 1979 death resulted from exposure to asbestos products the defendants manufactured or distributed. It sounded in negligence, breach of warranty, and strict liability.
  • The motion: In September 1981, a year after suit was filed, Celotex moved for summary judgment. It argued Catrett had "failed to produce evidence" that any Celotex product proximately caused the injuries "within the jurisdictional limits" of the District Court. It noted that Catrett's interrogatory answers identified no witnesses who could testify to the decedent's exposure to Celotex products.
  • No supporting affidavits: Celotex did not submit affidavits or other evidence negating exposure.
  • The response: Catrett produced three documents: a transcript of the decedent's deposition, a letter from an official of a former employer whom Celotex planned to call as a trial witness, and a letter from an insurance company. All tended to show exposure to Celotex products in Chicago during 1970-1971.
  • Hearsay objection: Celotex argued the three documents were inadmissible hearsay and could not be considered.
  • Ruling below: In July 1982 the District Court granted the motion because there was "no showing that the plaintiff was exposed to the defendant Celotex's product in the District of Columbia or elsewhere within the statutory period."

Procedural Posture

The District Court granted summary judgment for Celotex, and a divided D.C. Circuit panel reversed, holding the motion "fatally defective" because Celotex made no effort to adduce evidence supporting it (Bork, J., dissenting). The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a conflict with the Third Circuit.

Issue

Must a party moving for summary judgment on an issue where the nonmoving party bears the burden of proof at trial support its motion with affidavits or other evidence negating the nonmoving party's claim?

Holding

No. Rule 56 contains no express or implied requirement that the movant submit evidence negating the opponent's claim, and the movant may discharge its burden by pointing out an absence of evidence supporting the nonmoving party's case; the judgment was reversed and remanded in an opinion by Justice Rehnquist, joined by Justices White, Marshall, Powell, and O'Connor (5-4).

Reasoning

  • Rule 56(c) text: The plain language mandates summary judgment, after adequate time for discovery, against a party who cannot establish an essential element on which it bears the burden of proof at trial.
  • Why "no genuine issue": A complete failure of proof on an essential element "necessarily renders all other facts immaterial." The movant is then entitled to judgment as a matter of law.
  • Directed verdict analogy: The summary judgment standard mirrors the directed verdict standard under Rule 50(a), citing Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.
  • Movant's initial responsibility: The movant always must inform the court of the basis for its motion and identify the portions of the pleadings, depositions, interrogatory answers, admissions, and affidavits, if any, that it believes show no genuine issue of material fact.
  • "If any": Rule 56(c)'s reference to "the affidavits, if any" suggests no affidavit requirement.
  • Rules 56(a) and (b): Claimants and defendants may move "with or without supporting affidavits." Any doubt is removed by this language.
  • Rule 56(e) "made and supported": Catrett argued her duty to respond arose only for motions "made and supported as provided in this rule." The Court held a motion relying solely on the pleadings, depositions, interrogatory answers, and admissions on file is properly "made and supported." The nonmovant must then go beyond the pleadings and designate "specific facts showing that there is a genuine issue for trial."
  • What the nonmovant may use: The nonmovant need not produce evidence in a form admissible at trial and need not depose its own witnesses. It may oppose with any material listed in Rule 56(c) except the mere pleadings.
  • *Treatment of Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co.: The Court called Adickes's statement about the movant's initial burden "accurate in a literal sense" and agreed the motion in Adickes* should have been denied on that record. The Court refused to read that language to require the movant to produce evidence on an issue where the nonmovant bears the burden of proof.
  • 1963 amendment to Rule 56(e): The last two sentences were added to stop parties from resisting a properly made motion by resting on their pleadings. They were not adopted to add to the movant's burden. The D.C. Circuit's reading would turn an amendment meant to facilitate summary judgment into one that makes it harder to obtain.
  • Sua sponte power: District courts may enter summary judgment sua sponte when the losing party had notice that it had to come forward with all its evidence. It would "defy common sense" if filing a motion barred a result the court could reach on its own.
  • No railroading: The motion came a year after suit and after discovery. Premature motions can be handled under Rule 56(f), which allows denial or continuance when the nonmovant has not had full discovery.
  • Policy: Summary judgment is "an integral part of the Federal Rules as a whole," which aim "to secure the just, speedy and inexpensive determination of every action" (Rule 1). Under notice pleading, the motion to dismiss rarely screens out factually insufficient claims, and summary judgment has taken that role. Rule 56 must protect both the right to a jury trial on claims with a factual basis and the right of opponents to show before trial that claims lack one.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • White, J., concurring: Agrees the movant need not always support its motion with evidence negating the claim and may rely on depositions, interrogatory answers, and the like. Insists the movant must still discharge its burden: a motion with no support or a "conclusory assertion that the plaintiff has no evidence" is not enough. A plaintiff need not depose its own witnesses or obtain their affidavits to defeat a motion asserting only a lack of support. Notes Celotex conceded that if Catrett named a witness, summary judgment requires Celotex to show that witness's possible testimony raises no genuine issue. Joins the remand because the Court of Appeals never addressed that question.
  • Brennan, J., dissenting (joined by Burger, C.J., and Blackmun, J.): Does not disagree with the majority's legal analysis but says it fails to explain clearly what a movant must do, which will create confusion. Lays out a framework: the movant bears an initial burden of production (which shifts) and an ultimate burden of persuasion (which never shifts). Where the nonmovant bears the trial burden, the movant may either (1) submit affirmative evidence negating an essential element or (2) show the nonmovant's evidence is insufficient. Under the second option, the movant must affirmatively demonstrate there is no evidence in the record and must attack any record evidence it overlooked. Applying this, Celotex failed its burden: the record already held the three documents and Catrett's stated intent to call Hoff as a witness, and Celotex ignored them. Finds the case indistinguishable from Adickes. Argues the remand is unclear: reversing rather than vacating implies the Court of Appeals should assume Celotex met its initial burden, while White, the fifth vote, wants that question reexamined. Suggests White's view controls, citing Marks v. United States. Calls the remand "a waste of time" given Celotex's admission about Hoff.
  • Stevens, J., dissenting: Argues Celotex's motion and argument rested on lack of exposure within the District of Columbia, and Catrett made an adequate showing (possibly not in admissible form) of exposure in Illinois. The District Court's bench reference to "or elsewhere" did not validate the ruling. Would affirm the reversal on that narrow ground of plain error and avoid what he calls an "abstract exercise in Rule construction." Finds the majority's "superior knowledge of local law" rationale for remand "bewildering," since no local-law question exists. Criticizes the Court's tendency toward a presumption of reversal.

Notes

  • Scope of the rule: The holding addresses motions on issues where the nonmoving party bears the burden of proof at trial. It operates only "after adequate time for discovery."
  • Movant still has a burden: The movant must inform the court of the basis for its motion and identify the record portions it relies on. White and Brennan both stress that a conclusory assertion of no evidence is insufficient.
  • Left open on remand: Whether Catrett's showing in opposition was adequate, and whether that showing, if reduced to admissible evidence, would carry her burden at trial. The Court of Appeals had declined to reach either question.
  • Admissibility at summary judgment: The nonmovant need not produce evidence in a form admissible at trial. The Court did not decide whether Catrett's documents were inadmissible hearsay.
  • Remand ambiguity: Brennan flags the tension between the majority's choice to reverse and White's view that the initial-burden question remains open.
  • District Court's ruling: In footnote 2 the majority rejects Stevens's reading, taking the District Court at its word ("or elsewhere") and noting the Court of Appeals read it the same way.
  • Relied on: Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc. (summary judgment mirrors directed verdict); Rule 1; Rule 56(f); the Bork dissent below and Wright & Miller on sua sponte summary judgment.
  • Explained (not overruled): Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co. The Court accepts its statement of the movant's initial burden as literally accurate and its result as correct, and rejects reading it to require the movant to produce negating evidence.
  • Circuit split: Rejects the D.C. Circuit's position, which conflicted with the Third Circuit's in In re Japanese Electronic Products. Notes the Fifth Circuit's Fontenot v. Upjohn Co. also rejected the D.C. Circuit's view.
