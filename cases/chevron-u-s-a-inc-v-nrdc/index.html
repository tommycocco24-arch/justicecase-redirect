Black-Letter Rule

When a court reviews an agency's construction of a statute it administers, it first asks whether Congress has directly spoken to the precise question at issue; if congressional intent is clear, the court and the agency must give effect to it. If the statute is silent or ambiguous on the specific issue, the court asks only whether the agency's answer is based on a permissible construction of the statute, and may not substitute its own construction for a reasonable agency interpretation.

Facts

  • Statute: The Clean Air Act Amendments of 1977 required States that had not met EPA's national air quality standards ("nonattainment" States) to run a permit program for "new or modified major stationary sources." Permits carry stringent conditions, including the lowest achievable emission rate (LAER).
  • Undefined term: The 1977 Amendments did not define "stationary source" for the permit program and made no reference to the "bubble concept." Section 111(a)(3) defined "stationary source" for the separate NSPS program as "any building, structure, facility, or installation which emits or may emit any air pollutant." Section 302(j) defined "major stationary source" by a 100-tons-per-year threshold.
  • The regulation: In October 1981, EPA let States adopt a plantwide definition of "stationary source." An existing plant could install or modify one piece of equipment without meeting permit conditions if total plant emissions would not increase. All emitting devices in the same industrial grouping were treated as if inside a single "bubble."
  • Agency history: EPA had used a plantwide definition in a 1974 NSPS program, endorsed it for certain nonattainment SIPs in 1979, then in 1980 adopted a dual definition (plant or component) relying mainly on two D.C. Circuit decisions. After a new administration began a government-wide review of regulatory burdens in 1981, EPA returned to the plantwide definition.
  • EPA's reasons: EPA found the issue unaddressed by statute or legislative history. It reasoned that the dual definition discouraged modernization and replacement of older, dirtier equipment, that one definition across programs reduced confusion, and that other requirements would still secure attainment.
  • Parties: NRDC and other environmental groups (respondents) challenged the rule. Chevron and industry groups intervened to defend it (petitioners).

Procedural Posture

Respondents petitioned the D.C. Circuit for review, and that court set aside the regulations as contrary to law, holding that under its own precedents the bubble concept was inappropriate in programs meant to improve air quality. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Is EPA's plantwide definition of "stationary source," allowing States to treat all pollution-emitting devices in one industrial grouping as a single "bubble," a permissible construction of the Clean Air Act's nonattainment permit provisions?

Holding

Yes. Because Congress did not have a specific intention on applying the bubble concept to the permit program, the Court defers to EPA's definition as a permissible construction that reasonably accommodates progress in reducing air pollution with economic growth; reversed (Justice Stevens wrote for the Court; the opinion text does not state a vote count, and Justices Marshall, Rehnquist, and O'Connor took no part in the decision).

Reasoning

  • Lower court's error: The D.C. Circuit found that Congress had no intent on the question, then imposed its own "static judicial definition" anyway. Once it found no congressional intent, its job was to ask whether the Administrator's view was reasonable. Whether the bubble concept seemed "inappropriate" to the court was beside the point.
  • The two-step framework:
  • Step one: Has Congress directly spoken to the precise question? Courts use "traditional tools of statutory construction." If intent is clear, it controls. The judiciary is the final authority on statutory construction and must reject agency readings contrary to clear congressional intent.
  • Step two: If the statute is silent or ambiguous, is the agency's answer a permissible construction? The agency's reading need not be the only permissible one or the one the court would have reached on its own.
  • Express vs. implicit delegation: Agency power to administer a program includes filling gaps Congress left, explicitly or implicitly (Morton v. Ruiz). Where Congress explicitly leaves a gap, the resulting legislative regulations get controlling weight unless arbitrary, capricious, or manifestly contrary to the statute. Where the delegation is implicit, a court may not substitute its own construction for a reasonable agency interpretation.
  • Long-standing deference: The Court treated deference as settled law, quoting United States v. Shimer: a reasonable accommodation of conflicting policies committed to the agency should stand unless the statute or its history shows Congress would not have sanctioned it.
  • Text: Section 302(j) explains "major" (100 tons) and equates a source with a "facility," but does not compel any reading of "source." "Facility" can ordinarily mean an integrated collection of elements, so reading "major source" as an entire plant fits common usage. Section 111(a)(3) does not apply to the permit program but sheds as much light as anything in the statute. Its overlapping, illustrative terms can be read to cover "any discrete, but integrated, operation which pollutes," and if they reveal any intent, it was to enlarge the agency's regulatory power. Parsing these general terms does not reveal an actual congressional intent.
  • Respondents' § 173 argument: Respondents argued the plantwide definition effectively waives LAER for new units, which the statute forbids. The Court called this circular: § 173 addresses the consequences of being a "source" and does not define the term.
  • Legislative history: It is "unilluminating" and silent on the precise issue. General remarks were not made with this narrow issue in mind (Jewell Ridge Coal). Senator Muskie's statement does not say what a new source is. The history does show Congress sought to accommodate economic growth and environmental improvement, and is consistent with broad EPA discretion.
  • Changing interpretations: EPA's shifting definitions do not forfeit deference. An initial interpretation "is not instantly carved in stone"; an agency must reconsider its policy on a continuing basis. Using different definitions in different contexts supports the view that the term is flexible.
  • Origin of the 1980 position: The maintain/improve distinction "may well be a sensible one," but Congress never articulated it. The D.C. Circuit's decisions, more than Congress or EPA, drove EPA's 1980 dual definition.
  • Policy and institutional roles: Respondents were relitigating a policy fight they lost in the agency and in the 32 jurisdictions that chose the bubble. Deference was warranted because the scheme is technical and complex, the agency acted in a detailed and reasoned way, and the decision reconciles conflicting policies. Whether Congress meant to delegate, never considered the question, or could not form a coalition "matters not." Judges are neither experts nor politically accountable. Agencies may rely on the incumbent administration's policy views within their delegation, and the Chief Executive is accountable to the people. A challenge that really targets the wisdom of the agency's policy, rather than its reasonableness within a gap Congress left, must fail (TVA v. Hill).

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope of the holding: The Court upheld EPA's definition of "source" for the nonattainment permit program in § 172(b)(6). The meaning of "proposed source" in § 173(2) was expressly not at issue.
  • What the Court declined to decide: It did not determine why Congress left the gap (deliberate delegation, oversight, or political stalemate). It held the reason irrelevant for judicial purposes.
  • Judgments, not opinions: Respondents did not defend the D.C. Circuit's reasoning. Because the Court reviews judgments, it still asked whether the judgment could stand, and respondents could defend it on any ground supported by the record (Ryerson, LeTulle, Langnes).
  • Precedents relied on:
  • Morton v. Ruiz: agency power to fill gaps left implicitly or explicitly by Congress.
  • United States v. Shimer: deference to reasonable accommodations of conflicting policies committed to the agency; source of the closing "effective reconciliation" language. The Shimer quotation itself cites NBC v. United States, NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Republic Aviation, SEC v. Chenery, and NLRB v. Seven-Up Bottling.
  • Capital Cities Cable, Inc. v. Crisp: cited as accord.
  • FEC v. Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee, SEC v. Sloan, and others: courts reject agency readings contrary to clear congressional intent.
  • FEC v. DSCC, Zenith Radio, Train v. NRDC, Udall v. Tallman, and others: agency reading need not be the only permissible one or the court's preferred one.
  • Russell Motor Car Co. and Jewell Ridge Coal: interpretive canons applied to the text and legislative history.
  • TVA v. Hill: policy choices belong to the political branches.
  • Precedents rejected: The D.C. Circuit's reliance on Alabama Power Co. v. Costle and ASARCO Inc. v. EPA to create a bright-line rule (bubble mandatory in maintenance programs, forbidden in improvement programs). The Court found that distinction was not one Congress articulated. It did not purport to overrule those decisions.
