Black-Letter Rule

When a court reviews an agency's construction of a statute the agency administers, it first asks whether Congress has directly spoken to the precise question at issue; if congressional intent is clear, the court and the agency must give it effect. If the statute is silent or ambiguous on the specific issue, the court may not impose its own construction and asks only whether the agency's answer is based on a permissible construction of the statute. A reasonable agency interpretation that reconciles competing policies within a gap left by Congress is entitled to deference, even if the agency has changed its view and even if it is not the reading the court would have chosen.

Facts

  • Statute: The Clean Air Act Amendments of 1977 required nonattainment States to establish permit programs for "new or modified major stationary sources." Permits carry stringent conditions, including offsets and the lowest achievable emission rate (LAER).
  • Undefined term: The 1977 Amendments did not define "stationary source" for the permit program. Section 111(a)(3) defined it for the NSPS program as "any building, structure, facility, or installation which emits or may emit any air pollutant." Section 302(j) defined "major stationary source" by a 100-ton threshold and equated it with "major emitting facility."
  • The regulation: In October 1981, EPA let States adopt a plantwide definition of "stationary source." Under this "bubble concept," a plant may install or modify one piece of equipment without meeting permit conditions if total plant emissions do not increase.
  • Agency history: EPA had used a plantwide definition in a 1974 NSPS program, proposed plantwide approaches for complete Part D SIPs in 1979, and adopted a dual definition in 1980 in reliance on two D.C. Circuit decisions. After a 1981 government-wide review of regulatory burdens, a new administration's EPA returned to the plantwide definition.
  • EPA's reasons (1981): The issue was not squarely addressed in the statute or legislative history. The dual definition discouraged modernization and replacement of older, dirtier equipment. A single definition simplified the rules. Other requirements (reasonable further progress, NSPS, review of significant net increases) would still serve attainment goals.
  • Parties: Respondents NRDC, Citizens for a Better Environment, and North Western Ohio Lung Association challenged the regulation. Chevron and several industry groups intervened to defend it.

Procedural Posture

Respondents petitioned the D.C. Circuit for review, and that court set aside the regulations, holding that under its precedents the bubble concept was inappropriate in programs designed to improve air quality. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed.

Issue

Is EPA's regulation allowing States to adopt a plantwide definition of "stationary source" for the nonattainment permit program based on a reasonable construction of the Clean Air Act?

Holding

Yes. Because Congress did not address whether the bubble concept applies to the nonattainment permit program, and EPA's plantwide definition is a permissible construction that seeks to accommodate progress in reducing air pollution with economic growth, the court must defer; the judgment of the Court of Appeals is reversed (opinion by Justice Stevens; the opinion does not state a vote count, and notes that Justices Marshall and Rehnquist took no part in the consideration or decision and Justice O'Connor took no part in the decision).

Reasoning

  • The framework. Step one: has Congress directly spoken to the precise question? A court answers this using traditional tools of statutory construction, and the judiciary remains the final authority on statutory meaning and must reject constructions contrary to clear congressional intent. Step two: if the statute is silent or ambiguous, is the agency's answer a permissible construction?
  • Explicit vs. implicit delegation. Where Congress explicitly leaves a gap, the resulting legislative regulations get controlling weight unless arbitrary, capricious, or manifestly contrary to the statute. Where delegation is implicit, a court may not substitute its own construction for the administrator's reasonable interpretation.
  • Standard at step two is deferential. The agency's construction need not be the only permissible one, or the one the court would have reached if the question had first arisen in court.
  • Lower court's error. After finding that Congress had no intent on the bubble question, the D.C. Circuit adopted a static judicial definition based on its own view of the program's purposes. Once ambiguity was found, its only job was to ask whether EPA's view was reasonable.
  • Text, step one. Section 302(j) says what "major" means but sheds virtually no light on "stationary source." It equates source with "facility," and treating a major facility as a whole plant is no affront to ordinary English. Section 111(a)(3)'s list ("building, structure, facility, or installation") could support either reading. The terms overlap and are not directed at the question. If anything, the illustrative list was meant to enlarge the agency's regulatory power.
  • Section 173 argument rejected. Respondents argued the bubble lets new units escape the non-waivable LAER requirement. The Court called this circular: § 173 governs the consequences of being a source and does not define the term.
  • Legislative history, step one. The history is unilluminating and silent on the precise issue. General remarks, including Senator Muskie's, were not made with this narrow issue in mind. The history does show Congress sought to accommodate economic growth and environmental improvement, and it is consistent with broad EPA discretion.
  • Step two, reasonableness. The plantwide definition fits the economic-growth purpose. EPA gave a reasonable explanation that it also serves the environmental goals, supported by the rulemaking record and private studies.
  • Changed interpretations. An agency's shift in position does not forfeit deference. An initial interpretation "is not instantly carved in stone," and the agency must consider its policy on a continuing basis. Using different definitions in different contexts reinforces that the term is flexible. The 1980 position was driven mainly by the D.C. Circuit's precedents, not by anything in the statute.
  • Why deference. The scheme is technical and complex, EPA considered the matter in a detailed and reasoned way, and the decision reconciles conflicting policies. It does not matter whether Congress deliberately left the question to the agency, never considered it, or could not reach consensus.
  • Accountability and expertise. Judges are not experts and are not part of a political branch. An agency with delegated policymaking authority may rely on the incumbent administration's policy views, and the Chief Executive is accountable to the people. A challenge that really attacks the wisdom of the agency's policy, rather than whether it is a reasonable choice within a congressional gap, must fail.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Narrow application. The holding is that EPA's plantwide definition of "source" in the nonattainment permit program (the term "major stationary sources" in § 172(b)(6)) is permissible. The Court expressly noted that the meaning of "proposed source" in § 173(2) is not at issue.
  • Step one limits deference. Deference applies only after a court, using traditional tools of construction, finds no clear congressional intent on the precise question.
  • Reviews judgments, not opinions. Respondents did not defend the D.C. Circuit's reasoning. The Court still examined whether the judgment could stand on respondents' alternative textual and legislative-history arguments, which it may consider as grounds supported by the record.
  • D.C. Circuit precedents rejected. The Court rejected the bright-line rule drawn from Alabama Power Co. v. Costle and ASARCO Inc. v. EPA (bubble mandatory in maintenance programs, inappropriate in improvement programs). It said the distinction "may well be a sensible one" but was never articulated by Congress or found in the statute by EPA.
  • Relied on for deference to gap-filling: Morton v. Ruiz (agency power to fill gaps left implicitly or explicitly by Congress); United States v. Shimer (reasonable accommodation of conflicting policies committed to agency care should not be disturbed unless Congress would not have sanctioned it); Capital Cities Cable, Inc. v. Crisp; and the line of cases Shimer cites, including National Broadcasting Co. v. United States, NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., and SEC v. Chenery Corp.
  • Relied on for interpretive points: Russell Motor Car Co. v. United States (a word may have its own character, weighed against associated words conveying a common idea); Jewell Ridge Coal Corp. v. Mine Workers (general legislative remarks not made with the narrow issue in mind do not show congressional desire); TVA v. Hill (policy responsibilities vested in the political branches).
  • Relied on for judicial supremacy at step one: a string of cases including FEC v. Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee and SEC v. Sloan, cited for the rule that courts must reject constructions contrary to clear congressional intent.
  • Policy arguments redirected. The Court characterized respondents' position as a policy battle lost in the agency and in the 32 jurisdictions that opted for the bubble, and said such arguments belong before legislators or administrators.
