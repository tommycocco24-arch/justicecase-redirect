Black-Letter Rule

Malice for murder does not require an intent to kill. When a person intentionally does an uncalled-for act in callous disregard of its likely harmful effects on others, under circumstances where he must reasonably anticipate that death is likely to result, that "wickedness of disposition" and "hardness of heart" is malice. A resulting death is second-degree murder. Second-degree murder in Pennsylvania includes every element of first-degree murder except the specific intent to kill.

Facts

  • Parties: Defendant Malone, age 17, killed William H. Long, age 13. The two were friends. Malone and his mother were living with Long's family while Malone's father and brother served in the armed forces.
  • The gun: Malone got a 32-caliber revolver from his uncle's home the day before the shooting. That afternoon Long took a cartridge from his father's room, and the two put it in the revolver.
  • Loading: At a dairy store, Malone took out the gun and loaded the chamber to the right of the firing pin, then closed the gun.
  • "Russian Poker": At the lunch counter, Malone suggested they play "Russian Poker." Long replied, "I don't care; godhead." Malone pressed the revolver against Long's right side and pulled the trigger three times. The third pull fired and fatally wounded Long, who died two days later.
  • Reaction: Malone said, "Did I hit you, Billy? Gee, Kid, I'm sorry."
  • Defendant's testimony: Malone said he did not "expect to have the gun go off" and had no intention of harming Long. The jury accepted that he had no intent to kill.
  • Motive: There was no motive for the killing.

Procedural Posture

Malone was indicted for murder, convicted of second-degree murder, and sentenced to five to ten years in the penitentiary. The trial court denied a new trial, and Malone appealed to the Pennsylvania Supreme Court.

Issue

Can a defendant who kills without any intent to kill be convicted of second-degree murder, instead of only involuntary manslaughter, when he knowingly pressed a revolver loaded with one cartridge against the victim's side and pulled the trigger three times?

Holding

Yes. An intentional act of gross recklessness, done in wanton disregard of consequences the actor must reasonably anticipate are likely to cause death, shows malice and supports second-degree murder even without intent to kill, so all assignments of error are overruled and the judgment is affirmed (opinion by Chief Justice Maxey; the vote is not stated in the opinion text).

Reasoning

  • Grading of homicide. A specific intent to take life is essential to first-degree murder. Second-degree murder includes every element of first-degree murder except the intent to kill (Commonwealth v. Divomte).
  • Common-law malice. Malice was the "grand criterion" separating murder from other killings. It need not be aimed at the victim in particular. It covers "any evil design in general; the dictate of a wicked, depraved and malignant heart" (4 Blackstone 199). Blackstone's example: "coolly discharging a gun among a multitude of people."
  • Recklessness as malice. An act of gross recklessness for which the actor must reasonably anticipate that death is likely shows the state of mind termed malice. The court drew this from Com. v. Drum and supported it with:
  • Com. v. Mayberry: a driver who wantonly and recklessly hurls a car into a crowd faces the same consequences as one who kills by wantonly firing a gun.
  • Com. v. Hillman: approved charge stating that "reckless conduct that results in the death of another is malice," with the gun-into-a-crowd illustration.
  • Com. v. Knox: a person who aims a loaded gun at a vital part of another's body and fires is presumed to know death is likely.
  • Com. v. Arnold: malice may be implied from conduct, recklessness of consequences, or the cruelty of the crime.
  • Application. Malone intentionally pulled the trigger three times on a gun he knew held one bullet, aimed at a vital part of Long's body. The court described the fatal consequence as "at least sixty per cent certain." That is callous disregard of likely harmful effects, which is malice.
  • Accidental result vs. accidental act. A death may be unintended, and so "accidental," while the act causing it was intentional and malicious. Here the means were deliberate. Only the result was accidental. The court contrasted a negligent act that knocks someone off a stool, where both the act and the death could fairly be called accidental.
  • Motive. Lack of motive does not excuse. In a murder trial, proof of motive is always relevant and never necessary.
  • Jury charge, defendant's objections. Some excerpts, standing alone, might have prejudiced the defendant, such as the statement that all unlawful homicide is presumed malicious. A homicide can be unlawful without being presumptively malicious, as with involuntary manslaughter. But the judge correctly said that the presumption of malice does not arise until the Commonwealth makes out a prima facie case of felonious homicide (Com. v. Wucherer). Read as a whole, the charge protected the defendant.
  • Jury charge, errors favoring the defendant. The judge told the jury that the Commonwealth had to prove an intentional killing and that an accidental death required acquittal. That framing forced a choice between first-degree murder and acquittal. The judge also said the jury should acquit if "the shot was accidental," though no evidence supported that finding. These errors prejudiced only the Commonwealth, so the appellant cannot complain of them.
  • Duty to clarify. Relying on Sears v. Birbeck, the court stressed the trial judge's duty to clarify the issues for the jury. A technically correct charge can still be useless to the jury. Concrete illustrations help.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Limits. The holding rests on an intentional act aimed at a vital part of the body with a known loaded gun. The court distinguished involuntary manslaughter: death caused by an unlawful act, or a lawful act done in an unlawful manner. Murder must result from an intentional, felonious act. An inadvertent or negligent act is insufficient.
  • Presumption of malice. The so-called presumption of malice arises only after the Commonwealth makes out a prima facie case of felonious homicide (Com. v. Wucherer). The court rejected the unqualified statement that all unlawful homicide is presumed malicious, citing Com. v. Drum's qualified formulation.
  • Intent to cause serious harm (footnote). If A maliciously beats B intending enormous bodily harm without killing, and B dies, A can be guilty of second-degree murder even though the death was "accidental" in the sense of unintended.
  • Means vs. results (footnote). The court cited Arnstein v. Metropolitan L. Ins. Co. and O'Neill v. Met. Life Ins. Co. on the difference between intentional means and accidental results.
  • "Russian poker" defined (footnote). Participants take turns placing one cartridge in a revolver, spinning the cylinder, putting the muzzle to their own temple, and pulling the trigger.
  • Jury illustrations (footnotes). The court praised concrete illustrations in jury charges, citing Com. v. Harman (Chief Justice Gibson on circumstantial evidence) and Com. v. Webster (Chief Justice Shaw's footprints-in-snow example).
  • Relied on: 4 Blackstone 199; Com. v. Drum; Commonwealth v. Divomte; Com. v. Mayberry; Com. v. Hillman; Com. v. Knox; Com. v. Arnold; Com. v. Wucherer; Sears v. Birbeck.
  • Overruled: None.
  • Disposition detail. The record was remitted to the trial court so the sentence could be carried out.
