Black-Letter Rule

Involuntary manslaughter at Massachusetts common law requires wanton or reckless conduct: intentional conduct, by commission or by omission where there is a duty to act, that involves a high degree of likelihood that substantial harm will result to another. The standard is both subjective and objective. A defendant is reckless if he actually realized the grave danger, or if an ordinary normal person in the same circumstances would have realized it. Negligence and gross negligence are not enough, because there is no "criminal negligence" at Massachusetts common law.

Facts

  • Control: Welansky owned all the stock of New Cocoanut Grove, Inc., which ran a Boston night club. He took all the profits, ran the corporation without regard to corporate forms, and dominated its officers and employees. He was not shown to have delegated responsibility for the number or condition of exits to anyone.
  • Absence: Welansky was hospitalized from November 16, 1942, through the night of the fire. He testified that he "knew it would be all right" and that "the same system" would continue. There was no evidence of any condition that night, apart from the match, that departed from usual practice before he fell ill.
  • Exits: Patrons' ordinary exits were a single revolving door and a door to Broadway from the new Cocktail Lounge. Emergency exits were defective:
  • The panic-bar door at the head of the Melody Lounge stairway was regularly key-locked.
  • The office vestibule door was blocked in the evening by a coat rack and clothing.
  • The main dining room's panic doors were hidden behind hooked Venetian doors with tables placed in front, and one panic door would not open on pressure.
  • Two Shawmut Street doors in areas closed to patrons were kept locked by Welansky's direction.
  • Building plans: Approved plans called for an exit and fire doors in the Cocktail Lounge. These had not been provided when Welansky last knew the premises, and their construction indicated he did not intend to provide them.
  • Crowd: On a busy Saturday night, the club held a crowd that could be found to exceed Welansky's own estimate of reasonable capacity. It could also be found the crowd was no larger than on earlier Saturdays.
  • Fire: A sixteen-year-old bar boy lit a match to find a light bulb in an artificial palm tree in the basement Melody Lounge. The palm and the low cloth ceiling ignited, and the fire spread rapidly through the club. The lights went out. Firemen found the locked exits and had to force them open. Bodies were piled at the exits, and many patrons died.
  • Electrical evidence: The Commonwealth introduced evidence of defective wiring. There was no evidence Welansky knew of it, or that it caused the fire or the deaths.

Procedural Posture

Welansky, his brother James Welansky, and employee Jacob Goldfine were indicted for manslaughter in two multi-count indictments. The codefendants were acquitted. Welansky was convicted on nineteen counts and sentenced to concurrent State prison terms of twelve to fifteen years. He appealed with one hundred twenty-four assignments of error.

Issue

Can the owner who controls premises open to the public be convicted of involuntary manslaughter for intentionally failing to provide safe means of escape for business invitees, where he did not cause the fire that killed them?

Holding

Yes. Where there is a duty of care to business visitors on premises the defendant controls, wanton or reckless conduct can consist of an intentional failure to take that care in disregard of probable harmful consequences. The Commonwealth need only prove that death resulted from the defendant's wanton or reckless disregard of patrons' safety in the event of fire from any cause. Judgments affirmed (Lummus, J.).

Reasoning

  • Omission as reckless conduct: Wanton or reckless conduct usually involves an affirmative act, such as driving or firing a gun. A duty of care to invited business visitors makes an intentional failure to act equally culpable.
  • Elements of wanton or reckless conduct:
  • Intentional conduct (an act, or an omission where there is a duty to act).
  • A high degree of likelihood that substantial harm will result to another.
  • Disregard of, or indifference to, probable consequences to that other or to that other's rights.
  • Intent goes to conduct: The conduct must be intended. The resulting harm need not be. "Wilful" adds nothing except confusion unless it modifies "wanton or reckless conduct." "Wanton" and "reckless" are practically synonymous and carry the same legal consequences.
  • Subjective and objective standard: The court approved the trial judge's charge:
  • If the defendant actually realized the grave danger, his later voluntary act or omission is reckless, whatever an ordinary person would have realized.
  • If the defendant was too "stupid" or "heedless" to see the danger, he is still reckless if an ordinary normal person would have realized it.
  • "A man may be reckless within the meaning of the law although he himself thought he was careful."
  • Knowing facts that would cause a reasonable person to know the danger equals knowing the danger.
  • Difference in kind: Wanton or reckless conduct differs from negligence and gross negligence in the degree of risk and the voluntary taking of risk. The difference is so marked that it amounts in law to a difference in kind. Criminal liability begins only once conduct crosses into recklessness.
  • Doctrinal chain to manslaughter:
  • Wanton or reckless conduct is the legal equivalent of intentional conduct.
  • Bodily injury caused by it is assault and battery.
  • Manslaughter is a battery that causes death.
  • So if death results, the actor is guilty of manslaughter.
  • Causation of the fire is irrelevant: "Fire in a place of public resort is an ever present danger." The Commonwealth did not need to prove Welansky caused the fire.
  • Personal fault: Criminal responsibility is personal. The Commonwealth bore the burden of showing the reckless failure was Welansky's and not that of someone to whom the duty had been entrusted.
  • The conditions that night matched his regular practice.
  • He had not delegated responsibility for exits.
  • So the acts that night were not "transitory and unauthorized acts" of servants that could relieve him of liability.
  • Corporate form: That the corporation could have been indicted did not shield Welansky. He fully controlled the corporation and could not escape criminal responsibility by using a corporate form.
  • Building code violation: Violating the statute requiring approved exits and fire doors is not negligence per se. Standing alone, it would not warrant a finding of wanton or reckless conduct. It may be considered with other evidence, so admitting it was proper.
  • Electrical evidence: With no proof of knowledge or causal relation, a guilty verdict could not lawfully rest on the wiring defects. The judge could admit the evidence initially. The defendant's remedy was a motion to strike or a limiting instruction, and he requested neither.
  • Pleading: The indictments, read with the Commonwealth's specifications, met art. 12 of the Declaration of Rights by giving reasonable knowledge of the crime charged. Particulars beyond that were discretionary.
  • The statutory short-form manslaughter indictment ("assault and beat . . . and by such assault and beating, did kill") may be used for involuntary manslaughter.
  • The motions to quash and the motion in arrest of judgment were properly denied.
  • Other trial issues:
  • The jury view and post-fire photographs were proper. Walls, most partitions, and some furniture remained, and any material changes could have been shown by evidence.
  • Evidence of Welansky's control, including fictitious corporate records, was admissible because he contested control until his own late testimony admitted it. Any prejudice was his own doing.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope of duty: Liability for omission rests on the duty owed to invited business visitors on premises the defendant controls. The court contrasted employer cases where, at common law, employees contractually assumed the risk of the factory as it appeared and could not recover for fire injuries (Jones v. Granite Mills; Keith v. Granite Mills; Pauley v. Steam Gauge & Lantern Co.; Huda v. American Glucose Co.). It criticized Cloutier v. Oakland Park Amusement Co. for failing to distinguish those cases from the invitee case.
  • *Disapproval of Query v. Howe: The court declined to give "unqualified approval" to language in Query v. Howe* and similar cases that disregard of another's rights must be as complete as though those rights did not exist. Taken literally, it would let a "trifling regard" for others excuse flagrant wrongdoing.
  • Terminology history: Older cases used "wilful negligence," "wanton negligence," "gross negligence," "culpable negligence," and "criminal negligence." The court read those cases as in fact requiring wanton or reckless conduct: "The terminology, not the law, is what has changed." Cases cited include Commonwealth v. Hartwell, Commonwealth v. Pierce, and Commonwealth v. Hawkins.
  • Expressly left open: Whether "gross negligence" in G. L. (Ter. Ed.) c. 265, § 30 really means wanton or reckless conduct "has not been decided."
  • Limits on the theory of liability: A conviction could not rest on the electrical defects. A statutory violation alone does not establish recklessness. Some specifications, such as employing minors, had "little or no relation" to reckless conduct.
  • Key precedents relied on:
  • Commonwealth v. Pierce: subjective-and-objective standard.
  • Banks v. Braman and Aiken v. Holyoke Street Railway: definition of wanton or reckless conduct, and omission where there is a duty.
  • Restatement of Torts § 500: definition of the standard.
  • Commonwealth v. Guillemette, Commonwealth v. Arone, Commonwealth v. Jones, and Commonwealth v. Maguire: no criminal negligence at common law.
  • Commonwealth v. Hawkins: reckless conduct causing injury is battery.
  • Minasian v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. and Commonwealth v. Velleco: manslaughter as a battery causing death.
  • Commonwealth v. Stevens and Commonwealth v. Anthony: personal fault, and nonliability for servants' transitory acts.
  • Commonwealth v. Hayes: art. 12 sufficiency standard.
  • Out-of-state authority: Queen v. Senior and State v. Benton.
  • Comparable cases noted:
  • Commonwealth v. Hendrick, a 1925 Superior Court trial over a night club building collapse, used a similar jury instruction but never reached the SJC.
  • Building collapse cases: People v. Buddensieck; State v. Ireland.
  • Fire case: Commonwealth v. Rhoads.
  • Other jurisdictions requiring the substantial equivalent of recklessness: People v. Angelo; People v. Lynn; State v. Cope.
