Black-Letter Rule

A complaint should not be dismissed for failure to state a claim unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove no set of facts in support of the claim that would entitle the plaintiff to relief. Under Rule 8(a)(2), a complaint need only give "a short and plain statement of the claim" that gives the defendant fair notice of what the claim is and the grounds on which it rests. The Rules do not require the plaintiff to plead detailed facts, because discovery and other pretrial procedures exist to sharpen the facts and issues.

Facts

  • Parties: Black members of the Brotherhood of Railway and Steamship Clerks sued the Brotherhood, its Local Union No. 28, and certain officers of both. They sued as a class on behalf of themselves and other Black employees similarly situated.
  • Bargaining relationship: Petitioners worked for the Texas and New Orleans Railroad at its Houston Freight House. Local 28 was their designated bargaining agent under the Railway Labor Act. The union's contract with the Railroad protected unit employees against discharge and loss of seniority.
  • Job losses: In May 1954, the Railroad purported to abolish 45 jobs held by Black employees, who were discharged or demoted. The complaint alleged the jobs were never abolished. Whites filled them, except where a few Black employees were rehired to their old jobs with loss of seniority.
  • Union inaction: Despite repeated pleas, the union, "acting according to plan," did nothing to protect petitioners and refused them protection comparable to what it gave white employees. The complaint also alleged the union generally failed to represent Black employees equally and in good faith.
  • Relief sought: Declaratory judgment, injunction, and damages for violation of the statutory right to fair representation.
  • Railroad not joined: The Railroad was not a defendant.

Procedural Posture

Respondents moved to dismiss on three grounds: exclusive Adjustment Board jurisdiction, failure to join the Railroad as an indispensable party, and failure to state a claim. The District Court dismissed for lack of jurisdiction without reaching the other grounds, the Fifth Circuit affirmed apparently on the same ground, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does a complaint state a claim under the Federal Rules when it alleges racially discriminatory representation by a union in general terms, without setting out specific facts supporting the allegations of discrimination?

Holding

Yes: under the "no set of facts" standard and Rule 8's notice-pleading requirement, the complaint stated a claim for breach of the union's statutory duty of fair representation, the courts below erred in dismissing for lack of jurisdiction, and the Railroad was not an indispensable party, so the judgment is reversed and remanded (opinion by Justice Black).

Reasoning

  • Jurisdiction. Section 3 First (i) of the Railway Labor Act covers only "disputes between an employee or group of employees and a carrier or carriers." This suit is between employees and their bargaining agent, so it falls outside that section. The Adjustment Board has no power under any provision of the Act to protect employees from discrimination by their union. The collective bargaining agreement is at most incidentally involved.
  • Reaching the other grounds. The District Court never ruled on joinder or failure to state a claim. The Court decided them anyway because both sides briefed and argued them and respondents asked for affirmance on those grounds.
  • Joinder. The Railroad's rights and interests are unaffected by a suit to enforce the union's duty of fair representation. No relief is sought from the Railroad, and none that could bind it is likely. If an issue later requires joinder, the Railroad or respondents can request it then.
  • Substantive claim. Steele and later cases hold that the Railway Labor Act prohibits racial discrimination in representation. If proven, the allegations show a "manifest breach" of the duty to represent all unit employees fairly and without hostile discrimination.
  • Duty continues after the contract is signed. The duty not to draw "irrelevant and invidious" distinctions does not end when the agreement is made. Collective bargaining is a continuing process that includes day-to-day contract adjustments, new problems, and protection of rights already secured. A facially fair contract can be administered in a flagrantly discriminatory way with the union's active or tacit consent.
  • Alternative remedies are no defense. Employees can file their own grievances or sue the employer, but that gives no sanction to the union's discrimination. The Act gives the majority's agent great power. Individuals and small groups lack comparable bargaining power, and a minority cannot choose another agent.
  • Pleading standard. Respondents argued the complaint lacked specific facts. The Federal Rules do not require a claimant to set out in detail the facts underlying the claim. Rule 8(a)(2) requires only a short and plain statement giving fair notice of the claim and its grounds. The illustrative forms appended to the Rules confirm this.
  • Why notice pleading works. Simplified pleading is possible because liberal discovery and other pretrial tools disclose the basis of claims and defenses and narrow disputed issues. The opinion lists motions for a more definite statement and to strike, the motion for judgment on the pleadings, pretrial conferences (Rule 16), discovery (Rules 26-37), summary judgment (Rule 56), and amendment (Rule 15).
  • Policy. Rule 8(f) directs that pleadings be construed "to do substantial justice." The Rules reject treating pleading as "a game of skill in which one misstep by counsel may be decisive." The purpose of pleading is to facilitate a proper decision on the merits.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Expressly left open: whether the union had any obligation to handle grievances at all. The Court held only that once a union undertakes to bargain or present grievances for some employees, it cannot refuse to do the same in good faith for others because of race.
  • Joinder ruling is provisional. It rests on the allegations and relief demanded. The Court left room for joinder if a later issue requires it.
  • Narrow posture. The Court held only that the complaint survives a motion to dismiss. It held that the allegations, "if proven," would establish a breach. It made no merits findings.
  • Relied on (duty of fair representation): Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co., the source of the "irrelevant and invidious" language; also Tunstall, Graham, and Howard as the line of cases following Steele. Wallace Corp. v. Labor Board and Syres v. Oil Workers International Union are cited with "cf."
  • Relied on (duty continues after the contract): Dillard v. Chesapeake & Ohio R. Co. and Hughes Tool Co. v. Labor Board.
  • Relied on ("no set of facts" rule): described as "the accepted rule," citing Leimer v. State Mutual Life Assur. Co., Dioguardi v. Durning, and Continental Collieries v. Shober.
  • Relied on (purpose of pleading): Maty v. Grasselli Chemical Co., cited with "cf."
  • Distinguished: Slocum v. Delaware, L. & W. R. Co. is "not applicable here" because this case involves no employee-carrier dispute.
  • Disapproved: Hayes v. Union Pacific R. Co. (a court of appeals decision relied on below), which the Court said "was decided incorrectly."
  • Rules discussed: Rule 8(a)(2) (short and plain statement); Rule 8(f) (construe pleadings to do substantial justice); the appended forms; the pretrial devices listed above.
