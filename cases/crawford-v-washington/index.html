Black-Letter Rule

Under the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause, testimonial statements of a witness absent from trial are admissible against a criminal defendant only if the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. A judicial finding that a testimonial statement is reliable cannot substitute for confrontation, so the Ohio v. Roberts "indicia of reliability" test no longer governs testimonial hearsay. "Testimonial" covers, at a minimum, prior testimony at a preliminary hearing, before a grand jury, or at a former trial, and statements made in police interrogations.

Facts

  • Parties: Petitioner Michael Crawford stabbed Kenneth Lee, who had allegedly tried to rape Crawford's wife, Sylvia.
  • Interrogations: After Miranda warnings, detectives interrogated Crawford and Sylvia twice each. Sylvia was in custody, was herself a potential suspect, and was told her release "depend[ed] on how the investigation continues."
  • Conflicting accounts: Crawford said he thought Lee pulled something out before the stabbing but was "not positive." Sylvia, answering often leading questions, described Lee reaching into his pocket and said Lee had nothing in his hands after the stabbing. Her account arguably undercut self-defense.
  • Charges and defense: The State charged assault and attempted murder. Crawford claimed self-defense.
  • Marital privilege: Washington's marital privilege kept Sylvia off the stand. The privilege does not reach a spouse's out-of-court statements that fit a hearsay exception.
  • Evidence at trial: The State offered Sylvia's tape-recorded police statement as a statement against penal interest, since she had led Crawford to Lee's apartment. Crawford never had a chance to cross-examine her.
  • Use at trial: The prosecutor played the tape and called it "damning evidence" that "completely refutes [petitioner's] claim of self-defense." The jury convicted Crawford of assault.

Procedural Posture

The trial court admitted the statement under Roberts as bearing particularized guarantees of trustworthiness, and the jury convicted. The Washington Court of Appeals reversed on a nine-factor reliability test, the Washington Supreme Court unanimously reinstated the conviction on the theory that the spouses' statements "interlocked," and the U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Did the State violate the Confrontation Clause by admitting Sylvia's tape-recorded statement to police against Crawford when he had no opportunity to cross-examine her?

Holding

Yes. Testimonial statements of an absent witness are admissible only where the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine, Sylvia's police-interrogation statement was testimonial and untested by cross-examination, and so the judgment of the Washington Supreme Court is reversed and remanded (opinion by Justice Scalia; the opinion text does not state the vote).

Reasoning

  • Text is indeterminate. "Witnesses against" could mean in-court witnesses, anyone whose statements are offered, or something in between. The Court turns to history.
  • History, England. The common-law tradition favored live testimony under adversarial testing. The Marian statutes brought in civil-law-style pretrial examinations by justices of the peace. Raleigh's trial, where Cobham's examination and letter were read to the jury over Raleigh's demand to face him, is the paradigm abuse. King v. Paine (1696) settled that an absent witness's examination was inadmissible without a prior opportunity to cross-examine. By 1791, courts applied that rule even to justice-of-the-peace examinations in felony cases.
  • History, America. Colonists protested ex parte examinations and admiralty procedure. Revolutionary-era declarations of rights guaranteed confrontation. Antifederalists objected to the Constitution's omission of the right. Early state cases (State v. Webb, State v. Campbell) required a prior opportunity to cross-examine.
  • First inference: the Clause targets testimonial statements. The principal evil was the civil-law use of ex parte examinations as evidence. The Clause does not apply only to in-court testimony, and its reach does not depend on "the law of Evidence for the time being." "Witnesses" are those who "bear testimony," and testimony is "[a] solemn declaration or affirmation made for the purpose of establishing or proving some fact." A formal statement to government officers is testimonial. A casual remark to an acquaintance is not.
  • Police interrogations are testimonial. They closely resemble Marian examinations. The lack of an oath is not dispositive, since Cobham's examination was unsworn. Police performing the investigative role are no different from justices of the peace, who had an investigative and prosecutorial function.
  • Second inference: unavailability plus prior cross-examination is required. The Clause is read as the common-law right, admitting only exceptions established at the founding. Prior cross-examination was a necessary condition, not merely one way to show reliability. Founding-era hearsay exceptions mostly covered statements that were by their nature nontestimonial, such as business records and statements in furtherance of a conspiracy.
  • Case law fits the rule in its results. Testimonial statements of absent witnesses have been admitted only where the declarant was unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. Reliability factors were considered when the statement was nontestimonial (Dutton v. Evans).
  • Roberts' rationale is wrong in two directions. It is too broad, because it applies the same scrutiny to hearsay far from the Clause's core concerns. It is too narrow, because it admits ex parte testimony on a mere finding of reliability.
  • Confrontation is a procedural guarantee. The Clause's goal is reliability, but it "commands, not that evidence be reliable, but that reliability be assessed in a particular manner: by testing in the crucible of cross-examination." Roberts substitutes a "wholly foreign" method. "Dispensing with confrontation because testimony is obviously reliable is akin to dispensing with jury trial because a defendant is obviously guilty."
  • Roberts in practice is unpredictable. Courts use multi-factor tests and treat opposite facts as signs of reliability (detailed vs. fleeting, custody vs. no custody, immediate vs. two years later). Its "unpardonable vice" is admitting core testimonial statements, including accomplice confessions, plea allocutions, grand jury testimony, and prior trial testimony. Some courts found reliability in the very features that make a statement testimonial.
  • This case shows the failure. Three Washington courts reached inconsistent reliability conclusions on different factors. Each relied on assumptions cross-examination might have undermined: Sylvia said she shut her eyes and was "in shock," and the "interlocking" ambiguity made testing more necessary.
  • The Court declines to reweigh reliability factors. Doing so would "perpetuate, not avoid, what the Sixth Amendment condemns." The Framers distrusted judicial discretion, and vague standards offer little protection in politically charged cases like Raleigh's.
  • Nontestimonial hearsay. States may have flexibility in developing hearsay law for nontestimonial statements, either under Roberts or under an approach that exempts them from Confrontation Clause scrutiny altogether.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Chief Justice Rehnquist, joined by Justice O'Connor, concurring in the judgment. Agrees the statement was inadmissible but dissents from overruling Roberts. The testimonial/nontestimonial line is no better rooted in history than existing doctrine: at common law, unsworn out-of-court statements generally were not substantive evidence at all, so the Framers' concern was sworn affidavits and depositions, and any broader "testimonial" category is an arbitrary "proxy." Founding-era English and state law was not settled enough to support a categorical exclusion rule, and hearsay exceptions (co-conspirator statements, spontaneous declarations, medical-treatment statements, dying declarations) rest on reliability that being testimonial does not undermine. Cross-examination is a functional tool and may be "superfluous" in a given case. Stare decisis counsels against overruling a long-established precedent, and leaving "testimonial" undefined leaves prosecutors without answers. The result follows from Idaho v. Wright, which held a statement is not admissible merely because other evidence corroborates it, so overruling Roberts was unnecessary.

Notes

  • Rule structure for outline. (1) Is the statement testimonial? (2) Is the declarant absent from trial? (3) If so, admissible only if the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. Reliability is irrelevant to testimonial statements.
  • Expressly left open.
  • A comprehensive definition of "testimonial." The Court lists three candidate formulations (petitioner's, the White concurrence's, the NACDL amicus's) without choosing.
  • Whether White v. Illinois's rejection of limiting the Clause to testimonial statements survives. The Court says its analysis "casts doubt" on that holding but does not resolve it.
  • Whether the Sixth Amendment includes an exception for testimonial dying declarations. If so, it is "sui generis."
  • The definition of "interrogation." The term is used colloquially, and Sylvia's structured, recorded statement qualifies "under any conceivable definition."
  • Waiver through the marital privilege and harmless error. The State did not challenge the Washington Supreme Court's rejection of waiver or the Court of Appeals' finding of harmfulness, and the Court expresses no opinion.
  • Limits the Court states.
  • When the declarant appears for cross-examination at trial, the Clause places no constraints on use of prior testimonial statements (California v. Green).
  • The Clause does not bar testimonial statements used for purposes other than the truth of the matter asserted (Tennessee v. Street).
  • Forfeiture by wrongdoing is accepted. It rests on equitable grounds and does not claim to measure reliability (Reynolds v. United States).
  • Business records and statements in furtherance of a conspiracy are described as by their nature nontestimonial.
  • Overruled or displaced. Ohio v. Roberts' reliability framework no longer governs testimonial statements. The majority rejects its rationale while noting its result (preliminary-hearing testimony with prior examination) fits the rule. The concurrence describes the decision as overruling Roberts.
  • Relied on. Mattox v. United States (prior trial testimony admissible given prior cross-examination; Clause read as the common-law right); Pointer v. Texas (Clause applies to the States); Mancusi v. Stubbs, California v. Green, Kirby v. United States (prior testimony requires adequate opportunity to cross-examine); Barber v. Page, Motes v. United States (unavailability required even with prior cross-examination); Bruton v. United States, Douglas v. Alabama, Roberts v. Russell (accomplice confessions excluded); Lilly v. Virginia (testimonial statements excluded; accomplice confessions not firmly rooted); Bourjaily v. United States and Dutton v. Evans (nontestimonial statements analyzed under broader tests).
  • Distinguished. Lee v. Illinois did not create an "interlocking confession" exception. It rejected the State's premise that the statements interlocked, and the inference the State drew is "possible" but not "inevitable." Parker v. Randolph addressed limiting instructions in joint trials. White v. Illinois is "arguably in tension," but it decided only whether the Clause imposes an unavailability requirement. United States v. Burr was fundamentally about hearsay rules for conspiracy statements.
