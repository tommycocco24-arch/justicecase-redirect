Black-Letter Rule

A trial court has no Sixth Amendment duty to inquire into multiple representation on its own unless it knows or reasonably should know that a particular conflict exists. A defendant who raised no objection at trial must show that an actual conflict of interest adversely affected the lawyer's performance. Once the defendant shows this, prejudice is presumed and need not be proven. The rule applies equally to retained and appointed counsel, because the State's conduct of the trial supplies the state action.

Facts

  • Charges: Sullivan, Carchidi, and DiPasquale were indicted for the first-degree murders of a labor official and his companion, who were shot at a Teamsters' Local 107 office in Philadelphia.
  • Key witness: A janitor, McGrath, saw the three defendants in the building just before the shooting. After he heard what sounded like firecrackers, Carchidi told him to leave and say nothing.
  • Joint representation: Two privately retained lawyers, DiBona and Peruto, represented all three defendants in the state proceedings. Sullivan accepted them because he could not afford his own lawyer. Friends of the three defendants paid part of the fee. None of it came from Sullivan or his family.
  • No objection: Neither Sullivan nor his lawyers ever objected to the multiple representation.
  • Sullivan's trial: Sullivan was tried first and separately. The evidence was entirely circumstantial and consisted mainly of McGrath's testimony. The defense rested without presenting evidence. The jury convicted, and Sullivan received life imprisonment. Both codefendants were later acquitted at separate trials.
  • Conflicting post-conviction testimony: Peruto testified that he did not want the defense to go on because it would expose defense witnesses for the two upcoming trials. DiBona testified that he had encouraged Sullivan to testify. Other evidence suggested Sullivan did not want to testify because cross-examination might reveal an extramarital affair. Carchidi said he would have testified to rebut McGrath.

Procedural Posture

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court affirmed the conviction, and on a second direct appeal it found "no dual representation in the true sense" and affirmed the denial of collateral relief. The District Court denied habeas relief, the Third Circuit reversed on the ground that "some showing of a possible conflict of interest" was enough, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve issues left open in Holloway v. Arkansas.

Issue

Can a state prisoner who did not object at trial obtain federal habeas relief by showing only that his retained counsel's multiple representation created a possible conflict of interest?

Holding

No: the possibility of conflict is insufficient to impugn a conviction, and a defendant who raised no objection at trial must establish that an actual conflict of interest adversely affected his lawyer's performance, so the judgment was vacated and remanded (opinion by Justice Powell; the vote is not stated in the opinion text).

Reasoning

  • Scope of habeas review (Part II): The 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d) presumption of correctness covers only basic, primary, or historical facts (Townsend v. Sain, quoting Brown v. Allen). The lawyers' actual roles are facts in this sense. Whether those roles amount to "multiple representation" is a mixed question of law and fact (cf. Brewer v. Williams; Neil v. Biggers). A federal court may review that question independently.
  • Multiple representation existed: Both lawyers prepared the defense with all three defendants, both advised Sullivan on resting, and both played important roles at all three trials. The trial transcript shows that Peruto, the lawyer who claimed he only assisted, actually rested Sullivan's defense.
  • State action and retained counsel (Part III): A state criminal trial is state action (Lisenba v. California; Moore v. Dempsey). Under Gideon v. Wainwright and Johnson v. Zerbst, a trial without adequate counsel risks injustice, and the State that convicts through such a trial deprives the defendant of liberty (Argersinger v. Hamlin). Inadequate assistance does not satisfy the Sixth Amendment (McMann v. Richardson; Tollett v. Henderson; Brooks v. Tennessee; Ferguson v. Georgia; Geders; Herring). The "often uninformed decision to retain a particular lawyer" cannot reduce constitutional protection. There is no basis for treating retained and appointed counsel differently.
  • No automatic duty to inquire (Part IV-A): Holloway requires trial courts to investigate timely objections. Nothing requires them to start an inquiry in every case of joint representation. Defense counsel have an ethical obligation to avoid conflicting representations and to advise the court promptly when a conflict arises. The Court cites ABA Code DR 5-105, EC 5-15, and ABA Defense Function Standard § 3.5(b). Absent special circumstances, a trial court may assume either that there is no conflict or that the lawyer and clients knowingly accept the risk. The attorney is "in the best position professionally and ethically" to spot a conflict (Holloway, quoting State v. Davis).
  • Applied to Sullivan: No duty to inquire arose. Separate trials reduced the potential for divergent interests. No one objected. The opening argument was consistent with the view that none of the defendants was involved and indicated a willingness to call witnesses. Resting was, on its face, a reasonable tactic given the weak circumstantial case.
  • Actual conflict required (Part IV-B): A possible conflict exists in almost every case of multiple representation. Presuming ineffectiveness from mere possibility would bar joint representation even where "a common defense . . . gives strength against a common attack" (Holloway, quoting Frankfurter's dissent in Glasser).
  • Precedent contrasted: In Glasser v. United States, counsel failed to cross-examine a key witness and failed to resist arguably inadmissible evidence in order to protect a codefendant. This "struggle to serve two masters" was an actual conflict, and the conviction was reversed. In Dukes v. Warden, the record showed no actual lapse in representation, so habeas relief was denied.
  • No prejudice requirement: Glasser establishes that unconstitutional multiple representation is never harmless error. Once an actual conflict adversely affects performance, the defendant need not show prejudice (Holloway). Until the defendant shows that counsel "actively represented conflicting interests," the constitutional predicate is missing.
  • Remand (Part IV-C): Sullivan relied on Peruto's admission. The State relied on DiBona's contrary testimony and on Sullivan's own reluctance to testify. The Third Circuit had not weighed this evidence under the correct standard.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Brennan (concurring in Part III and in the result): Brennan agrees that ineffective retained counsel involves state action. He rejects Part IV. He argues that a choice to share counsel must be knowing and intelligent, so the trial judge must play a positive role. Once joint representation is discovered, which charging papers and appearances usually reveal, the court must at least advise defendants of the hazards. Silence cannot be treated as a knowing choice. If the record had shown a knowing choice, Brennan would accept the majority's standard. Because Sullivan received no advice and showed a significant possibility of conflict, Brennan would presume his representation suffered and remand so the State could rebut that presumption. He also argues that here the separate trials helped create the divergence of interests.
  • Justice Marshall (concurring in part and dissenting in part): Marshall joins Parts I through III. He agrees with Brennan that the trial court must inquire, warn defendants of the risks, and confirm an informed choice. He dissents from the standard. In his view, the test should be whether an "actual, relevant conflict of interests" existed. If one did, the conviction must be reversed. Requiring adverse effect is "unduly harsh" and "incurably speculative," since the evil lies in what counsel refrains from doing (Holloway). Lawyers will rarely testify against themselves, as Peruto did. Drawing on ABA Canon 6 and DR 5-105, he defines the terms. A possible conflict exists if the clients' interests may diverge and place the attorney under inconsistent duties. An actual conflict exists if their interests do diverge on a material factual or legal issue or a course of action. He would affirm the Third Circuit. He also notes that if "actively represented conflicting interests" means acting under the constraint of inconsistent duties, his view and the Court's "may not be so far apart." Finally, he says an informed choice of counsel would not operate as a waiver of later conflict claims.

Notes

  • Scope: The actual-conflict/adverse-effect standard applies to a defendant who raised no objection at trial. Holloway governs when there was a timely objection and the court failed to investigate.
  • Trigger for judicial inquiry: The duty to inquire arises only when the court "knows or reasonably should know that a particular conflict exists." The opinion also refers to "special circumstances."
  • No finding on the merits: The Court did not decide whether Sullivan's lawyers had an actual conflict. It remanded for that determination.
  • Expressly left open: In a footnote, the Court declined to decide whether an appellate court's statements can count as "determination[s] after a hearing on the merits" under § 2254(d).
  • State-action question: The petitioners did not raise this argument below. The Court addressed it as a predicate to the question on which certiorari was granted (Vance v. Terrazas).
  • Supervisory practice: In a footnote, the Court described federal appellate courts' supervisory-power rules requiring inquiry into joint representation as "a desirable practice," citing proposed Fed. R. Crim. P. 44(c). That practice rests on supervisory power, not the Sixth Amendment.
  • Professional Responsibility hook: The constitutional rule rests on defense counsel's ethical duty to avoid conflicts and to alert the court. A footnote cites a survey: 70% of responding public defender offices had a strong policy against multiple representation in criminal cases, and 49% never undertook it. It adds that the private bar "may be less alert" to the issue.
  • Precedent rejected: The Court rejected the Third Circuit's rule (from Walker v. United States) that "some showing of a possible conflict of interest or prejudice, however remote" warrants reversal. A footnote states that a substantial majority of the circuits already required an actual conflict.
  • Relied on: Holloway v. Arkansas; Glasser v. United States; Gideon v. Wainwright; Johnson v. Zerbst; Argersinger v. Hamlin; Townsend v. Sain; McMann v. Richardson.
  • Distinguished: Glasser (actual conflict shown, conviction reversed) and Dukes v. Warden (no actual lapse, relief denied). Holloway is distinguished on the ground that it involved timely, repeated objections.
  • Overruled: None.
