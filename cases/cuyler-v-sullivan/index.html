Black-Letter Rule

A defendant who raised no objection at trial to multiple representation must show that an actual conflict of interest adversely affected his lawyer's performance; the mere possibility of conflict is not enough. Once he shows that, he need not show prejudice. Absent an objection, a trial court has no Sixth Amendment duty to inquire into multiple representation unless it knows or reasonably should know that a particular conflict exists, and these rules apply equally to retained and appointed counsel.

Facts

  • Crime: Sullivan, Carchidi, and DiPasquale were indicted for the first-degree murders of a labor official and his companion, shot in the official's office at a Teamsters' local headquarters in Philadelphia.
  • Representation: Two privately retained lawyers, DiBona and Peruto, represented all three defendants. Sullivan accepted them because he could not afford his own lawyer. Their fees came partly from funds raised by friends of the three defendants, and none came from Sullivan or his family.
  • No objection: Neither Sullivan nor his lawyers ever objected to the multiple representation.
  • Separate trials: Sullivan was tried first. The evidence was entirely circumstantial, mainly the testimony of a janitor, McGrath. The defense rested without presenting evidence. The jury convicted and fixed life imprisonment. The codefendants were later acquitted at separate trials.
  • Conflicting testimony at postconviction hearing: DiBona said he encouraged Sullivan to testify. Peruto said he did not want the defense to go on because it would expose defense witnesses for the two upcoming trials. Other testimony suggested Sullivan did not want to testify because cross-examination might reveal an extramarital affair. Carchidi said he would have testified to rebut McGrath.

Procedural Posture

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court affirmed the conviction and denial of state collateral relief, finding no true dual representation; the federal District Court denied habeas, and the Third Circuit reversed, holding that any showing of a possible conflict required reversal. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve issues left open in Holloway v. Arkansas.

Issue

Can a state prisoner obtain federal habeas relief on a Sixth Amendment claim by showing only that his retained counsel, to whose multiple representation he never objected, represented potentially conflicting interests?

Holding

No; absent an objection at trial, a defendant must show that an actual conflict of interest adversely affected his lawyer's performance, so the Third Circuit's possible-conflict standard was wrong, and the judgment is vacated and remanded (opinion by Justice Powell; the text does not state the vote).

Reasoning

  • Scope of habeas review (Part II): Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), only state-court findings of basic, primary, or historical fact get a presumption of correctness (Townsend v. Sain). Findings about the roles DiBona and Peruto played are facts. Whether those roles amounted to multiple representation is a mixed question of law and fact, open to federal review. The Court agreed with the Third Circuit that both lawyers represented all three defendants, noting that Peruto, not DiBona, rested Sullivan's defense.
  • State action and retained counsel (Part III): A state criminal trial is state action (Lisenba, Moore v. Dempsey). Under Gideon and Johnson v. Zerbst, a trial without adequate counsel infects the trial itself, so a conviction obtained that way is a state deprivation of liberty. The Sixth Amendment requires adequate assistance, not just appointment (McMann v. Richardson, Brooks v. Tennessee, Ferguson v. Georgia). The often uninformed choice to retain a lawyer cannot reduce constitutional protection, so retained and appointed counsel are treated the same.
  • No automatic duty to inquire (Part IV-A): Holloway requires trial courts to investigate timely objections. Nothing requires courts to start an inquiry in every case. Defense counsel have an ethical obligation to avoid conflicting representations and to tell the court promptly when a conflict arises. Trial courts may therefore assume, absent special circumstances, that there is no conflict or that lawyer and clients knowingly accept the risk. The trial court must inquire only if it knows or reasonably should know that a particular conflict exists.
  • Application of the inquiry rule: No duty arose here. Separate trials reduced the potential for divergent interests. No one objected. DiBona's opening was consistent with a defense that none of the defendants was involved and suggested willingness to call witnesses needed at later trials. Resting the defense was facially a reasonable tactic given weak circumstantial evidence.
  • Actual conflict standard (Part IV-B): Holloway reaffirmed that multiple representation violates the Sixth Amendment only if it produces a conflict. Because a possible conflict exists in almost every multiple representation, presuming ineffectiveness from possibility alone would bar multiple representation even where a common defense helps against a common attack.
  • Precedent contrasted: In Glasser, counsel failed to cross-examine a key witness and failed to resist arguably inadmissible evidence to protect a codefendant, an actual conflict that impaired the defense. In Dukes v. Warden, the record showed no actual lapse in representation, so relief was denied.
  • No prejudice requirement: Glasser established that unconstitutional multiple representation is never harmless error. Once a defendant shows a conflict actually affected the adequacy of his representation, he need not show prejudice. Until he shows counsel actively represented conflicting interests, he has not established the constitutional predicate.
  • Policy points: Defense counsel is best placed to identify conflicts; trial courts rely on counsel's good faith. Footnotes endorse federal courts' use of supervisory power to require inquiry (proposed Fed. R. Crim. P. 44(c)) as desirable practice, distinct from a constitutional command.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Brennan (concurring in Part III and in the result): Agrees that retained counsel's ineffectiveness involves state action. Rejects Part IV. Because defendants' choice of joint representation must be knowing and intelligent (Johnson v. Zerbst, Glasser), the trial court must find out about joint representation and at least affirmatively advise defendants of its hazards. Silence is not a knowing choice. Where no such advice appears on the record, a defendant who shows a significant possibility of conflict should get a presumption that his representation suffered, which the State may rebut on remand. Also argues that here separate trials created, rather than reduced, the potential for divergence.
  • Justice Marshall (concurring in part and dissenting in part): Joins Parts I through III. Agrees with Brennan that trial courts must inquire into multiple representation, warn of the risks, and confirm informed choice, though such a determination would not waive later conflict claims. Dissents from the standard: the question should be whether an actual, relevant conflict existed, and if so, reversal follows. Requiring a showing of adverse effect is harsh, speculative, and often impossible, since the harm lies in what counsel refrains from doing (Holloway). Defines an actual conflict as divergence of clients' interests on a material factual or legal issue or course of action, drawing on ABA Canon 6, DR 5-105, and the ABA Defense Function Standards. Notes that if the majority's "actively represented conflicting interests" language means acting under inconsistent duties is itself adverse effect, the two views may be close. Would affirm the Third Circuit.

Notes

  • Limits of the holding: The actual-conflict/adverse-effect standard governs defendants who raised no objection at trial. Where an objection is made and the court fails to give the defendant a chance to show the conflict, Holloway controls.
  • Remand: The Court did not decide whether Sullivan met the standard. The Third Circuit must weigh Peruto's admission against DiBona's contrary testimony and evidence that Sullivan himself wanted to avoid testifying.
  • Expressly left open: Whether statements of a state appellate court that heard no evidence can be "determination[s] after a hearing on the merits of a factual issue" under § 2254(d).
  • State action issue: Addressed although not raised in the Court of Appeals, because it was a predicate to the question presented and both sides briefed it.
  • Relied on: Holloway v. Arkansas (duty to investigate timely objections; multiple representation alone not a violation; no prejudice requirement); Glasser v. United States (actual conflict, never harmless error); Dukes v. Warden (no relief without actual lapse); Townsend v. Sain (fact versus law); Gideon, Johnson v. Zerbst, Argersinger, McMann (right to adequate counsel).
  • Distinguished: Dukes contrasted with Glasser on whether an actual lapse appeared in the record.
  • Rejected: The Third Circuit's rule from Walker v. United States that "some showing of a possible conflict of interest or prejudice, however remote" requires reversal.
  • Professional Responsibility tie-in: The majority places primary responsibility for spotting conflicts on defense counsel's ethical obligations (ABA Code DR 5-105, EC 5-15; ABA Defense Function § 3.5(b)). A footnote cites a survey that most public defender offices have a strong policy against multiple representation and suggests the private bar may be less alert to the danger.
  • Overruled: None identified in the opinion.
