Black-Letter Rule

A court may exercise general (all-purpose) jurisdiction over a foreign corporation only when the corporation's affiliations with the forum State are so continuous and systematic as to render it essentially at home there. The paradigm forums are the corporation's place of incorporation and its principal place of business. A substantial, continuous, and systematic course of business in a State does not by itself suffice, and the at-home inquiry appraises the corporation's activities in their entirety, nationwide and worldwide.

Facts

  • Parties: Twenty-two plaintiffs, all residents of Argentina (one a citizen of Chile, the rest Argentine citizens), sued Daimler, a German public stock company headquartered in Stuttgart that manufactures Mercedes-Benz vehicles primarily in Germany.
  • Claims: Plaintiffs alleged that Daimler's Argentinian subsidiary, MB Argentina, collaborated with state security forces during Argentina's 1976-1983 "Dirty War" to kidnap, detain, torture, and kill certain MB Argentina workers, including plaintiffs or their close relatives. They sought to hold Daimler vicariously liable under the Alien Tort Statute, the Torture Victim Protection Act, and California and Argentine law (wrongful death and intentional infliction of emotional distress).
  • Location of conduct: The alleged events centered on MB Argentina's plant in Gonzalez Catan, Argentina. No part of the alleged collaboration took place in California or anywhere else in the United States.
  • MBUSA: Jurisdiction rested on the California contacts of Mercedes-Benz USA (MBUSA), an indirect Daimler subsidiary organized in Delaware with its principal place of business in New Jersey. MBUSA is Daimler's exclusive U.S. importer and distributor and sells to independent dealerships nationwide. MBUSA was not a defendant.
  • California contacts: MBUSA has a regional office in Costa Mesa, a Vehicle Preparation Center in Carson, and a Classic Center in Irvine. It is the largest supplier of luxury vehicles to the California market, and its California sales account for 2.4% of Daimler's worldwide sales.
  • Contract: The General Distributor Agreement labels MBUSA an independent contractor buying and selling for its own account, disclaims any agency relationship, and denies MBUSA authority to bind Daimler.
  • Plaintiffs' theory: At oral argument, plaintiffs' counsel agreed their theory would allow Polish plaintiffs injured in a Daimler vehicle rollover in Poland to sue Daimler in California.

Procedural Posture

After jurisdictional discovery, the Northern District of California dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction, finding Daimler's own California contacts insufficient and refusing to impute MBUSA's contacts on an agency theory. A Ninth Circuit panel first affirmed over Judge Reinhardt's dissent, then on rehearing replaced that opinion with one by Judge Reinhardt sustaining jurisdiction on an agency theory; the Ninth Circuit denied rehearing en banc over the dissent of eight judges, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permit a California court to exercise general jurisdiction over Daimler, a German corporation, based on its U.S. subsidiary's California contacts, for claims by foreign plaintiffs arising from conduct entirely outside the United States?

Holding

No. Even assuming MBUSA is at home in California and its contacts are imputable to Daimler, Daimler is not essentially at home in California, so due process bars general jurisdiction there; Justice Ginsburg wrote for the Court, and the Ninth Circuit's judgment was reversed.

Reasoning

  • Framework. Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 4(k)(1)(A), federal courts ordinarily follow state law on personal jurisdiction. California's long-arm statute reaches the full extent of the Constitution, so the only question is federal due process.
  • History. Pennoyer v. Neff tied jurisdiction to territorial bounds. International Shoe replaced that with minimum contacts and "fair play and substantial justice," shifting focus to the relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation (Shaffer v. Heitner).
  • Two categories. Specific jurisdiction covers suits that arise out of or relate to the defendant's forum contacts (Helicopteros). General jurisdiction covers any and all claims, but only where the corporation is essentially at home in the forum (Goodyear). Specific jurisdiction has become the centerpiece of modern doctrine, while general jurisdiction plays a reduced role and has not been stretched beyond traditional limits.
  • Precedent line on general jurisdiction.
  • Perkins v. Benguet: the "textbook case." Ohio could exercise general jurisdiction because, during the wartime shutdown, the company's president directed all its activities from Ohio, making Ohio its principal, if temporary, place of business.
  • Helicopteros: purchases, training trips, and a negotiation session in Texas were not continuous and systematic general business contacts. Mere purchases, even regular ones, are not enough.
  • Goodyear: stream-of-commerce sales of a small percentage of tires in North Carolina could support specific jurisdiction at most; they could not make foreign subsidiaries at home there.
  • Agency theory (Part IV-A). The Court did not decide whether an agency theory can ever support general jurisdiction. It held the Ninth Circuit's version could not stand in any event.
  • The Ninth Circuit asked whether MBUSA's services were so "important" that Daimler would perform them itself if MBUSA did not exist. That test "stacks the deck," because anything a corporation does through a subsidiary or distributor is something it would otherwise do by other means.
  • Applied that way, the theory would subject a foreign corporation to general jurisdiction wherever it has an in-state subsidiary or affiliate, sweeping beyond even the "sprawling view" rejected in Goodyear.
  • In a footnote, the Court added that the Ninth Circuit's "control" inquiry, which relied on a contract expressly disclaiming agency, did little to cure the overbreadth.
  • At-home analysis (Part IV-B).
  • Place of incorporation and principal place of business are the paradigm bases. They are unique and easily ascertainable, and they guarantee plaintiffs at least one clear forum for any claim. The Court cited Hertz Corp. v. Friend for the value of simple jurisdictional rules.
  • Goodyear identified those places as paradigms without limiting general jurisdiction to them. Plaintiffs' proposed test of general jurisdiction wherever a corporation "engages in a substantial, continuous, and systematic course of business" is "unacceptably grasping."
  • International Shoe used "continuous and systematic" to describe specific jurisdiction. Its general-jurisdiction language required operations "so substantial and of such a nature" as to justify suit on unrelated claims. The question is whether affiliations render the corporation essentially at home, meaning comparable to a domestic enterprise.
  • Neither Daimler nor MBUSA is incorporated or headquartered in California. If these contacts sufficed, the same global reach would exist in every State where MBUSA's sales are sizable. That would deny defendants the minimum predictability Burger King requires.
  • Footnote 20: the inquiry does not focus solely on the magnitude of in-state contacts. It appraises the corporation's activities in their entirety, nationwide and worldwide, because a corporation operating in many places can scarcely be at home in all of them.
  • Reasonableness prong. The Court, in a footnote, rejected Justice Sotomayor's approach. The Asahi multifactor reasonableness check is a second step for specific jurisdiction and is not a free-floating test. When a corporation is genuinely at home, a second-step inquiry is superfluous, and importing the Asahi checklist would complicate an issue that should be resolved at the outset.
  • Transnational context (Part IV-C).
  • The Ninth Circuit's reliance on U.S. interests in redressing human-rights abuses was undercut because the ATS and TVPA claims were infirm after Kiobel (presumption against extraterritoriality) and Mohamad (TVPA reaches only natural persons).
  • The Ninth Circuit ignored international comity. The EU allows general suit only at a corporation's statutory seat, central administration, or principal place of business. The Solicitor General reported that expansive U.S. general jurisdiction has impeded judgment-recognition negotiations and could discourage foreign investment.
  • These considerations reinforced the conclusion that jurisdiction would not accord with fair play and substantial justice.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Sotomayor, concurring in the judgment. No other Justice is listed as joining in the opinion text.
  • Ground for decision: She would reverse under the reasonableness prong (Burger King; Asahi factors). Foreign plaintiffs were suing a foreign defendant over foreign conduct, had not shown California was more convenient, and Germany had a far greater interest. She would leave open whether reasonableness applies to general jurisdiction, since plaintiffs conceded it applied throughout the litigation.
  • Process objection: The majority decided an issue not argued or passed on below, raised by Daimler only in a footnote of its merits brief, and outside the question presented. The record was undeveloped on facts that could matter under Perkins, such as where key files were kept and whether California employees made strategic decisions.
  • Substance objection: She read the majority as adopting a new "proportionality" rule. Under that rule, a corporation's in-state contacts must be weighed against its worldwide activities, so that Daimler is "too big for general jurisdiction." Prior cases (Perkins, Helicopteros, Goodyear) looked only at in-state contacts. Reciprocal fairness turns on the benefits a defendant takes from the forum, so contacts elsewhere are immaterial.
  • Perkins: She argued Perkins described Ohio as a "limited" part of the company's business, with significant operations, managers, and board meetings elsewhere. In her view, the proportionality test would have reversed the result in Perkins.
  • Predictability: She argued the majority's test is less predictable and will expand jurisdictional discovery into every forum where a company does business.
  • Concession: Daimler's concession that MBUSA is at home in California, combined with the assumption that MBUSA's contacts are attributable, should have been dispositive.
  • Alternatives: Forum non conveniens, 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), reasonableness, and legislative amendment of long-arm statutes could address unfairness without a narrow constitutional rule.
  • Injustices: She identified four. The rule curtails state sovereignty over companies that divide management among several States. It favors large conglomerates over small businesses. It produces an incongruity with Burnham tag jurisdiction. It shifts risk of loss to injured individuals, possibly leaving them no U.S. forum.
  • Circuit precedent: She noted every circuit to consider it had applied reasonableness to general jurisdiction, and that the majority's footnote discards those cases without briefing.

Notes

  • Assumptions, not holdings: The Court assumed, for this decision only, that MBUSA is at home in California, because Daimler did not contest the point below. It also assumed MBUSA's contacts could be imputed to Daimler.
  • Expressly left open:
  • Whether an agency theory can ever support general jurisdiction over a parent. The Court noted a subsidiary might be an agent for some purposes and not others.
  • Whether, in an exceptional case like Perkins, a corporation can be at home somewhere other than its place of incorporation or principal place of business (footnote 19). The Court said Daimler's California activities "plainly do not approach that level."
  • Not at issue:
  • Plaintiffs never invoked specific jurisdiction.
  • Plaintiffs did not appeal the ruling that Daimler's own contacts were too sporadic.
  • Plaintiffs never argued MBUSA was Daimler's alter ego. The Court noted several circuits permit imputation of a subsidiary's contacts only on an alter-ego showing.
  • Agency and specific jurisdiction: A footnote recognizes agency relationships may be relevant to specific jurisdiction, since a corporation can purposefully avail itself of a forum through agents or distributors (International Shoe; Asahi, O'Connor, J.). It does not inevitably follow that the same reasoning applies to general jurisdiction.
  • Oral argument hypotheticals: A footnote uses them to separate the categories. A California plaintiff injured in California by a Daimler vehicle presents a specific-jurisdiction question. Polish plaintiffs injured in Poland present a general-jurisdiction question.
  • Precedents relied on:
  • International Shoe: minimum contacts and the specific/general distinction.
  • Goodyear: the at-home standard.
  • Perkins: treated as an exceptional case in which Ohio served as a surrogate for the head office.
  • Helicopteros.
  • Shaffer.
  • Burger King: predictability.
  • Hertz: simple jurisdictional rules.
  • Keeton: describing Perkins.
  • Kiobel and Mohamad: weakening the ATS and TVPA claims.
  • Precedents distinguished or limited:
  • Asahi reasonableness factors: confined to the specific-jurisdiction inquiry.
  • Barrow S.S. Co. v. Kane and Tauza v. Susquehanna Coal Co.: they upheld "doing business" general jurisdiction, but as Pennoyer-era decisions cited without elaboration in Perkins, they "should not attract heavy reliance today."
  • Precedent rejected: The Ninth Circuit's agency test from Doe v. Unocal ("sufficiently important" services), at least as applied to general jurisdiction here.
  • Overruled: The majority expressly overrules no decision of the Court. The concurrence contends the majority's footnote implicitly rejects the circuit decisions applying reasonableness to general jurisdiction.
  • Specific jurisdiction as the answer to the concurrence: A footnote states that, given how specific jurisdiction has flourished, it would be hard to conjure up an example of the "deep injustice" the concurrence predicts. The reciprocal-fairness passage of International Shoe addressed specific jurisdiction only.
