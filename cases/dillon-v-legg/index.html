Black-Letter Rule

A negligent defendant owes a duty of due care to a bystander who suffers shock resulting in physical injury from witnessing injury to another, if the injury to the bystander was reasonably foreseeable. Foreseeability is decided case by case and guided by three factors: (1) the plaintiff's nearness to the scene, (2) whether the shock came from sensory and contemporaneous observation of the accident, and (3) how closely the plaintiff and the victim are related. The bystander's recovery depends on the defendant's adjudicated liability to the primary victim.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff Margery Dillon is the mother of Erin Lee Dillon, an infant. Cheryl Dillon, another infant daughter, is joined as a plaintiff. Defendant Legg is the driver.
  • Accident: Defendant negligently drove his car into Erin while she lawfully crossed a road. Erin died of her injuries.
  • Mother's claim: The mother alleged she was in close proximity to the collision and personally witnessed it. She alleged great emotional disturbance, shock, and injury to her nervous system, causing physical and mental pain.
  • Sister's claim: Cheryl alleged the same kind of injury from witnessing the collision.
  • Zone of danger: The mother admittedly was not within the zone of danger. Cheryl may have been, because the evidence conflicted on whether she was on the curb.
  • Defense: Defendant pleaded contributory negligence of the mother, the sister, and the child.

Procedural Posture

The trial court granted judgment on the pleadings against the mother's count and dismissed it, apparently because she was outside the zone of danger, but denied the motion on Cheryl's count because Cheryl might have been within that zone or feared for her own safety. The mother appealed to the California Supreme Court.

Issue

Does a mother who was outside the zone of danger state a cause of action in negligence for shock and resulting physical injury suffered from witnessing the defendant negligently kill her child?

Holding

Yes. A defendant owes a duty to a bystander whose shock-induced physical injury was reasonably foreseeable under the nearness, contemporaneous-observation, and close-relationship factors, and the mother's allegations satisfy all three, so the judgment is reversed. (Tobriner, J., joined by Peters, Most, and Sullivan, JJ.; Traynor, C.J., and Burke and McComb, JJ., dissenting. 4-3.)

Reasoning

  • The zone-of-danger rule is artificial. The court could not justify relief for the sister and denial for the mother based on the happenstance that the sister stood a few yards closer.
  • Impact rule already rejected. California held impact unnecessary in Cook v. Maier. The only reason for the zone-of-danger requirement is that someone in the zone fears impact, so once impact is abandoned, the zone concept collapses.
  • Duty is a policy conclusion. Quoting Prosser, duty is "a shorthand statement of a conclusion," the sum of policy considerations that entitle a plaintiff to protection. The court traced duty to a late 19th-century device used to curb jury awards.
  • Fear of fraud fails as a basis for denying duty.
  • The factual premise is doubtful. A mother who sees her child killed will suffer physical injury from shock. Sloane v. Southern California Ry. Co. recognized that fright can cause physical injury.
  • The possibility of fraud in some cases does not justify rejecting a whole class of claims. Courts must sort meritorious from fraudulent claims case by case (Emery v. Emery; Klein v. Klein).
  • The fraud risk is no greater than in areas where California already allows recovery: fear for one's own safety (Lindley v. Knowlton; Webb; Vanoni), parasitic mental-suffering damages, and intentional infliction of emotional distress (State Rubbish Collectors Assn. v. Siliznoff).
  • Fear of indefinite liability fails too.
  • Liability is limited by reasonable foreseeability, which is the chief element of duty absent overriding policy (Grafton v. Mollica; Palsgraf: "The risk reasonably to be perceived defines the duty to be obeyed").
  • Harper & James: a plaintiff outside the zone of physical risk can still be within the zone of risk if the defendant should foresee fright or shock severe enough to cause substantial injury in a person normally constituted.
  • Duty must be decided case by case, but guidelines can steer the analysis. The court laid out the three factors and said they "shade into each other."
  • Foreseeability is objective: what the ordinary person should reasonably have foreseen, excluding the remote and unexpected.
  • Application. All three factors are present. A negligent driver who kills a young child may reasonably expect the mother to be nearby and to suffer trauma on witnessing it.
  • Courts can draw lines. The court cited lines drawn in the open-car cases (Richardson v. Ham; Hergenrether v. East; Richards v. Stanley), third-party negligent drafting (Lucas v. Hamm; Biakanja v. Irving), intentional infliction (Siliznoff), the abolition of privity despite Winterbottom v. Wright, and products liability.
  • English law. English courts allow recovery and still limit it: Hambrook v. Stokes Bros., Chadwick v. British Railways Board, Boardman v. Sanderson, and Bourhill v. Young (no recovery for a casual bystander lacking "the customary phlegm").
  • History of shifting limits. The cases moved from no recovery, to impact, to fear for oneself, to zone of danger. Each artificial exception gave way, and general tort principles should govern instead.
  • Footnoted points.
  • Allowing recovery for fear for oneself but not for others invites false claims of self-fear and penalizes honest plaintiffs.
  • The zone of danger must include those exposed to emotional injury, since no distinction can be drawn between physical injury and emotional injury flowing from it.
  • A "flood of litigation" shows a need for redress and is no reason to refuse cases.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Traynor, C.J. (dissenting): Dissents for the reasons in Amaya. Amaya was correctly decided and should not be overruled.
  • Burke, J. (dissenting, joined by McComb, J.):
  • Amaya, decided in 1963, considered and rejected every argument the majority makes, and it involved a mother who saw her 17-month-old struck. No American jurisdiction has allowed recovery since, and the majority relies only on English cases applying Hambrook, which Amaya rejected.
  • The guidelines restate Prosser's suggestions, which Amaya rejected, and they collapse into arbitrariness. Burke asks how close the relationship must be, how near the plaintiff must be, how soon the shock must occur, and what happens if the plaintiff was mistaken or the victim assumed the risk.
  • The zone-of-danger rule is coherent. It protects objective fear of impact to oneself (Waube v. Warrington).
  • The majority misreads Waube, which rested on proportionality to culpability and the lack of a stopping point as well as fraud. Burke warns of "the fantastic realm of infinite liability."
  • The majority misreads Lindley and Reed v. Moore. Neither held that a plaintiff in the zone may recover for fear for another.
  • The majority's statement that the child's contributory negligence bars the mother's and sister's recovery conflicts with the rule against imputing negligence between family members.
  • The issue belongs to the Legislature, which left Amaya undisturbed for five years.
  • Burke would affirm.

Notes

  • Narrow scope: The ruling is confined to shock that resulted in physical injury.
  • Expressly left open: Whether duty exists when some factors are absent or carry reduced weight. The court said future cases will draw lines on "facts more subtle." It also did not decide whether disinterested spectators' claims raise a greater fraud problem.
  • Derivative liability: If defendant is found not liable for the child's death because of the contributory negligence of the mother, sister, or child, neither the mother nor the sister recovers. The bystander claim rests on the defendant's adjudicated liability to the victim.
  • Overruled: Amaya v. Home Ice, Fuel & Supply Co., 59 Cal.2d 295, to the extent inconsistent.
  • Distinguished: Amaya's posture. Here the out-of-zone mother's claim sits beside the possibly in-zone sister's claim, which exposes the zone rule's arbitrariness.
  • Rejected or criticized: The zone-of-danger rule (Reed v. Moore), Waube v. Warrington's fraud rationale, and the Winterbottom "no limit" argument.
  • Relied on:
  • Cook v. Maier (impact unnecessary)
  • Sloane (fright causes physical injury)
  • Emery and Klein (fraud risk does not bar a class of claims)
  • Lindley, Webb, and Vanoni (fear for oneself is compensable)
  • Acadia and Easton (parasitic damages)
  • Siliznoff (intentional infliction)
  • Palsgraf and Grafton (foreseeability defines duty)
  • The open-car and privity cases listed above
  • Hambrook, Chadwick, Boardman, and Bourhill
  • *Reading of Lindley:* The majority reads it as sustaining recovery for combined fear for the children and for oneself. Burke disputes this.
