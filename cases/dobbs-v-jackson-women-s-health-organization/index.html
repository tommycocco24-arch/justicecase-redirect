Black-Letter Rule

The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects an unenumerated right only if the right is "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" (Washington v. Glucksberg). The Constitution confers no right to abortion, so Roe v. Wade and Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey are overruled. State abortion regulations get rational-basis review: they carry a strong presumption of validity and must be sustained if the legislature could rationally have thought they serve legitimate state interests.

Facts

  • Statute: Mississippi's Gestational Age Act bars abortion when the probable gestational age is greater than 15 weeks, except in a medical emergency or a case of severe fetal abnormality.
  • Legislative findings: The legislature found that at 12 weeks the "unborn human being" has taken on "the human form" in all relevant respects. It also found that most abortions after 15 weeks use dilation and evacuation, which it called "a barbaric practice, dangerous for the maternal patient, and demeaning to the medical profession." It noted that only six other countries permitted elective abortion after 20 weeks.
  • Parties: Respondents are Jackson Women's Health Organization, an abortion clinic, and one of its doctors. Petitioners are Mississippi officials, including State Health Officer Thomas Dobbs.
  • Viability: 15 weeks is several weeks before the point at which a fetus is now regarded as viable. Respondents placed viability at 23 or 24 weeks.
  • Arguments: Mississippi argued that Roe and Casey were wrongly decided and that the Act survives rational-basis review. Respondents argued that "no half-measures" were available: the Court had to reaffirm or overrule Roe and Casey.

Procedural Posture

Respondents sued in federal district court the day the Act was enacted. The district court granted them summary judgment and permanently enjoined the Act because it banned abortions before viability, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari on whether "all pre-viability prohibitions on elective abortions are unconstitutional."

Issue

Does the Constitution confer a right to obtain an abortion that prohibits Mississippi from banning elective abortions after 15 weeks of pregnancy, before viability?

Holding

No. The Constitution confers no right to abortion because that right is neither deeply rooted in the Nation's history and tradition nor part of a broader entrenched right recognized in precedent. Roe and Casey are overruled, the Act survives rational-basis review, and the Fifth Circuit's judgment is reversed and the case remanded. Justice Alito wrote for the Court, joined by Justices Thomas, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett, which made the vote 5-4 on overruling. Chief Justice Roberts concurred only in the judgment, which made the judgment 6-3.

Reasoning

  • Text: The Constitution makes no express reference to abortion, so those claiming the right must show it is implicit in the text. The Court said Roe was "remarkably loose" here. Roe placed the right inside an unmentioned privacy right drawn from five Amendments without settling where it lived. Casey rested the right solely on Fourteenth Amendment "liberty."
  • Equal protection foreclosed: Regulating a procedure only one sex can undergo is not a sex-based classification and does not trigger heightened scrutiny unless it is a "mere pretext" for invidious discrimination (Geduldig v. Aiello). The "goal of preventing abortion" is not invidiously discriminatory animus against women (Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic). Abortion laws get the same review as other health and safety measures.
  • Two categories of substantive due process: The Clause protects (1) the Bill of Rights guarantees incorporated against the States and (2) a "select list" of fundamental rights not mentioned in the Constitution. Abortion falls in the second category.
  • The test applied to both categories: The Court asks whether the right is "deeply rooted in [our] history and tradition" and essential to the "scheme of ordered liberty." It cited Timbs, McDonald, and Glucksberg. A careful historical inquiry is required because "liberty" alone gives little guidance. The Court must guard against confusing the Amendment with its own views. It invoked Lochner as the warning case.
  • History, common law: Bracton, Coke, Hale, and Blackstone all treated post-quickening abortion as a crime. Pre-quickening abortion was still "unlawful," and an abortionist was guilty of murder if the woman died. No common-law authority recognized a positive right to abortion.
  • History, American law: A 19th-century wave of statutes criminalized abortion at all stages. By 1868, 28 of 37 States did so, and the rest but one followed by 1910. Thirty States still banned abortion at all stages when Roe was decided. The Court called this "an unbroken tradition" running from the common law to 1973.
  • Rebuttals rejected:
  • Roe's claim that abortion was doubtfully a common-law crime rested on discredited scholarship.
  • The quickening rule did not show that anyone thought States lacked power to ban earlier abortions.
  • The claim that 19th-century statutes had illegitimate motives fails. The Court disfavors motive arguments (United States v. O'Brien), and the cited statements came from a few supporters, not legislators.
  • Broader-right argument: Framing the right as privacy or autonomy (Casey's "concept of existence") "prove[s] too much." At that level of generality it could license rights to illicit drug use and prostitution.
  • Precedents distinguished: Roe's and Casey's supporting cases involved marriage, contraception, intimacy, child-rearing, and bodily integrity. None of them involved destroying "potential life." Abortion is "inherently different" (Roe) and "a unique act" (Casey). Those cases therefore do not support an abortion right, and this holding does not undermine them.
  • Stare decisis framework: Stare decisis is "not an inexorable command" and is "at its weakest" in constitutional cases. The Court applied the Janus and Ramos factors and found that all five weighed strongly in favor of overruling:
  • Nature of the error: Roe was "egregiously wrong" from the start, like Plessy. It short-circuited the democratic process on a question the Constitution leaves to the people.
  • Quality of the reasoning: Roe had no grounding in text, history, or precedent. Its trimester scheme read like legislation. It conflated two senses of privacy, informational and decisional (Whalen v. Roe). It never justified the viability line, which moves with medical technology and location. Casey kept the viability line without defending it and added an "undue burden" test with no firm grounding.
  • Workability: The undue-burden test is "inherently standardless."
  • Its three subsidiary rules ("substantial obstacle," informed-choice measures, and "unnecessary health regulations") overlap and conflict.
  • The "large fraction" test is unclear.
  • The Justices split over the cost-benefit reading (Whole Woman's Health vs. June Medical).
  • The circuits split repeatedly.
  • Effect on other areas of law: The abortion cases distorted the rules on facial challenges, third-party standing, res judicata, severability, constitutional avoidance, and First Amendment doctrine.
  • Reliance: The Court agreed with Casey that concrete reliance interests, the kind found in property and contract, are absent. Casey's "intangible" reliance on the right's social effects is an empirical dispute courts are ill-equipped to judge.
  • **Casey's legitimacy concern rejected:* The fear that overruling a "watershed" case will look political is an extraneous influence the Court cannot consider. No precedent is permanently exempt from ordinary stare decisis analysis. Otherwise Plessy and Lochner* would still be law.
  • Roberts's middle path rejected: No party advocated it. Stare decisis is "a doctrine of preservation, not transformation," and viability was central to Roe, Casey, Danforth, Colautti, and Whole Woman's Health. The approach would also only postpone the core question.
  • Standard of review: With no fundamental right, rational basis applies (Ferguson v. Skrupa, Heller v. Doe, Williamson v. Lee Optical). Legitimate interests include:
  • respect for and preservation of prenatal life at all stages;
  • maternal health and safety;
  • eliminating gruesome or barbaric procedures;
  • the integrity of the medical profession;
  • mitigating fetal pain;
  • preventing discrimination based on race, sex, or disability.
  • Application: The legislature's findings on protecting unborn life and on dilation and evacuation supply a rational basis, so the challenge fails.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Thomas, J., concurring: He joins the majority in full. He writes that "due process of law" guarantees only process, making "substantive due process" an oxymoron. In future cases the Court "should reconsider all" substantive due process precedents, including Griswold, Lawrence, and Obergefell. It could then ask whether any of those rights rest on another provision, such as the Privileges or Immunities Clause. He adds that abortion would fail under any plausible approach.
  • Kavanaugh, J., concurring: The Constitution is "neutral" on abortion and leaves it to the people, the States, and Congress. It neither outlaws nor legalizes abortion. He frames the stare decisis test as three parts: (i) the precedent is egregiously wrong, (ii) it has caused significant negative jurisprudential or real-world consequences, and (iii) overruling would not unduly upset legitimate reliance. He argues that Casey's predictive judgment that the controversy would end has failed. Overruling Roe does not threaten Griswold, Eisenstadt, Loving, or Obergefell. In his view, a State may not bar residents from traveling to another State for an abortion, and it may not retroactively punish pre-decision abortions.
  • Roberts, C.J., concurring in the judgment: He would discard the viability line under ordinary stare decisis, because it was unbriefed, unreasoned, and eroded by Gonzales v. Carhart's broader state interests. He would go no further. In his view any abortion right extends only to "a reasonable opportunity to choose," and 15 weeks suffices "absent rare circumstances." He grounds this in judicial restraint: "If it is not necessary to decide more to dispose of a case, then it is necessary not to decide more." He cites Webster as a model for partial overruling. He notes that reliance concerns would not arise under his approach. He doubts that a ban from conception must be treated the same as a 15-week ban.
  • Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan, JJ., dissenting:
  • Roe and Casey struck a balance between women's liberty and equality and the State's interest in potential life. The majority erases the woman's side.
  • Reading the Fourteenth Amendment through 1868 practice consigns women to second-class citizenship, because the ratifiers were men who did not see women as equals.
  • The Court has repeatedly rejected the frozen-in-time approach. The dissent points to Loving, Obergefell, and Justice Harlan's dissent in Poe v. Ullman.
  • The abortion right is part of a single fabric of precedent covering bodily integrity (Winston, Rochin, Harper, Cruzan), contraception, intimacy, and marriage. Either the majority's history-only reasoning threatens those rights or the majority does not believe its own reasoning.
  • Kavanaugh's "neutrality" is a choice to take sides against women.
  • On stare decisis, women have relied on the right, the framework proved workable, and nothing in law or fact has changed. Casey was "a precedent about precedent." The Court reverses course only "because the composition of this Court has changed."
  • The dissent warns that States may enact bans with no rape, incest, or health exceptions, may impose interstate restrictions, and that a federal ban could follow.

Notes

  • Overruled: Roe v. Wade and Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey. This includes the viability rule and the undue-burden test.
  • Expressly limited: The decision "concerns the constitutional right to abortion and no other right." "Nothing in this opinion should be understood to cast doubt on precedents that do not concern abortion." Each precedent gets its own stare decisis analysis, and reliance and workability differ for those cases.
  • No view on personhood: The majority says its decision rests on no view about when prenatal life has rights or legally cognizable interests.
  • Privileges or Immunities Clause: In footnote 22, the Court says the result would be the same under that clause, since any right there would also need roots in history and tradition.
  • Accepted for argument's sake: The Court did not dispute Casey's claim that 1868 practices do not mark the outer limits of Fourteenth Amendment liberty. It reasoned that abortion "is nothing new."
  • Relied on:
  • Glucksberg, Timbs, McDonald, Palko, and Duncan for the history-and-tradition test.
  • Geduldig and Bray on equal protection.
  • Janus, Ramos (Kavanaugh, J.), Payne, Kimble, Agostini, and Pearson on stare decisis.
  • Brown/Plessy, West Coast Hotel/Adkins/Lochner, and Barnette/Gobitis as overruling models.
  • Ferguson v. Skrupa, Heller v. Doe, Williamson v. Lee Optical, and Gonzales v. Carhart on rational basis and legitimate interests.
  • Distinguished: Griswold, Eisenstadt, Carey, Loving, Turner v. Safley, Moore v. East Cleveland, Pierce, Meyer, Skinner, Winston v. Lee, Washington v. Harper, Rochin, Lawrence, and Obergefell. The ground is that none involved the destruction of "potential life."
  • **Casey's own overrulings:* The opinion notes that Casey itself overruled Akron v. Akron Center for Reproductive Health and Thornburgh in toto and overruled Roe* in part by discarding the trimester scheme.
  • Exceptions: The Act's medical-emergency and fetal-abnormality exceptions were not separately analyzed. The dissent says the majority does not address whether a State may bar a medically needed abortion.
