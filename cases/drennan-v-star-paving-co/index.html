Black-Letter Rule

Under Restatement of Contracts § 90, a subcontractor's bid that is silent on revocation becomes irrevocable when the subcontractor should reasonably expect the general contractor to rely on it and the general contractor reasonably does so by submitting its own binding bid. The law implies a subsidiary promise not to revoke, and reasonable reliance substitutes for consideration. The general contractor then gets an opportunity to accept after winning the prime contract, provided the general contractor accepts promptly, does not reopen bargaining, and had no reason to know of a mistake in the bid.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff Drennan, a licensed general contractor, sued defendant Star Paving, a paving subcontractor.
  • Bidding custom: General contractors in the area customarily received subcontractor bids by phone on bid day and relied on them to compute their own bids.
  • The bid: On the bid deadline day, defendant's estimator phoned in a paving bid of $7,131.60 according to plans and specifications and repeated it on request. It was the lowest paving bid.
  • Plaintiff's reliance: Plaintiff used the figure in his $317,385 prime bid and named defendant as paving subcontractor. He had to name subcontractors doing 0.5% or more of the work and post a 10% bidder's bond guaranteeing he would enter the contract if awarded.
  • Award and revocation: Plaintiff won the prime contract. The next morning defendant's engineer said the bid was a mistake and refused to do the work for less than $15,000.
  • Cover: After several months seeking the lowest bid, plaintiff hired L & H Paving for $10,948.60.
  • No reason to suspect error: Paving bids in the Lancaster desert area usually varied by 160% between highest and lowest.

Procedural Posture

The trial court found that defendant made a definite offer and that plaintiff relied on it, and entered judgment for plaintiff for $3,817 (the difference between defendant's bid and plaintiff's cost) plus costs. Defendant appealed.

Issue

Did the general contractor's reasonable reliance on the subcontractor's bid make that bid irrevocable, even though the subcontractor tried to revoke before the general contractor accepted?

Holding

Yes; under Restatement § 90, the subcontractor's bid carried an implied subsidiary promise not to revoke, made binding by the general contractor's reasonable and foreseeable reliance, and the subcontractor's unilateral mistake did not excuse it, so the judgment is affirmed (Traynor, J., joined by Gibson, C.J., and Shenk, Schauer, Spence, and McComb, JJ.).

Reasoning

  • No traditional contract: No evidence showed defendant offered to keep its bid open in exchange for plaintiff's use of it (no option supported by consideration). Plaintiff's use of the bid was not an acceptance binding him to award the subcontract (no bilateral contract).
  • § 90 applies in California: The court quoted § 90 and cited California cases adopting it. Defendant's bid was a promise. Defendant had reason to expect plaintiff would use it if lowest, and it induced action "of a definite and substantial character."
  • Revocation terms: Had the bid expressly stated or clearly implied it was revocable before acceptance, the court would have treated it that way. Because it was silent, the court asked what conditions on revocation are imposed by law or reasonably inferable in fact.
  • Analogy to § 45: For unilateral contracts, an offer is no longer revocable at will before complete performance. Comment b to § 45 implies a subsidiary promise not to revoke once part performance begins and cross-references § 90 for reliance. The court extended the logic: reasonable reliance causing a foreseeable prejudicial change in position supports implying a subsidiary promise not to revoke an offer for a bilateral contract.
  • Consideration not required: § 90's purpose is to bind promises lacking bargained-for consideration. Reasonable reliance holds the offeror in place of consideration. The court followed Northwestern Engineering Co. v. Ellerman, which applied § 90 on similar facts and reasoned that doing so no more abolishes consideration than an ordinary estoppel does.
  • Subcontractor's stake in reliance: Defendant bid to win the subcontract. It knew its bid might be lowest and used. A lower sub-bid made plaintiff's prime bid more competitive, improving defendant's own chances. Defendant had reason to expect and to want reliance. Because plaintiff was bound by his own bid, fairness required that he have at least a chance to accept after award.
  • Limits on the general contractor: The general contractor cannot delay acceptance after award hoping for a better price, and cannot reopen bargaining while claiming a continuing right to accept the original offer (R. J. Daum Const. Co. v. Child). Plaintiff promptly told defendant it was getting the subcontract.
  • Mistake: A general contractor who had reason to believe a bid was erroneous cannot justifiably rely on it, and § 90 would not apply. Plaintiff had no such reason given the usual 160% spread. Defendant's mistake misled plaintiff about paving costs and so counts as an additional reason to enforce.
  • Duty of care and loss allocation: Defendant bid knowing it might be used, could foresee harm from an underestimate, and acted in its own business interest. The court did not decide whether these facts would support a negligence claim (citing Biakanja v. Irving), but found them persuasive that the mistake should not defeat § 90 recovery. Between the sub who made the error and the general who reasonably relied, the loss falls on the party who caused it.
  • Mitigation: The complaint adequately alleged the cover contract. Uncontradicted evidence showed plaintiff spent months seeking bids and took the lowest. Any pleading uncertainty was waived by failure to raise it by special demurrer (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 430, subd. 9, 434).

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope of the rule: The implied promise not to revoke arises only where the bid is silent on revocation. An express or clearly implied reservation of the right to revoke would control.
  • Reliance must be justifiable: No § 90 recovery if the general contractor had reason to know of the bid error.
  • General contractor's obligations: The rule protects only prompt acceptance after award. Delay or bid shopping and renegotiation forfeits the right to accept the original offer.
  • One-sided protection: The court found no evidence that plaintiff's use of the bid bound him to award the subcontract to defendant.
  • Left open: Whether the subcontractor's conduct would support a negligence recovery if pleaded on that theory.
  • Relied on: Restatement §§ 90 and 45 (with comment); Edmonds v. County of Los Angeles and other California § 90 cases; Northwestern Engineering Co. v. Ellerman (S.D.); Robert Gordon, Inc. v. Ingersoll-Rand Co.; R. J. Daum Const. Co. v. Child (limits on delay and renegotiation); Williston and Corbin. James Baird Co. v. Gimbel Bros. is cited with a "cf." signal and not discussed.
  • Distinguished: M. F. Kemper Const. Co. v. City of Los Angeles and Brunzell Const. Co. v. G. J. Weisbrod, Inc. (with Lemoge Electric v. San Mateo County) were rescission cases where the offeree knew or should have known of the mistake and could be restored to status quo. In Leo F. Piazza Paving Co. v. Bebek & Brkich, defendants intended only to give "some kind of an idea to use," so there was no offer or promise on which reliance was reasonably expected. Bard v. Kent held an unsupported option revoked by the optionor's death, but § 90 was not pleaded and the reliance did not appear definite and substantial.
  • Overruled: None.
  • Damages measure applied: Cost of cover minus the bid price ($10,948.60 − $7,131.60 = $3,817).
