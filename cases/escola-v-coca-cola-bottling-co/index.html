Black-Letter Rule

Res ipsa loquitur applies only if (1) defendant had exclusive control of the thing causing the injury and (2) the accident is of a kind that ordinarily would not occur without defendant's negligence. Where the accident happens after defendant gave up control, the control element is met if defendant controlled the instrumentality at the time of the alleged negligent act and plaintiff offers evidence permitting a reasonable inference that the instrumentality was not changed after leaving defendant's possession and was carefully handled by plaintiff and any third person. That showing satisfies only the control element. Plaintiff must still satisfy the second element, and the doctrine then supplies only an inference of negligence. Once the inference arises, whether defendant's rebuttal evidence dispels it is ordinarily a jury question.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff, a restaurant waitress, sued defendant bottling company, which bottled and delivered the bottle to her employer.
  • Delivery and storage: Defendant's driver stacked several cases on the floor under and behind the counter, where they stayed at least thirty-six hours.
  • The accident: Plaintiff set the top case on a nearby cabinet and moved bottles one at a time into the refrigerator. The fourth bottle "exploded in my hand," breaking into two jagged pieces and cutting her hand deeply.
  • Careful handling: A coworker testified plaintiff held the bottle waist high and that it did not bang the case, the door, or another bottle. Witnesses described a loud pop.
  • Missing evidence: A restaurant employee threw the pieces away. Plaintiff described them, and a diagram showed the fracture line.
  • Prior explosions: Defendant's driver had seen other Coca Cola bottles explode and had found broken bottles in the warehouse, cause unknown.
  • No specific negligence: Plaintiff could show no specific negligent act and relied entirely on res ipsa loquitur.
  • Testing evidence: A chemical engineer for the bottle manufacturer testified that standard pressure tests (sample bottles held at 450 psi for one minute, against normal Coca Cola pressure under 50 psi) and thermal shock tests are "pretty near" infallible. Defendant reused bottles without retesting them.
  • Defendant's rebuttal: Defendant offered evidence that it regulated and checked pressure and visually inspected the glass at several stages.

Procedural Posture

The jury returned a verdict for plaintiff and judgment was entered on it. Defendant appealed, arguing res ipsa loquitur did not apply and the evidence was insufficient to support the judgment.

Issue

Could plaintiff rely on res ipsa loquitur to raise an inference that the bottler's negligence caused the bottle to explode, when the accident occurred after the bottle left the bottler's control?

Holding

Yes. The evidence supported a reasonable inference that the bottle was not damaged after delivery, so it was defective when defendant relinquished control. Although it was unclear whether an excessive charge or a defect in the glass caused the explosion, there was a sufficient showing that neither cause would ordinarily have been present if due care had been used, and defendant had exclusive control over both charging and inspection. Plaintiff could therefore rely on res ipsa to supply an inference of negligence, and the jury could weigh defendant's rebuttal evidence; judgment affirmed (Gibson, C.J., joined by Shenk, Curtis, Carter, and Schauer, JJ.; Traynor, J., concurring in the judgment).

Reasoning

  • Framework: The court did not resolve the split among jurisdictions on exploding bottles. It asked only whether the facts here met the two conditions for the doctrine.
  • Control after delivery: The court rejected the view that the thing cannot speak for itself once defendant has given up control. Under the "more logical view," control at the time of the negligent act suffices if plaintiff first proves the instrumentality's condition did not change afterward.
  • Plaintiff's burden on post-delivery handling:
  • Plaintiff must show due care during the period after defendant lost control. Defendant need not show that something happened to the bottle (quoting Dunn v. Hoffman Beverage Co.).
  • Plaintiff must also show she handled the bottle carefully. This eliminates the possibility that plaintiff caused the accident (quoting Prosser's locomotive engineer example).
  • Plaintiff need not rule out every remote possibility. Evidence permitting a reasonable inference that the bottle was not exposed to harmful outside forces and was carefully handled by everyone who touched it is enough. That question then goes to the trier of fact.
  • Application to the record: No instruction was given on this specific point, but defendant did not claim error. The evidence supported an inference of no post-delivery damage. Sound, properly prepared bottles do not ordinarily explode when carefully handled, so the bottle was defective when defendant relinquished control.
  • Negligence element, cause by cause: The explosion came from excessive pressure, a defect in the glass, or both. Res ipsa applies if the evidence shows a probability that defendant was negligent in any of these respects.
  • Overcharge: Charging was in defendant's exclusive control. It is common knowledge that overcharging does not ordinarily occur without negligence.
  • Visible defect: Discoverable defects ordinarily do not escape reasonable inspection, so overlooking one supports an inference of improper inspection.
  • Invisible defect: The court treated this as the hard case. Unlike Honea, the record contained evidence of standard, nearly infallible industry tests. New bottles therefore are unlikely to carry hidden defects. If hidden defects develop in used bottles, the bottler has a duty to test before refilling or, if testing is not commercially practicable, not to reuse them. Either way, the defect was discoverable by reasonable and practicable tests.
  • Conclusion on elements: It was unclear which cause produced the explosion. Neither would ordinarily be present with due care, and defendant exclusively controlled both charging and inspection.
  • Rebuttal evidence: Defendant's evidence of careful pressure checks and visual inspections did not defeat the doctrine. Whether rebuttal evidence dispels the inference is ordinarily a jury question (Druzanich v. Criley; Michener v. Hutton).

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Traynor, J., concurring in the judgment: Negligence should no longer be the basis for recovery in cases like this. A manufacturer should bear absolute liability when an article it places on the market, knowing it will be used without inspection, proves to have a defect that injures people.
  • Policy: Responsibility should be fixed where it most effectively reduces hazards. The manufacturer can anticipate and guard against hazards the public cannot. Injured persons are unprepared to bear the loss. The manufacturer can insure against the risk and spread it as a cost of doing business. Placing liability on the manufacturer discourages marketing defective products.
  • Res ipsa as disguised strict liability: An injured plaintiff can rarely refute the manufacturer's evidence of care. By leaving it to the jury whether the inference is dispelled regardless of the evidence against it, the negligence rule "approaches the rule of strict liability." It is "needlessly circuitous" to impose liability without negligence under a negligence label. That responsibility should be fixed openly.
  • Analogies: (1) California's food adulteration statute imposes criminal liability without fault, including for containers. (2) Retailers already bear absolute liability to buyers through implied warranties of safety and then recoup from manufacturers, which is circuitous and wasteful. (3) MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co. already eliminated the privity requirement for negligence and treated the injured person as the real party in interest. (4) Courts already extend the manufacturer's warranty to consumers of food without privity.
  • Warranty as tort: The fictions courts use to reach consumers are unnecessary if the warranty is severed from the sales contract and grounded in tort as strict liability. Historically, warranty actions sounded in tort.
  • Changed market: Mass production, inaccessible manufacturing processes, and reliance on advertising and trademarks mean consumers accept products on faith. The manufacturer's obligation must keep pace.
  • Limit: Liability should be defined by the product's safety in normal and proper use. It should not reach injuries that cannot be traced to the product as it reached the market.

Notes

  • Narrow ground: The majority decided the case on negligence via res ipsa loquitur. It did not adopt strict liability. Only Traynor's concurrence proposed it.
  • Left open: The majority expressly declined to analyze the reasoning of the many out-of-state cases on both sides of the exploding-bottle split.
  • Instruction issue not reached: No instruction was given on plaintiff's burden to show post-delivery care. Defendant did not raise this as error, so the court did not decide its effect.
  • Relied on:
  • Honea v. City Dairy, Inc.: source of the two-element test and collected cases on control at the time of the negligent act.
  • Hinds v. Wheadon (cf.) and Prosser on Torts: res ipsa conditions.
  • Dunn v. Hoffman Beverage Co.: plaintiff, not defendant, must show due care after defendant lost control.
  • Olson v. Whitthorne & Swan: plaintiff must account for own conduct.
  • MacPherson v. Canada Dry Ginger Ale, Inc.: post-delivery handling becomes a fact question.
  • Druzanich v. Criley and Michener v. Hutton: whether rebuttal dispels the inference is ordinarily for the jury.
  • Shearman and Redfield on Negligence: the doctrine requires evidence showing at least a probability of legal wrong by defendant.
  • Distinguished: Honea, where the court refused to take judicial notice of the bottling industry's methods for finding hidden defects. Here the record contained expert evidence of those methods.
  • Cited without discussion: Cases applying the doctrine to exploding bottles (e.g., Payne, Stolle, Bradley, Ortego, Macres) and cases refusing it (e.g., Gerber v. Faber, Loebig's Guardian, Stewart). The majority does not overrule or disapprove any of them.
  • Overruled: None.
  • Rehearing: Defendant's petition for rehearing was denied. Edmonds, J., voted for rehearing.
