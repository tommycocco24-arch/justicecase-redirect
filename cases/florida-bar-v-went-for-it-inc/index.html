Black-Letter Rule

A state bar may bar lawyers from sending targeted direct-mail solicitations to accident or disaster victims and their relatives for 30 days after the event. Lawyer advertising is commercial speech, so the restriction gets intermediate scrutiny under Central Hudson. The rule survives because (1) the bar asserted a substantial interest in protecting victims' privacy and tranquility and the profession's reputation, (2) unrebutted statistical and anecdotal evidence showed the rule directly and materially advances that interest, and (3) the ban is reasonably tailored, being narrow in scope and duration and leaving ample alternative channels open.

Facts

  • The Rules: Florida Bar Rule 4-7.4(b)(1) barred a lawyer from sending a written solicitation about personal injury, wrongful death, or an accident or disaster involving the recipient or a relative, unless the event occurred more than 30 days before the mailing. Rule 4-7.8(a) barred lawyers from taking referrals from referral services that contact prospective clients in ways the lawyer could not. Together they created a 30-day blackout on targeting victims, directly or indirectly.
  • The Bar's study: Before adopting the Rules, the Bar ran a 2-year study with hearings, commissioned surveys, and public comment. It submitted a 106-page summary to the District Court.
  • Survey data: Lawyers mailed 700,000 direct solicitations in Florida each year, 40% aimed at accident victims or survivors. 54% of the general public surveyed called post-accident contact a privacy violation. Among recipients of lawyer direct mail, 45% thought it was designed to exploit gullible or unstable people, 26% called it an invasion of privacy, and 27% said their regard for the legal profession and judicial process dropped.
  • Anecdotes: The record held critical newspaper editorials ("Scavenger Lawyers") and recipient complaints, including a letter sent to a mother three days after her husband's funeral and one received on the day of a nephew's funeral.
  • Plaintiffs: Lawyer G. Stewart McHenry and the lawyer referral service McHenry wholly owned, Went For It, Inc., wanted to send targeted solicitations within 30 days of accidents. McHenry was later disbarred for unrelated reasons, and Florida lawyer John T. Blakely was substituted.
  • No rebuttal: Respondents never refuted the Bar's evidence beyond a conclusory claim that the Rule lacked "any factual basis."

Procedural Posture

Plaintiffs sued in the Middle District of Florida for declaratory and injunctive relief. The Magistrate Judge recommended summary judgment for the Bar. The District Court rejected that recommendation and granted summary judgment for plaintiffs under Bates and later cases. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed, while noting it was "disturbed" that Bates and its progeny required that result. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does a state bar rule barring lawyers from sending targeted direct-mail solicitations to accident or disaster victims and their relatives within 30 days of the event violate the First and Fourteenth Amendments?

Holding

No. In the circumstances presented, the 30-day ban survives Central Hudson intermediate scrutiny because it serves a substantial interest, is backed by unrebutted evidence of real harm, and is reasonably tailored. The Eleventh Circuit is reversed. Justice O'Connor wrote for the Court, and four Justices dissented.

Reasoning

  • Framework. Lawyer advertising is commercial speech with "a limited measure of protection, commensurate with its subordinate position in the scale of First Amendment values" (Fox, quoting Ohralik). The Court applied the Central Hudson test:
  • Threshold: The State may freely regulate commercial speech that concerns unlawful activity or is misleading. This advertising was neither.
  • Prong 1: The government must assert a substantial interest.
  • Prong 2: The restriction must directly and materially advance that interest.
  • Prong 3: The regulation must be "narrowly drawn."
  • Only the State's actual interests count. Under Edenfield, the Court will not substitute other suppositions for the precise interests the State asserts.
  • Prong 1: substantial interest.
  • The Bar asserted an interest in protecting the privacy and tranquility of victims and their families from intrusive lawyer contact.
  • That interest feeds the Bar's broader goal of protecting the profession's reputation and the administration of justice.
  • The Court relied on precedent that states have broad power to regulate the professions (Goldfarb, Ohralik, Cohen v. Hurley).
  • It relied on precedent that clients' privacy is a substantial interest (Edenfield).
  • It relied on precedent that the privacy and tranquility of the home is an interest of "the highest order" (Carey v. Brown, Frisby).
  • Prong 2: direct and material advancement.
  • The State must show the harms are real and that the restriction alleviates them to a material degree. "Mere speculation or conjecture" is not enough (Rubin, Edenfield).
  • Edenfield failed because the State had no studies or anecdotes.
  • The Bar here offered both statistical and anecdotal evidence, and respondents never rebutted it.
  • Responding to the dissent's methodology attack, the Court said the case law does not require empirical data to come with a "surfeit of background information." It cited cases allowing reliance on studies from other locales (Renton, Barnes (Souter, J., concurring in judgment)) and, under strict scrutiny, on history, consensus, and "simple common sense" (Burson).
  • *Distinguishing Shapero. The Eleventh Circuit treated Shapero* as controlling. The Court gave three reasons it does not control:
  • Shapero's discussion of privacy was "casual." That State never argued privacy and relied only on overreaching.
  • Shapero involved a broad ban on all targeted mail, at any time, to any recipient.
  • That State offered no evidence of actual harm.
  • The relevant intrusion. The intrusion here does not come from the lawyer learning of the accident, which may take only reading the newspaper. It comes from confronting victims with that information "while wounds are still open" in order to solicit business. An untargeted letter to the public at large is "different in kind."
  • *Distinguishing Bolger.*
  • Bolger rejected a paternalistic ban on offensive contraceptive mailers because recipients could simply throw them away.
  • Here the harm cannot be cured by a trip to the trash can. The Bar's concern is the demonstrable damage that offense does to the profession it regulates, not offense in the abstract.
  • The harm comes as much from receiving a targeted letter days after an accident as from what it says.
  • Prong 3: reasonable fit.
  • Under Fox, the least-restrictive-means test does not apply to commercial speech. The fit must be reasonable, not perfect, and "in proportion to the interest served."
  • The test is still more demanding than rational basis. Under Discovery Network, the existence of "numerous and obvious less-burdensome alternatives" is relevant to whether the fit is reasonable.
  • Overinclusiveness. Respondents objected that the ban does not distinguish by severity of injury. The Court found little deficiency here. Drawing lines by severity of pain or grief would be hard, and a uniform, brief 30-day ban is reasonable. The Court saw no obvious less-burdensome alternative.
  • Lost information. Respondents argued victims would lose information about their legal options. The Court rejected this because the ban is brief and ample alternative channels remain: TV, radio, newspapers, billboards, untargeted mail (including to "discrete segments" of the population), and the Yellow Pages.
  • No one identified a single case where the 30-day delay caused harm. Survey evidence suggested Floridians have little difficulty finding a lawyer.
  • Professional regulation context. Some attorney speech gets the strongest constitutional protection (Gentile, In re Primus). This case involves "pure commercial advertising." Because states have traditionally regulated lawyers extensively, it is especially appropriate to limit scrutiny to the level suited to commercial speech.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Kennedy, J., dissenting (joined by Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg, JJ.). The dissent agreed Central Hudson governs but argued the Rule fails all three prongs.
  • Prong 1, privacy: Shapero controls. It held that "the mode of communication makes all the difference" and that a letter can be put in a drawer or discarded.
  • Prong 1, offense: The majority's real interest is avoiding offense to listeners, which cannot justify suppressing speech (Carey v. Population Services, Zauderer, Bolger). Mail recipients are not a captive audience (Consolidated Edison). If those cases are being overruled, the Court should say so.
  • Prong 1, reputation: The reputational interest is circular. It assumes the very point to be proved, that solicitation within 30 days serves no legitimate purpose. It amounts to "censorship pure and simple."
  • Prong 2: The Bar's Summary of Record is a party-prepared document with no actual surveys, no methodology, and little sample information. It mostly concerns TV and phone-book advertising. At most two of its pages summarize a study of Floridians' attitudes toward direct mail. Nothing in the record ties the Rule to protecting grieving victims, and the Bar's counsel conceded at argument that what a typical victim receives is "not in the record."
  • Prong 3, overbreadth: The ban covers every injury regardless of severity. Line-drawing is workable, as criminal law shows by grading bodily harm. Prompt legal advice is often urgent because evidence must be preserved, and opposing parties and adjusters remain free to contact victims.
  • Prong 3, who bears the cost: The ban falls hardest on the least sophisticated victims, who cannot seek out lawyers on their own (citing Trainmen).
  • Prong 3, untried alternatives: The market is largely self-policing, and Florida's rescission rights for client contracts offer a less restrictive remedy.
  • Overall: The majority orders "a major retreat" from Bates "to shield its own profession from public criticism."

Notes

  • Narrow holding. The Court held only that the Rules are valid "in the circumstances presented here." It stressed the 30-day limit, the targeted nature of the mail, the unrebutted record, and the existence of alternative channels. It said respondents' information-loss argument "would have force" if the ban were not brief and alternatives were lacking.
  • Interest not decided. Below, the Bar also asserted an interest in preventing undue influence and overreaching (the Ohralik/Shapero interest). The Bar did not press it in the Supreme Court, so the Court did not consider it. A single substantial interest satisfies Prong 1 (Rubin).
  • Paternalism line. The majority rejected the claim that it was unsettling First Amendment precedent. It said the offense cases the dissent cited involve government acting paternalistically, while here the government acts in its own interests or on behalf of the entities it regulates.
  • Targeted vs. untargeted mail. Untargeted letters to the general population or to discrete segments remained permitted in Florida and were treated as "different in kind."
  • Precedents relied on: Central Hudson (framework); Fox (no least-restrictive-means requirement; reasonable fit); Edenfield and Rubin (evidentiary burden on Prong 2); Discovery Network (less-burdensome alternatives are relevant); Goldfarb, Ohralik, and Cohen v. Hurley (state power over the professions); Carey v. Brown and Frisby (residential privacy); Renton, Barnes, and Burson (acceptable forms of supporting evidence).
  • Precedents distinguished:
  • Shapero: no privacy argument was made, the ban was broad, and the State offered no evidence.
  • Edenfield: the State had no evidence or anecdotes.
  • Bolger: the ban was paternalistic, and the harm there could be avoided by discarding the mail.
  • Background lineage: Valentine v. Chrestensen gave no protection to commercial advertising. Virginia Bd. of Pharmacy changed course but reserved the question of professional advertising. Bates extended protection to lawyer price advertising. R.M.J., Zauderer, and Shapero confirmed that lawyer advertising is protected commercial speech.
  • Overruled: The majority overruled nothing and expressly denied unsettling precedent. The dissent argued the decision is in tension with Shapero, Zauderer, and Bolger.
  • Higher protection contexts: Attorney speech on public issues and on matters of legal representation can receive the strongest protection (Gentile, In re Primus). This case does not involve that category.
