Black-Letter Rule

Lawyer advertising is commercial speech. Commercial speech that is neither misleading nor about unlawful activity, like the advertising here, may be regulated if the government satisfies the three-prong Central Hudson test. A state bar may bar lawyers from sending targeted direct-mail solicitations to accident or disaster victims and their relatives for 30 days after the event. That ban serves a substantial interest in protecting victims' privacy and tranquility and the profession's reputation. The bar's unrebutted statistical and anecdotal record shows the ban advances that interest directly and materially. Its brief duration and the ample alternative channels left open make it a reasonable fit.

Facts

  • The Rules: Florida Bar Rule 4-7.4(b)(1) barred lawyers from sending written solicitations about personal injury, wrongful death, or an accident or disaster to the person involved or a relative unless the event occurred more than 30 days before the mailing. Rule 4-7.8(a) barred lawyers from taking referrals from a referral service that contacted prospective clients in ways the lawyer could not. Together they created a 30-day blackout on targeted solicitation of accident victims, direct or indirect.
  • The Bar's study: The Rules followed a 2-year Bar study of lawyer advertising, which included hearings, commissioned surveys, and public comment. The Florida Supreme Court adopted the amendments in 1990.
  • Record evidence: The Bar filed a 106-page summary of the study.
  • Florida lawyers mailed 700,000 direct solicitations a year, and 40% went to accident victims or survivors.
  • 54% of the public viewed contact about accidents as a privacy violation.
  • Among recipients, 45% said the mail was designed to exploit gullible or unstable people, and 27% said their regard for the legal profession and the judicial process dropped.
  • Newspaper editorials criticized lawyers who sent targeted mail to victims shortly after accidents. Citizen complaints described solicitations arriving soon after accidents, including one letter sent three days after a funeral and another received on the day of a funeral.
  • Plaintiffs: Lawyer G. Stewart McHenry and his wholly owned referral service, Went For It, Inc., wanted to keep contacting victims within 30 days. McHenry was later disbarred for unrelated reasons, and lawyer John T. Blakely was substituted.
  • Rebuttal: Respondents never refuted the Bar's evidence. They offered only the conclusory assertion that the Rule lacked "any factual basis."

Procedural Posture

Plaintiffs sued in the Middle District of Florida for declaratory and injunctive relief. A Magistrate Judge recommended summary judgment for the Bar. The District Court rejected that recommendation and granted summary judgment to plaintiffs under Bates and later cases. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed while stating it was "disturbed" that Bates and its progeny required the result. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does the Florida Bar's 30-day ban on targeted direct-mail solicitation of accident and disaster victims and their relatives violate the First and Fourteenth Amendments?

Holding

No. The 30-day ban satisfies all three prongs of Central Hudson. The Bar has a substantial interest in victims' privacy and in public confidence in the profession, its unrebutted study shows the ban directly and materially advances that interest, and the ban is narrow in scope and duration. The Eleventh Circuit's judgment was reversed (Justice O'Connor for the Court; Justice Kennedy, joined by three Justices, dissenting).

Reasoning

  • Framework.
  • Commercial speech has gained protection since Virginia Bd. of Pharmacy displaced Valentine v. Chrestensen, and Bates extended that protection to lawyers.
  • That protection is limited, given commercial speech's "subordinate position in the scale of First Amendment values" (Fox, quoting Ohralik).
  • Under Central Hudson, the state may freely regulate commercial speech that is misleading or concerns unlawful activity. Other commercial speech may be restricted only if three prongs are met:
  • Substantial interest: the state asserts a substantial interest.
  • Direct and material advancement: the restriction directly and materially advances that interest.
  • Narrow drawing: the regulation is narrowly drawn.
  • The court may not substitute its own suppositions for the interests the state actually asserts (Edenfield).
  • Prong 1 (substantial interest).
  • The asserted interest is protecting the privacy and tranquility of victims and families from intrusive lawyer contact.
  • That interest feeds the Bar's broader goal of protecting the profession's reputation and the administration of justice.
  • The Court credits it easily on two lines of authority:
  • States have broad power to regulate professions (Goldfarb, Ohralik, Cohen v. Hurley).
  • Protecting potential clients' privacy is a substantial interest (Edenfield), and residential privacy is an interest "of the highest order" (Carey v. Brown, Frisby).
  • Prong 2 (direct and material advancement).
  • The state must show the harms are real and that the restriction alleviates them to a material degree. Speculation and conjecture are not enough (Rubin v. Coors, Edenfield).
  • Edenfield failed because the state offered no studies and no anecdotes. Here the Bar offered both, and respondents never rebutted them.
  • The Court rejects the dissent's methodology critique. The case law does not require a "surfeit of background information." Other First Amendment cases have accepted studies from other locales (Renton, Barnes) and, under strict scrutiny, history, consensus, and "simple common sense" (Burson).
  • **Shapero distinguished.* The Eleventh Circuit treated Shapero* as controlling. The Court gives three differences:
  • Kentucky never asserted a privacy interest in Shapero. It argued only overreaching, so Shapero's privacy discussion was "casual" and "perfunctory."
  • Shapero involved a blanket ban on all targeted mail, with no time limit and no limit on recipients.
  • Kentucky offered no evidence of actual harm.
  • What the privacy harm is.
  • The intrusion comes from confronting victims with the solicitation "while wounds are still open."
  • The lawyer's learning about the accident is not the intrusion. Often that takes no more than reading the newspaper.
  • An untargeted letter is "different in kind" because it involves no willful or knowing affront to grieving people and does not cause the same reputational harm.
  • **Bolger distinguished.**
  • Bolger struck a paternalistic ban on "offensive" contraceptive mailings because recipients could simply throw them away.
  • Here the harm is outrage at the licensed profession. The Bar's concern is the demonstrable effect of that offense on the profession, and offense in the abstract is not the target.
  • The harm comes from receiving a targeted letter within days of an accident as much as from its contents. Throwing the letter away does not undo that.
  • Prong 3 (fit).
  • Fox rejects a least-restrictive-means test for commercial speech. The fit must be reasonable and "in proportion to the interest served."
  • The test is still more demanding than rational basis. "Numerous and obvious less-burdensome alternatives" are relevant (Discovery Network).
  • Overinclusiveness: the ban's failure to sort victims by severity of injury or grief is not a defect. Drawing those lines would be difficult, and a short, uniform temporal ban avoids them. The Court sees no obvious less-burdensome alternatives.
  • Access to information: the ban is brief, and many other channels stay open: TV, radio, newspapers, billboards, Yellow Pages, and untargeted mail to the public or to discrete segments of it.
  • Respondents identified no case where solicitation within 30 days avoided harm or its absence caused harm. Survey data suggested Floridians have little trouble finding a lawyer.
  • Professional context.
  • Some lawyer speech on public issues and matters of representation gets the strongest protection (Gentile, In re Primus).
  • This case involves pure commercial advertising.
  • The long tradition of state regulation of licensed lawyers makes limited scrutiny especially appropriate.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Kennedy, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg (dissenting). The dissent applies Central Hudson and concludes the Rule fails every prong.
  • Framing. The banned letters may be vital to victims' right to petition the courts, so the commercial speech rules must be applied with "exacting care."
  • Prong 1 (privacy).
  • Shapero controls. It held that the mode of communication "makes all the difference" and that a letter can be put in a drawer, ignored, or discarded, unlike in-person solicitation (Ohralik).
  • The majority's "different" privacy interest is really avoiding offense to listeners. Carey v. Population Services, Zauderer, and Bolger reject offense as a ground for suppressing speech.
  • Mail recipients are not a captive audience. If those cases are being overruled, the Court should say so.
  • Prong 1 (reputation).
  • The reputational interest is circular. It assumes targeted mail is improper, which is the very question in dispute.
  • Protecting lawyers' image by suppressing speech is "censorship pure and simple."
  • Prong 2.
  • The "Summary of Record" is a party-prepared document with no actual surveys, little on sample size or method, and mostly material about TV and phone-book ads.
  • Only two pages summarize a study of attitudes toward direct mail.
  • Nothing in the record shows the Rule actually protects grieving victims. The Bar's counsel admitted at argument that the record did not show what a typical victim receives.
  • Prong 3.
  • The ban covers all injuries regardless of gravity. Criminal law distinguishes degrees of bodily harm, so such lines are workable.
  • Prompt advice matters most right after an accident, when evidence must be preserved and opposing adjusters and investigators remain free to contact victims.
  • The "alternative channels" argument hints at protectionism for the established bar. The ban hurts the least sophisticated victims, who are least able to seek out counsel (citing Trainmen).
  • Less restrictive options exist. The problem is self-policing because clients will not hire lawyers who offend them, and Florida lets clients rescind certain attorney contracts.
  • Overall. The decision is the first major retreat from commercial speech protection since Bates, undertaken to shield the Court's own profession from criticism.

Notes

  • Narrow holding.
  • The Court holds the Rules valid "in the circumstances presented here."
  • The ban upheld is limited in time (30 days) and in target (accident and disaster victims and relatives).
  • The Court stresses that untargeted letters and other media remain available.
  • Interest not decided. Below, the Bar also asserted an interest in preventing undue influence and overreaching (cf. Shapero, Ohralik). The Bar did not press it in the Supreme Court, so the Court did not consider it. A single substantial interest is enough under prong one (Rubin v. Coors).
  • Paternalism line. The majority answers the dissent's charge that it unsettles precedent with a distinction. The government acting for its own interests or for entities it regulates differs from the government acting primarily out of paternalism. The dissent's cases (Bolger and similar) fall in the paternalism category.
  • Evidence standard.
  • Unrebutted statistical and anecdotal evidence satisfied Edenfield.
  • The Court did not require survey instruments, sample sizes, or methodology.
  • Respondents' failure to rebut the evidence below figured in the analysis.
  • Precedents relied on:
  • Central Hudson (test).
  • Fox (no least-restrictive-means requirement; subordinate status of commercial speech).
  • Edenfield and Rubin v. Coors (evidentiary burden).
  • Discovery Network (less-burdensome alternatives are relevant).
  • Goldfarb, Ohralik, and Cohen v. Hurley (state power over professions).
  • Carey v. Brown and Frisby (privacy of the home).
  • Renton, Barnes, and Burson (types of acceptable justification).
  • Bates, Shapero, Zauderer, and In re R.M.J. (lawyer advertising is protected commercial speech).
  • Precedents distinguished:
  • Edenfield (no evidence there).
  • Shapero (no privacy argument, a blanket ban, and no evidence).
  • Bolger (paternalistic offense rationale; trash-can remedy adequate).
  • Contrasted: Gentile and In re Primus involve lawyer speech on public issues and representation, which can receive the strongest protection.
  • Historical: Valentine v. Chrestensen's rule of no protection for commercial advertising was abandoned with Virginia Bd. of Pharmacy in 1976.
  • Overruled: none. The majority expressly denies unsettling precedent. The dissent argues the decision is in tension with Shapero, Zauderer, Carey v. Population Services, and Bolger.
