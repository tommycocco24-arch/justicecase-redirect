Black-Letter Rule

Specific jurisdiction requires (1) purposeful availment of the privilege of conducting activities in the forum and (2) claims that "arise out of or relate to" the defendant's forum contacts. The "relate to" prong does not always require proof of causation, though it "incorporates real limits." When a company systematically serves a market in a State for a product and that product malfunctions there and injures a resident, the State's courts have specific jurisdiction, even if the particular unit was designed, manufactured, and first sold elsewhere.

Facts

  • Defendant: Ford, incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Michigan, markets, sells, and services vehicles nationwide. It distributes over 2.5 million new vehicles a year to over 3,200 dealerships, supports a used-car resale market, advertises in every medium, and supplies replacement parts to dealers and independent shops.
  • Forum contacts: Ford advertised, sold (new and used), and serviced Explorers and Crown Victorias in Montana (36 dealerships) and Minnesota (84 dealerships). The Minnesota Supreme Court noted Ford had sold more than 2,000 1994 Crown Victorias in Minnesota.
  • Montana suit: Markkaya Gullett was driving her 1996 Explorer near her Montana home when a rear tire's tread separated. The car rolled into a ditch and she died. Her estate sued for design defect, failure to warn, and negligence.
  • Minnesota suit: Adam Bandemer was a passenger in a friend's 1994 Crown Victoria that rear-ended a snowplow on a rural Minnesota road. The air bag failed to deploy and he suffered serious brain damage. He sued for products liability, negligence, and breach of warranty.
  • The vehicles' history: Ford designed both cars in Michigan and built the Explorer in Kentucky and the Crown Victoria in Canada. It first sold the Explorer in Washington and the Crown Victoria in North Dakota. Later consumer resales and relocations brought the cars into Montana and Minnesota.
  • Concession: Ford conceded it purposefully availed itself of both States' markets. General jurisdiction lay only in Delaware and Michigan, as all parties agreed.

Procedural Posture

Ford moved to dismiss both suits for lack of personal jurisdiction. The trial courts' rulings were affirmed by the Montana and Minnesota Supreme Courts, which rejected Ford's causation argument. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and decided the cases together.

Issue

Does the Due Process Clause bar a State from exercising specific jurisdiction over a manufacturer that extensively serves the in-state market for a vehicle model, when a resident is injured in-state by that model but the particular car was designed, made, and first sold elsewhere?

Holding

No. Ford's activities in Montana and Minnesota are closely enough connected to the resident-plaintiffs' claims of in-state injury to support specific jurisdiction, because "relate to" does not demand strict causation. The judgments were affirmed. Justice Kagan wrote for the Court, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kavanaugh. Justice Alito concurred in the judgment, Justice Gorsuch (joined by Justice Thomas) concurred in the judgment, and Justice Barrett did not participate.

Reasoning

  • Framework. The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause limits state-court jurisdiction. Under International Shoe, the defendant must have contacts making the suit reasonable and consistent with "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice."
  • General jurisdiction. It applies only where the defendant is "essentially at home" (Goodyear). For a corporation, that means its place of incorporation and principal place of business (Daimler). For Ford, those are Delaware and Michigan.
  • Specific jurisdiction, element one: purposeful availment. The defendant must take "some act by which [it] purposefully avails itself" of the forum (Hanson). Contacts must be the defendant's own choice and not "random, isolated, or fortuitous" (Keeton). They must show the defendant "reached out beyond" its home, for example by exploiting a market there (Walden). Ford conceded this element.
  • Specific jurisdiction, element two: relatedness. Claims must "arise out of or relate to" the defendant's forum contacts (Bristol-Myers, quoting Daimler). Equivalently, there must be "an affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy, principally, [an] activity or an occurrence that takes place in the forum State" (Bristol-Myers, quoting Goodyear).
  • Text of the standard. "Arise out of" asks about causation. "Relate to," after the "or," contemplates that some relationships support jurisdiction without a causal showing. "Relate to" still "incorporates real limits" to protect out-of-state defendants. No precedent required a strict causal relationship.
  • Underlying values. The rules serve (1) fairness to defendants, through reciprocity for enjoying a State's benefits and protection (International Shoe) and fair warning that lets defendants "structure [their] primary conduct" (Burger King, World-Wide Volkswagen), and (2) interstate federalism, keeping States with "little legitimate interest" from encroaching on more affected States (Bristol-Myers).
  • Precedent squarely on point. World-Wide Volkswagen denied jurisdiction over a New York dealer but said that Audi and Volkswagen, which served the Oklahoma market, could be sued there when a car sold in New York caught fire in Oklahoma. The Court conceded this was technically dicta. It has been repeated in Keeton, Burger King, Goodyear, the Asahi plurality, and Daimler, which used a California resident suing Daimler over a California accident as its illustration of specific jurisdiction.
  • Application. Ford advertised, sold, and serviced the very models at issue in both States for years. Residents alleged those models malfunctioned and injured them there. That creates a strong "relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation," the "essential foundation" of specific jurisdiction (Helicopteros).
  • Ford's "same claims without forum contacts" argument. That argument restates the causation-only test the Court rejected. A similar argument was available in World-Wide Volkswagen. The Court also said the premise is doubtful: Ford's local ads, service, parts, and resale market may have led residents to own Fords. Plaintiffs did not allege those links, and jurisdiction should not "ride on the exact reasons for an individual plaintiff's purchase." In footnote 5, a plaintiff who moved to the forum with the car would stand in the same position.
  • Fairness. Ford enjoys the benefits of Montana and Minnesota law. In return, it owes an obligation that the models it markets there be safe to use there. Under World-Wide Volkswagen, an automaker regularly marketing a vehicle in a State has "clear notice" of suit there and can insure, raise prices, or exit.
  • Federalism. The forum States have strong interests in giving residents a convenient forum and enforcing safety rules (Burger King, Keeton). Ford's rule would channel suits to Washington and North Dakota, whose only tie is a long-ago first sale. That would weaken the Clause's "jurisdiction-allocating function."
  • **Bristol-Myers distinguished.** There, nonresident plaintiffs were not prescribed the drug, did not ingest it, and were not injured in California. The forum lacked any connection to their claims, which the Court characterized as forum-shopping. Here, plaintiffs are residents who used the products and were injured in the forum.
  • **Walden distinguished.* There, the defendant officer formed no forum contacts at all, so the Court never reached relatedness. Plaintiff residence and place of injury cannot create a defendant's contact. They can still bear on whether existing contacts relate to the suit, as Bristol-Myers* itself shows.
  • Rejection of Gorsuch's approach (n.3). The majority rejected any view that a nationwide corporation could be sued on any claim in any State. That would make "case-linked" jurisdiction "not case-linked at all."

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Alito, J., concurring in the judgment (no joiners). The case is settled by World-Wide Volkswagen and needs no change in doctrine. He would reject Ford's strict but-for proof requirement while still requiring some causal link. Here, a "causal in a broad sense" link exists, because Ford's advertising, sales, service, and parts put these cars on Montana and Minnesota roads. "Arise out of" and "relate to" overlap and simply restate International Shoe's minimum-contacts standard. Treating "relate to" as an independent basis is unnecessary and unwise, since "everything is related to everything else," and the majority does not say what its "real limits" are. Rough causation supplies that limit.
  • Gorsuch, J., joined by Thomas, J., concurring in the judgment. The majority's split of "arise out of" from "relate to" leaves an undefined "affiliation" test. It is unclear whether that test supplements or replaces causation, why first-sale States (North Dakota, Washington) would lack jurisdiction, and how to handle cases between "continuous" and "isolated" contacts such as the Maine decoy seller. The project is also unnecessary, since the majority's own account suggests a but-for link that would be easy to prove against large corporations. He questions the plaintiff's burden assumption (some States put the burden on the defendant) and sees nothing burdensome in proving purchase reasons. He then traces personal jurisdiction from Pennoyer's presence and consent rules through International Shoe, questions why corporations get special protection when individuals remain subject to tag jurisdiction (Burnham), and suggests the doctrine may return to asking about corporate presence or consent under the Constitution's original meaning. Nothing in that history lets Ford, which entered through "the front door," escape "out the back."

Notes

  • Elements for the outline (specific jurisdiction): (1) purposeful availment: deliberate, non-fortuitous contacts by the defendant itself; (2) relatedness: the claim arises out of (causation) or relates to (non-causal affiliation, with real limits) those contacts. Ford conceded element (1), so the case is about element (2).
  • Narrowness of the holding: The facts are a resident-plaintiff suing a company that extensively serves the in-state market for the same model, over an in-state accident.
  • Expressly left open:
  • A case where Ford marketed the models only in a different State or region.
  • Internet transactions and virtual "presence," citing Walden n.9.
  • The Maine retiree who sells decoys online. The Court agreed that resolving these cases does not resolve that hypothetical.
  • Isolated or sporadic sales, which have long been treated differently from continuous ones. The Court said this is not a rule that any seller is subject to jurisdiction wherever its product malfunctions.
  • The majority did not decide whether the first-sale States would have jurisdiction. It said only that their relationship to the litigation is "less significant."
  • Relied on: International Shoe; Hanson v. Denckla; World-Wide Volkswagen (including its dicta); Keeton; Burger King; Helicopteros; Goodyear; Daimler (including n.5 illustration); Asahi (O'Connor plurality); Bristol-Myers (relatedness formulations).
  • Distinguished: Bristol-Myers (no forum tie to nonresident plaintiffs' claims); Walden (defendant had no forum contacts, so relatedness was never reached).
  • Overruled: None.
  • Majority's response to concurrences (n.2): The Court applied International Shoe and its progeny as it has for 75 years, declining both Alito's suggested modern rethinking and Gorsuch's original-meaning approach.
