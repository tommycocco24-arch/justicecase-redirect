Black-Letter Rule

Battery requires an act done with the intent to bring about a harmful or offensive contact, and intent exists when the actor acts either for the purpose of causing the contact or with knowledge that the contact is substantially certain to result. Proof of substantial-certainty knowledge establishes intent even without any purpose to injure, embarrass, or play a prank. A child is subject to the same battery rules as an adult, and age matters only in deciding what the child actually knew.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff Ruth Garratt, an adult, sued Brian Dailey, a boy aged five years, nine months, for battery.
  • Setting: Brian was visiting with Naomi Garratt, the plaintiff's adult sister, in the backyard of the plaintiff's home. The trial court found Brian was a visitor, not a trespasser.
  • Plaintiff's version: Naomi testified that as the plaintiff started to sit in a wood and canvas lawn chair, Brian deliberately pulled it out from under her. The plaintiff did not testify about how or why she fell.
  • Trial court's findings: The court rejected Naomi's account and adopted Brian's. Brian moved the chair sideways a few feet and sat in it. He then saw the plaintiff about to sit where the chair had been, got up, and tried to move the chair back under her. Because of his small size and lack of dexterity, he failed, and she fell.
  • Findings on intent: The court found Brian had no willful or unlawful purpose, no intent to injure, no intent to cause an unauthorized or offensive contact, and no purpose to play a prank or commit assault and battery.
  • Injury and damages: The fall fractured the plaintiff's hip and caused other serious injuries. To avoid a retrial if the plaintiff won on appeal, the trial court fixed her damages at $11,000.

Procedural Posture

After a bench trial, the trial court entered judgment dismissing the action. The plaintiff appealed, asking the Washington Supreme Court to enter judgment for $11,000 or to order a new trial.

Issue

Can a defendant who moves a chair without any purpose to cause harmful or offensive contact be liable for battery if he knew with substantial certainty that the plaintiff would try to sit where the chair had been?

Holding

Yes. Battery intent is established if the actor knew with substantial certainty that the contact would result, even with no purpose to injure or embarrass. Because the trial court's findings did not expressly address Brian's knowledge, the case was remanded for clarification and for findings on that issue, with instructions to change the judgment if the findings warrant it. (Hill, J., with Schwellenbach, Donworth, and Weaver, JJ., concurring.)

Reasoning

  • Wrongful act required: The court began from the premise that Brian, "whether five or fifty-five," had to commit some wrongful act before he could be liable.
  • Infant liability generally: The authorities generally hold that a minor who commits a tort with force can be sued like any other person, though the court noted "certain notable exceptions" (citing Bohlen's article).
  • Governing rule: The court defined battery as "the intentional infliction of a harmful bodily contact upon another" and adopted Restatement of Torts § 13. Liability attaches to an act that is the legal cause of harmful contact if (a) the act is done with the intention of bringing about a harmful or offensive contact or apprehension of it, (b) the contact is not consented to (or consent was procured by fraud or duress), and (c) the contact is not otherwise privileged. Consent and privilege were not at issue, so the case turned on intent.
  • Two forms of intent: Under the Restatement comment, intent exists if the act is done (1) for the purpose of causing the contact, or (2) with knowledge that the contact is substantially certain to result.
  • Risk is not enough: Intentionally doing the act is insufficient, even if the actor realizes or should realize a "very grave risk" of contact. That realization may make conduct negligent or reckless. Intent requires realization of substantial certainty.
  • Applied to the plaintiff's version: Had the plaintiff proved Brian pulled the chair while she was sitting down, his act would plainly have been done to cause her contact with the ground, and she would recover (citing Vosburg v. Putney and Briese v. Maechtle). The trial court rejected that version of the facts.
  • Applied to the court's version: A battery would still be established if, when Brian moved the chair, he knew with substantial certainty that the plaintiff would try to sit where it had been. Lack of intent to injure, prank, embarrass, or batter would not absolve him if he had that knowledge (citing Mercer v. Corbin). Without that knowledge, moving the chair was not wrongful, and there could be no liability.
  • Why remand: A finding of no knowledge could be inferred from the existing findings. But before dismissal, there should be no question that the trial court actually decided the knowledge issue, since intent could be inferred from knowledge.
  • Role of age: The court applied the battery law applicable to adults. Brian's age matters only in determining what he knew, where his experience, capacity, and understanding are material.
  • Motive versus intent: The trial court's findings negating intent to injure or embarrass addressed motivation. On remand, the plaintiff can recover if Brian had substantial-certainty knowledge, "still without reference to motivation."

Remaining procedural claims (all rejected):

  • No directed judgment: The record did not support entry of a $11,000 judgment for the plaintiff.
  • No change of theory: The trial court consistently recognized that the plaintiff's version, if proved, was a battery. Its comments on intent applied the law to the facts as it found them, so the plaintiff was not prejudiced.
  • Cross-examination: About twenty pages of cross-examination showed no denial of the right. The one restriction, on statements about Brian having chairs pulled out from under him at kindergarten, was harmless because the fact came in when Brian was asked directly.
  • Liability insurance exhibit: An infant's tort liability does not depend on the size or existence of his estate. That matters only to a plaintiff trying to enforce a judgment, so excluding the insurance policy was not error.
  • Newly discovered evidence: In a bench trial the judge could assess whether the evidence would change the result, and denial of the motion was not an abuse of discretion.
  • Pretrial deposition: The court said the deposition "should have been allowed," possibly under court supervision to protect the child, if Brian was to testify at trial. It declined to hold the refusal an abuse of discretion and found no prejudicial error.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Narrow disposition: The court neither affirmed nor reversed. It remanded for clarification of findings on Brian's knowledge, with instructions to change the judgment if the findings warrant it.
  • Fact question left to the trial court: The court did not decide whether Brian had substantial-certainty knowledge. It acknowledged that a finding of no knowledge could be inferred from the existing findings.
  • Costs: Costs on appeal abide the final result. The plaintiff gets them if judgment is entered for her, and the defendant gets them if the dismissal stands.
  • Rehearing: Petition for rehearing denied May 3, 1955.
  • Defined only partially: The court called its battery definition "not all-inclusive but sufficient for our purpose."
  • Deposition dicta: The court noted that review of pretrial discovery rulings appears limited to appeal from final judgment and called that review "illusory," citing Prof. Louisell.
  • Authorities relied on:
  • Paul v. Hummel, Huchting v. Engel, Briese v. Maechtle, Cooley, Prosser, Kent, and Am. Jur.: a minor who commits a tort with force is liable like any other person.
  • Bohlen, 23 Mich. L. Rev. 9: cited for the "notable exceptions" to that general rule.
  • Restatement of Torts § 13 and comment on clause (a): elements of battery and the purpose/substantial-certainty definition of intent.
  • Prosser on Torts § 8: cited alongside the Restatement on intent.
  • Vosburg v. Putney and Briese v. Maechtle: pulling a chair while the plaintiff sits would be battery. Vosburg also supports recovery without any purpose to injure or embarrass.
  • Mercer v. Corbin: absence of intent to injure does not absolve a defendant who had the requisite knowledge.
  • No precedent overruled or distinguished.
