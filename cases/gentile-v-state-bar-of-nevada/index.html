Black-Letter Rule

A State may discipline a lawyer participating in a pending case for extrajudicial statements under the "substantial likelihood of material prejudice" standard of ABA Model Rule 3.6. That standard is less demanding than the clear-and-present-danger test that governs restraints on the press, and it satisfies the First Amendment. A trial-publicity rule is void for vagueness as applied, however, where its safe-harbor provision uses undefined terms of degree ("general," "without elaboration") that fail to give a lawyer fair notice of where permitted comment ends and create a real possibility of discriminatory enforcement.

Facts

  • The theft: Four kilograms of cocaine and almost $300,000 in travelers' checks used in a Las Vegas police (Metro) undercover operation went missing from a safety deposit vault at Western Vault Corporation. Grady Sanders owned Western Vault.
  • Pre-indictment publicity: For about a year, press reports quoted police officials expressing confidence in their officers. The reports said the two detectives with vault access had been cleared after passing polygraph tests, and they said the investigation was pointing toward Sanders. Western Vault went out of business.
  • The press conference: Gentile, Sanders' defense counsel, called the first formal press conference of his career after Sanders was indicted. Kennedy says it was hours after the indictment. Rehnquist and the Nevada Supreme Court say it was the day after, the same day as the arraignment. Gentile said:
  • the evidence would prove Sanders innocent;
  • Detective Steve Scholl was the person most likely to have stolen the drugs and checks;
  • Sanders was a "scapegoat" covering for "crooked cops";
  • four of the other alleged victims were "known drug dealers and convicted money launderers."

He also implied that videotapes showed Scholl with symptoms of cocaine use.

  • Gentile's preparation and restraint: The night before, Gentile and two colleagues spent several hours researching Rule 177. At the conference he declined several requests for detail, saying "ethics prohibit me from doing so."
  • Purpose: The disciplinary board found that Gentile sought to counter adverse public opinion, refute media reports about his client, fight perceived prosecution efforts to poison the juror pool, and present Sanders' side. It also found that Gentile knew Scholl would be a prosecution witness and believed the others he described would be too.
  • Trial: The trial took place on schedule about six months later. Neither side sought a continuance or a change of venue. No juror recalled Gentile or his press conference, and the jury acquitted Sanders on all counts.

Procedural Posture

The State Bar of Nevada charged Gentile with violating Nevada Supreme Court Rule 177. The Southern Nevada Disciplinary Board found a violation and recommended a private reprimand. The Nevada Supreme Court affirmed, finding by clear and convincing evidence that Gentile knew or reasonably should have known his comments had a substantial likelihood of materially prejudicing the case, and Gentile sought review in the U.S. Supreme Court.

Issue

Did Nevada violate the First Amendment by disciplining Gentile under Rule 177 for his press-conference statements?

Holding

Yes: a five-Justice majority, in Chief Justice Rehnquist's opinion for the Court on Parts I and II, held that the "substantial likelihood of material prejudice" standard is constitutional for lawyers in pending cases, but a different five-Justice majority, in Justice Kennedy's opinion for the Court on Parts III and VI, held that Rule 177 as interpreted and applied was void for vagueness because its safe harbor gave inadequate notice, and the judgment was reversed. Justice O'Connor joined both majorities and supplied the fifth vote in each.

Reasoning

On the standard (Rehnquist, Parts I–II, opinion of the Court):

  • History of bar regulation: Courts have long regulated admission to practice and disciplined lawyers. "Membership in the bar is a privilege burdened with conditions" (In re Rouss, quoted in Theard v. United States).
  • Line of rules: The Court traced lawyer-publicity rules from the Alabama Code of 1887 to ABA Canon 20 (1908) and then to the post-Sheppard Advisory Committee standards that led to Rule 3.6. Thirty-one States plus Nevada use the substantial-likelihood test.
  • Participants vs. strangers: In the courtroom, a lawyer's speech is "extremely circumscribed" (Sacher; Fisher v. Pace). Outside it, a majority in In re Sawyer indicated that lawyers in pending cases face ethical limits on speech that ordinary citizens do not. Sheppard v. Maxwell called collaboration between counsel and the press "highly censurable and worthy of disciplinary measures." Seattle Times v. Rhinehart permitted restrictions on trial participants.
  • Press cases distinguished: Nebraska Press, Bridges, Pennekamp, and Craig v. Harney involved press or nonparticipant speech. None involved lawyers representing parties to a pending proceeding.
  • Professional-speech balancing: The lawyer advertising and solicitation cases (Bates, Ohralik, Peel) balanced the State's interest in regulating a specialized profession against the lawyer's First Amendment interest. The Court applied that balancing here.
  • Why lawyers' speech is different: Lawyers have special access to information through discovery and client communications. Their statements are therefore "likely to be received as especially authoritative."
  • State interests: The rule targets two evils:
  • comments likely to influence the actual outcome of the trial; and
  • comments likely to prejudice the jury venire, even if an untainted panel can ultimately be found.

Curative devices such as voir dire and change of venue impose serious costs and may not fully work.

  • Narrow tailoring: The rule reaches only speech substantially likely to cause material prejudice. It is viewpoint neutral and applies to all lawyers in the case. It "merely postpones" comment until after trial.

On vagueness (Kennedy, Part III, opinion of the Court):

  • Text of the safe harbor: Rule 177(3) lets a lawyer state "without elaboration . . . the general nature of the . . . defense," "[n]otwithstanding" subsections 1 and 2. By that grammar, a lawyer within the safe harbor need fear no discipline. This holds even if the lawyer comments on witness credibility and even if the statement is substantially likely to prejudice the proceeding.
  • No fair notice: "General" and "elaboration" are "classic terms of degree" with no settled legal usage. A lawyer "must guess at its contours" (Grayned v. City of Rockford).
  • Trap for the wary: Gentile studied the rule, tried to comply, and declined to elaborate, yet he was disciplined anyway. The board and the Nevada Supreme Court offered only terse rejections of his safe-harbor defense.
  • Discriminatory enforcement: Vagueness doctrine guards against arbitrary enforcement (Kolender v. Lawson; Smith v. Goguen). The Court assumed no discrimination occurred here. The question is whether the rule makes discrimination "a real possibility." That concern is acute when the rule falls on the criminal defense bar, whose mission is to challenge the State, and when the speech criticizes the government.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Kennedy, Parts I, II, IV, and V (plurality; joined by Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens):
  • Political speech: The press conference was "classic political speech" criticizing police and prosecutors, which lies at the core of the First Amendment.
  • Application: Even under the lower standard, an independent review of the record (Bose) shows no substantial likelihood of material prejudice. Several facts support that conclusion:
  • the statements came six months before trial;
  • Clark County had more than 600,000 residents;
  • much of the information had already been published;
  • Gentile avoided polygraph results, confessions, and inadmissible evidence;
  • no juror recalled the conference.
  • Precedent: Mu'Min v. Virginia offers a "pointed contrast": far heavier publicity there did not even require individual voir dire. Timing matters (Patton v. Yount; Stroble).
  • Lawyer's role: Defense counsel may take reasonable steps to defend a client's reputation, including in "the court of public opinion."
  • Against a diminished standard: The advertising and discovery cases (Bates, Ohralik, Peel, Seattle Times) do not support a lower standard for this kind of speech. Sawyer, Sheppard, and Nebraska Press never addressed the permissible scope of such restrictions.
  • Even under balancing: No empirical or anecdotal evidence shows defense-attorney publicity prejudicing prosecutions. The State has many other channels for adverse publicity. As applied, the rule fails even the Seattle Times/Procunier test.
  • Rehnquist, Part III (dissent; joined by White, Scalia, Souter):
  • Rule 177(2): The list is an illustrative guide and creates no evidentiary presumption. It tracks Sheppard's list of prejudicial matters.
  • Overbreadth: The rule is not overbroad. It reaches only participating lawyers and requires a substantial likelihood of prejudice, which "will rarely be met" in bench trials.
  • Vagueness: The rule is not vague as applied. Read against subsections 1 and 2, "general" and "without elaboration" clearly exclude Gentile's detailed accusations against Scholl and the witnesses (National Dairy Products: notice is judged against the charged conduct). Gentile admitted that his objective was to influence potential jurors.
  • Substantial likelihood: On independent review, and giving "most respectful attention" to the state court, the statements were substantially likely to prejudice the trial. They were highly inflammatory and "timed to have maximum impact."
  • Actual prejudice: Requiring actual prejudice would let a lawyer escape discipline whenever charges were dropped or pleaded out for unrelated reasons.
  • Counter-publicity: Responsive publicity is no defense. The remedy for prosecutorial abuse is disciplining the prosecutor, not self-help by defense counsel.
  • O'Connor (concurring):
  • On the standard: She joined Rehnquist Parts I–II. Lawyers are officers of the court and may be held to a less demanding standard than the press, though they do not forfeit their First Amendment rights (Sawyer, Stewart, J.).
  • On vagueness: She joined Kennedy Parts III and VI and found the safe harbor void for vagueness. Gentile tried to stay within it and declined to elaborate. That both sides had "valid arguments" about its scope shows the rule gives insufficient guidance.

Notes

  • Split decision: The case produced two majorities:
  • The substantial-likelihood standard is constitutional for participating lawyers (Rehnquist + O'Connor).
  • Nevada's rule was void for vagueness as applied (Kennedy + O'Connor).

Kennedy's conclusion that the record showed no substantial likelihood of prejudice commanded only four votes.

  • Ground for reversal: The judgment rests on vagueness in the safe harbor, Rule 177(3), as Nevada interpreted it. The Court did not hold that Gentile's speech was protected.
  • Scope disputed: Kennedy described the case as limited to Nevada's interpretation and said it did not decide the validity of Model Rule 3.6. Rehnquist responded that Gentile brought a facial challenge, so the validity of other States' substantial-likelihood rules was called into question.
  • Left open:
  • Nonparticipating lawyers: Rehnquist's majority expressed no opinion on rules regulating statements by lawyers not participating in the pending case.
  • Presumptions under Rule 177(2): Kennedy declined to address whether the subsection creates a presumption of prejudice. Only the dissenting Part III rejected that reading.
  • Outer limits: Kennedy called the case "a poor vehicle" for defining the constitutional outer limits of regulating attorney speech about pending cases.
  • Relied on:
  • For the standard: Sheppard v. Maxwell, In re Sawyer, Seattle Times v. Rhinehart, the Bates/Ohralik/Peel line, and Justice Brennan's Nebraska Press concurrence on attorneys' fiduciary duties.
  • For vagueness: Grayned, Kolender, and Smith v. Goguen.
  • For independent review: Pennekamp and Bose.
  • Distinguished:
  • Press and nonparticipant speech: Nebraska Press, Bridges, Pennekamp, Craig v. Harney, and Wood v. Georgia. The sheriff in Wood spoke as a private citizen with no connection to his official duties.
  • Kennedy's plurality: It distinguished Seattle Times because Gentile used no discovery information. It described the Sawyer, Sheppard, and Nebraska Press statements as dicta.
  • Overruled: No case is overruled.
  • Professional Responsibility tie-in: Rule 177 was nearly identical to ABA Model Rule 3.6. The case links Rule 3.6, the duty of confidentiality under Model Rule 1.6 (cited by Kennedy), and the lawyer-as-"officer of the court" concept.
