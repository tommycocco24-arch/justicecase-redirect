Black-Letter Rule

Congress's power to regulate commerce "among the several States" covers all commercial intercourse, including navigation and the carriage of passengers, that concerns more states than one. That power reaches into the interior of each state but stops short of commerce that is completely internal to a single state. Under the Supremacy Clause, a state law that collides with an act of Congress passed under this power must yield, even if the state enacted it in the exercise of powers acknowledged to remain with the states.

Facts

  • The monopoly: New York statutes gave Robert Livingston and Robert Fulton the exclusive right to navigate all waters within New York's jurisdiction with boats moved by fire or steam, for a term of years. The statutes authorized injunctions against anyone else running steamboats in those waters.
  • Parties: Ogden held, by assignment from Livingston and Fulton, the exclusive right to run steamboats between Elizabethtown, New Jersey, and New York City. Gibbons owned two steamboats, the Stoudinger and the Bellona, and ran them on the same route.
  • Federal license: Gibbons's boats were enrolled and licensed to carry on the coasting trade under the 1793 Act of Congress for enrolling and licensing ships in the coasting trade and fisheries. Gibbons claimed that the licenses gave him the right to navigate between New Jersey and New York regardless of the New York laws.
  • Ogden's bill: Ogden sought an injunction against Gibbons using these boats, or any other steam-propelled boat, in New York waters. The bill complained that the boats were moved by steam. It did not specifically complain that they carried passengers.

Procedural Posture

The New York Court of Chancery granted the injunction and then made it perpetual, holding the monopoly laws valid and not repugnant to the Constitution or federal law. The Court for the Trial of Impeachments and Correction of Errors, New York's highest court, affirmed, and Gibbons appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issue

Do New York's steamboat monopoly laws violate the Constitution, insofar as they bar a vessel licensed under the federal coasting act from navigating New York waters by steam?

Holding

Yes. Congress's commerce power includes navigation between states. The federal coasting license authorizes the licensed vessel to carry on that trade, so New York's laws collide with the act of Congress and must yield under the Supremacy Clause, and the New York decree sustaining the injunction was erroneous. (Opinion by Marshall, C.J.; no vote count appears in the opinion text.)

Reasoning

  • Rejecting strict construction: The Court found no sentence in the Constitution requiring that enumerated powers be construed strictly. Words carry their "natural and obvious import." Where the extent of a power is seriously doubtful, the purposes for which it was granted should guide the reading.
  • "Commerce" includes navigation: Commerce "is traffic, but it is something more: it is intercourse." The Court gave four reasons:
  • No system of regulating commerce between nations could exclude navigation.
  • Congress had regulated American vessels and seamen since the founding, and everyone accepted that as commercial regulation.
  • The Constitution's own exceptions mark the extent of the power. Art. I, § 9 bars port preferences and bars requiring vessels bound to or from one state to enter, clear, or pay duties in another, and those clauses would make no sense unless navigation fell within the commerce power.
  • Even opponents of the embargo treated it as a commercial measure.
  • "Among the several States": "Among" means "intermingled with." Interstate commerce cannot stop at a state's external boundary and may be regulated inside a state's territory. The enumeration of classes of commerce presupposes something left out, which is the "completely internal" commerce of a state. That residue covers commerce carried on within one state that does not extend to or affect other states, and it is reserved to the state.
  • Nature of the power: To regulate is "to prescribe the rule by which commerce is to be governed." The power is "complete in itself," may be exercised to its utmost extent, and has no limits beyond those the Constitution prescribes. Within its sphere it is plenary. The main check on abuse is political: Congress's identity with the people and the influence of elections.
  • Exclusive vs. concurrent power (left unresolved): Gibbons argued that the power to regulate implies the whole power and leaves nothing for the states. The Court said there was "great force in this argument" and that it was "not satisfied that it has been refuted." It did not rest on that ground. Because Congress had in fact legislated, the Court said the only question was whether a state may regulate interstate commerce while Congress is regulating it.
  • Taxation analogy rejected: Taxation can be exercised by two governments at once, each for its own purposes. A state that regulates foreign or interstate commerce, by contrast, does "the very thing which Congress is authorized to do."
  • Art. I, § 10 restrictions: The bans on state import, export, and tonnage duties limit the taxing power. They do not imply any retained state power to regulate interstate commerce.
  • Police powers distinguished: The Court classed inspection, quarantine, and health laws, laws regulating a state's internal commerce, and laws on turnpikes and ferries as part of the "immense mass" of state legislation that Congress was never directly granted. Similar means may flow from distinct powers, and that does not make the powers identical.
  • Congressional acts cited by respondent: The Court read each act narrowly:
  • The quarantine acts of 1796 and 1799 treat state laws as health laws. They also show that Congress may control them as needed to regulate commerce.
  • The 1803 slave-importation act reflects a time-limited constitutional exception in Art. I, § 9. It proves no general state power over commerce.
  • The 1789 pilot act shows Congress adopting the state systems, "until further legislative provision," which confirms that Congress controls the subject.
  • The ability of states and individuals to build lighthouses reflects ordinary property rights. It does not show a power over commerce.
  • Supremacy Clause: The clause making federal laws supreme applies to state acts that stay within state powers but interfere with federal law. In a collision, it does not matter whether New York acted under a concurrent commerce power or under its power over domestic trade and police. Either way, the state law yields.
  • What the license does: The coasting act grants licensed vessels "the privileges" of the coasting trade, and those privileges cannot exist unless the trade itself may be carried on. The license's operative words ("license is hereby granted . . . to be employed in carrying on the coasting trade") are the legislature's words. A license means permission. A voyage from New Jersey to New York is coasting trade. Enrollment gives a vessel its American character, so the license must do something else, namely authorize the trade.
  • Passengers: The Court saw no distinction between vessels carrying people for hire and vessels carrying goods for hire. It relied on the "migration or importation" clause and the 1819 passenger-vessel act. It also found the point not raised on this record, because New York's laws and Ogden's bill turned on how the boats were propelled, not on what they carried.
  • Steam vs. sail: Federal navigation laws do not distinguish vessels by method of propulsion. A federal act expressly provides for enrolling and licensing steamboats. A state ban on steam navigation by a licensed vessel therefore comes "in direct collision" with the act of Congress.
  • Patent power not reached: Because the commerce ground decided the case, the Court did not examine the Progress Clause argument.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Johnson, J. (concurring in the judgment; no joiners stated):
  • Commerce power exclusive: He fully approved the judgment but reached it on different grounds. He would hold the commerce power exclusive. A sovereign's power over commerce is the power to limit and restrain it. The power to set those limits implies the power to decide what stays free, so it "can reside but in one potentate," and the grant carries the whole subject and leaves nothing for the states.
  • Navigation and history: He treated navigation as "the thing itself," inseparable from commerce, and not merely incidental to it. He relied on the founding history of conflicting state commercial laws and on the fact that existing state commercial laws "dropped lifeless" once the Constitution took effect.
  • The license: He disagreed with the majority's view of the license. He saw it as an exemption from certain duties and formalities. In his view, the right of interstate commercial intercourse belongs to everyone, and Gibbons would be entitled to reversal even if the licensing act were repealed.
  • Limited role for the license: He conceded one role for the license. Even if states kept some power until Congress acted, the coasting act fully expresses Congress's will on coastwise commerce, so state power over that subject is at an end.
  • Police powers: He treated health laws as distinct powers that may bear on the same objects as commerce. He called ferry and turnpike laws "commercial facilities," and inspection laws "of a more equivocal nature."
  • Truncation: The opinion text provided ends partway through his discussion of inspection laws.

Notes

  • Narrow ground of decision: The holding rests on actual conflict between New York's laws and the federal coasting act under the Supremacy Clause. The Court expressly declined to decide whether the commerce power is exclusive. It said only that Gibbons's exclusivity argument had "great force" and had not been refuted. The headnote statement that the power is "exclusively vested in Congress, and no part of it can be exercised by a State" goes beyond what the majority opinion actually holds.
  • Dormant vs. exercised power: The Court set aside the question whether the grant alone strips states of power, or whether states keep power until Congress acts, because Congress had already acted.
  • Internal commerce reserved: Commerce that is "completely internal," that does not extend to or affect other states, is reserved to the states. The Court added that Congress may license vessels sailing between ports in the same state as an incident of its express power. Doing so implies no direct power over purely internal commerce.
  • Passenger question not decided on this record: The Court said that whether carrying passengers is part of the coasting trade, and whether a license protects that occupation, "are not, and cannot be, raised in this case." It nonetheless indicated it saw no distinction between carrying people and carrying goods.
  • Patent clause open: The Court did not address whether the monopoly conflicts with Congress's power to promote science and the useful arts.
  • State police powers preserved: Inspection, quarantine, and health laws, internal commerce, turnpikes, and ferries remain subject to state legislation. Where those laws interfere with valid federal law, the federal law controls.
  • Precedent: The majority opinion cites no prior decisions by name. It rejects the conclusions of the New York legislature, Council of Revision, and courts, describing them as the judgment of those "from whose judgment we dissent." Counsel's arguments relied on Sturges v. Crowninshield, Houston v. Moore, and the New York case Livingston v. Van Ingen on concurrent powers. The majority does not discuss them.
  • Interpretive method: The opinion rejects strict construction of enumerated powers. It reads words in their natural sense, gives weight to the purposes of a grant, and uses exceptions to a power to mark its extent.
