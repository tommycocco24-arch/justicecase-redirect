Black-Letter Rule

The Sixth Amendment's guarantee of the assistance of counsel is a fundamental right, essential to a fair trial, and the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment makes it obligatory on the States. A state court must provide counsel to an indigent defendant charged with a felony, as Gideon was. Betts v. Brady's case-by-case "totality of facts" approach to appointing counsel in state court is overruled.

Facts

  • Charge: Gideon was charged in a Florida state court with breaking and entering a poolroom with intent to commit a misdemeanor. Florida law classified the offense as a felony.
  • Request for counsel: Gideon appeared without funds or a lawyer and asked the court to appoint counsel.
  • Denial: The trial judge refused because Florida law allowed appointment only for defendants charged with capital offenses. Gideon replied that the U.S. Supreme Court said he was entitled to counsel.
  • Self-representation: Gideon defended himself before a jury "about as well as could be expected from a layman." He gave an opening statement, cross-examined the State's witnesses, called his own witnesses, declined to testify, and argued his innocence.
  • Outcome at trial: The jury convicted him. He was sentenced to five years in state prison.
  • *Near-identity with Betts: The Court found the facts "strikingly like" those in Betts*, where an indigent Maryland robbery defendant was refused appointed counsel, defended himself, and was convicted.

Procedural Posture

Gideon filed a habeas corpus petition in the Florida Supreme Court, which denied all relief without an opinion. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari, appointed counsel for Gideon, and asked both sides to brief whether Betts v. Brady should be reconsidered.

Issue

Does the Fourteenth Amendment require a State to appoint counsel for an indigent defendant charged with a felony in state court?

Holding

Yes. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is fundamental and essential to a fair trial, so the Fourteenth Amendment makes it obligatory on the States. Betts v. Brady is overruled, and the judgment is reversed and remanded to the Florida Supreme Court for further action not inconsistent with the opinion (Black, J., for the Court; the opinion text does not state the vote).

Reasoning

  • Constitutional text: The Sixth Amendment provides that "[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence." The Court had already read this to require counsel for federal defendants unable to hire one, unless the right is competently and intelligently waived (Johnson v. Zerbst).
  • *Incorporation framework (accepted from Betts):* A Bill of Rights guarantee that is "fundamental and essential to a fair trial" is made obligatory on the States by the Fourteenth Amendment. Applied as a test:
  • Identify the Bill of Rights guarantee at issue (here, the Sixth Amendment right to counsel).
  • Ask whether it is a fundamental safeguard of liberty, "fundamental and essential to a fair trial."
  • If so, the Due Process Clause protects it against state invasion just as the Bill of Rights protects it against federal abridgment.
  • *Where Betts went wrong: Betts applied that framework, used historical data, and concluded that "appointment of counsel is not a fundamental right, essential to a fair trial." The Court held that conclusion was wrong. Had Betts* found the right fundamental, it would have had to require appointment in state court.
  • *Precedent before Betts: Powell v. Alabama had "unequivocally declared" after considering the same historical data that "the right to the aid of counsel is of this fundamental character." Powell limited its holding to its facts, but its conclusions about the fundamental nature of the right were "unmistakable." Grosjean, Johnson v. Zerbst, Avery v. Alabama, and Smith v. O'Grady reinforced the point. Betts* itself conceded that the Court's prior expressions "lend color" to the argument for appointed counsel.
  • *Characterizing Betts: The Court called Betts* "an abrupt break with its own well-considered precedents." Overruling it restores older and sounder principles.
  • Analogous incorporation cases: The Court noted it had treated as fundamental, and binding on the States, the First Amendment freedoms, the Fifth Amendment just compensation guarantee, the Fourth Amendment ban on unreasonable searches and seizures, and the Eighth Amendment ban on cruel and unusual punishment. Palko declined to apply double jeopardy to the States but recognized the "process of absorption" of fundamental guarantees.
  • "Reason and reflection" on the adversary system:
  • In an adversary system, a person haled into court who is too poor to hire a lawyer "cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him."
  • Governments spend heavily to prosecute and treat prosecutors as essential. Defendants who can afford it hire the best lawyers they can. Both facts show that lawyers in criminal courts "are necessities, not luxuries."
  • The ideal of every defendant standing "equal before the law" cannot be realized if a poor defendant faces his accusers without a lawyer.
  • Powell's account of the layman's helplessness: even an educated layman cannot judge whether an indictment is good, does not know the rules of evidence, may be convicted on inadmissible evidence, and "requires the guiding hand of counsel at every step in the proceedings against him."
  • The States' positions: Florida, supported by two other States, asked the Court to keep Betts. Twenty-two States, as amici, argued Betts was "an anachronism when handed down." The Court agreed with the twenty-two.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Douglas, J. (concurring; joins the Court's opinion): Gives a history of Justices who believed the Fourteenth Amendment applies the full Bill of Rights to the States, a view that has "never commanded a Court." He says today's decision "does not foreclose the matter." He rejects Justice Harlan's view that incorporated rights are lesser versions of the federal guarantee: rights protected against the States "are not watered-down versions of what the Bill of Rights guarantees."
  • Clark, J. (concurring in the result): Post-Betts cases (Bute, Uveges, Hamilton v. Alabama) already required counsel in all state capital prosecutions. The Court's decision therefore only erases a capital/noncapital distinction "which has no basis in logic and an increasingly eroded basis in authority." The Fourteenth Amendment protects "liberty" as it protects "life," so the quality of process cannot turn on the sanction involved. He relies on Kinsella, which rejected any capital/noncapital distinction for court-martial of civilian dependents by extending to noncapital cases Reid v. Covert's holding that dependents in capital cases could not be denied Article III and Fifth and Sixth Amendment protections; he says Kinsella foreshadowed today's decision. He notes that Avery v. Alabama might be read to imply an earlier recognition of the right, since it did not rely solely on the capital nature of the offense, and sees portents of the decision in Griffin v. Illinois and Ferguson v. Georgia.
  • Harlan, J. (concurring): Agrees Betts should be overruled but wants it given "a more respectful burial." He disputes that Betts was an "abrupt break." Powell rested on its special facts (ignorance, illiteracy, youth, public hostility, capital charge), and Betts extended Powell to noncapital cases while requiring special circumstances. Since Quicksall v. Michigan (1950), the Court had found no case lacking special circumstances, and it had come to treat a serious criminal charge alone as a special circumstance. Betts "is no longer a reality," and keeping a rule honored only with "lip service" harms the federal system. He would abandon the special circumstances rule at least for offenses carrying "the possibility of a substantial prison sentence," and says whether the rule reaches all criminal cases "need not now be decided." He does not read the Court as departing from Palko, as incorporating the Sixth Amendment "as such," or as carrying the full body of federal law over to the States.

Notes

  • Overruled: Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), and its "totality of facts" / special-circumstances approach to appointed counsel in state court.
  • Framework retained: The Court expressly accepted Betts's premise that a Bill of Rights guarantee "fundamental and essential to a fair trial" binds the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. It rejected only Betts's conclusion that counsel is not such a guarantee.
  • Scope of the facts: Gideon was charged with a felony and sentenced to five years. The majority's reasoning speaks broadly of "any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer." The majority did not separately address misdemeanors or other categories of offense.
  • Expressly left open (concurrences): Harlan left undecided whether the rule extends to all criminal cases. Douglas said the Court's decision does not foreclose total incorporation. Harlan and Douglas disagree on whether an incorporated right applies to the States with the same scope as against the federal government; the majority does not resolve that dispute.
  • Relied on: Powell v. Alabama (right to counsel is fundamental; Sutherland's "guiding hand of counsel" passage); Grosjean v. American Press Co.; Johnson v. Zerbst (federal right to appointed counsel absent competent and intelligent waiver); Avery v. Alabama; Smith v. O'Grady; Palko v. Connecticut (absorption of fundamental guarantees).
  • Distinguished / discussed: Hurtado v. California, whose "sweeping language" Powell had moved past. Palko refused to apply double jeopardy to the States but recognized absorption.
  • Criminal Law framing: The case ends the line Florida drew between capital and noncapital charges for appointed counsel. It ties the fairness of a conviction to the defendant's access to counsel in an adversary trial.
  • Disposition: Reversed and remanded to the Florida Supreme Court. The Court did not order a specific remedy beyond "further action not inconsistent with this opinion."
