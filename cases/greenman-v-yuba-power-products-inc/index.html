Black-Letter Rule

A manufacturer is strictly liable in tort when an article it places on the market, knowing that it is to be used without inspection for defects, proves to have a defect that causes injury to a human being. The plaintiff need not prove an express or implied warranty, and contract-warranty rules such as the sales act's notice requirement do not apply unless they serve the purposes of strict tort liability. To recover, the plaintiff must prove he was injured while using the product in a way it was intended to be used, as a result of a defect in design and manufacture of which he was not aware, that made the product unsafe for its intended use.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff, the injured user, sued the retailer and the manufacturer of the Shopsmith, a combination power tool usable as a saw, drill, and wood lathe.
  • Purchase: Plaintiff saw the retailer demonstrate the Shopsmith and studied the manufacturer's brochure. His wife bought one and gave it to him for Christmas in 1955.
  • Brochure statements: The trial court limited the jury to two statements: that "rugged construction of frame provides rigid support" and "insures perfect alignment of components," and that "every component has positive locks that hold adjustments through rough or precision work."
  • Injury: In 1957 plaintiff bought lathe attachments to turn a large piece of wood into a chalice. After several uneventful sessions, the wood flew out of the machine and struck him on the forehead, causing serious injuries.
  • Defect evidence: Plaintiff's experts testified that inadequate set screws let normal vibration move the tailstock away from the wood, and that more positive fastening methods would have prevented the accident.
  • Notice: About 10½ months after the injury, plaintiff gave the retailer and manufacturer written notice of claimed breaches of warranty and filed suit for breach of warranty and negligence.

Procedural Posture

The trial court found no evidence of retailer negligence or express warranty and no manufacturer liability on implied warranty, so it sent only the implied-warranty claim against the retailer and the negligence and express-warranty claims against the manufacturer to the jury. The jury found for the retailer and awarded plaintiff $65,000 against the manufacturer; after the trial court denied a new trial, the manufacturer appealed, and plaintiff appealed the retailer judgment only in the event the judgment against the manufacturer was reversed.

Issue

Is a consumer's claim against a manufacturer for injuries caused by a defective product barred because the consumer failed to give the seller notice of breach of warranty within a reasonable time under Civil Code § 1769?

Holding

No. Section 1769's notice requirement governs only parties to a contract of sale, and in any event a manufacturer is strictly liable in tort for a defective product that injures a person using it as intended, without any need to establish a warranty; the judgment is affirmed (Traynor, J., with Gibson, C.J., and Schauer, McComb, Peters, Tobriner, and Peek, JJ., concurring).

Reasoning

  • Evidence supported the verdict: Plaintiff's expert testimony let the jury reasonably find negligent construction. The jury could also reasonably find the brochure statements untrue, that they were express warranties, and that their breach caused the injury.
  • Manufacturer's argument: The manufacturer argued that late notice barred the warranty claim under § 1769, and that because the verdict could rest on warranty, negligence, or both, submitting the warranty claim was prejudicial error.
  • Statutory reading: Like the rest of the Uniform Sales Act, § 1769 addresses the rights of parties to a contract of sale. It does not require notice for warranties that arise independently of a sales contract between the parties.
  • Source of non-privity warranties: Those warranties come from common-law decisions. Courts have borrowed sales act definitions (§§ 1732, 1735) because they supplied useful standards. The statute did not require it.
  • Notice rule is a poor fit: Between immediate parties, notice is a sound commercial rule protecting sellers from delayed claims. Applied to personal injury and a remote seller, it becomes "a booby-trap for the unwary," because an injured consumer will not think to notify someone he never dealt with (quoting Prosser).
  • Timeliness not resolved: The court held that "even if" notice was untimely, the claim based on the brochure representations was not barred.
  • Warranty unnecessary: Strict liability began with unwholesome food and has spread to other products posing equal or greater hazards when defective, including a grinding wheel, bottles, vaccine, automobiles, tires, and an airplane.
  • Tort, not contract: Three features of the case law show the liability sounds in tort: courts abandoned the privity requirement, recognized the liability as imposed by law instead of assumed by agreement, and refused to let manufacturers define the scope of their own responsibility. So commercial warranty rules govern only where they also serve the purposes of strict tort liability.
  • Policy: The purpose of strict liability is to place the costs of injuries from defective products on the manufacturers who market them, and to keep those costs off injured persons who are powerless to protect themselves. Sales warranties serve this purpose "fitfully at best."
  • Application: Plaintiff could prove an express warranty only because he happened to read the brochure. The machine's presence on the market itself implied that it would safely do the jobs it was built for. Recovery should not turn on why he chose the machine or on the details of the sales chain. "The remedies of injured consumers ought not to be made to depend upon the intricacies of the law of sales."
  • Jury instructions: Two of the manufacturer's requested instructions were adequately covered by those given. The third lacked evidentiary support.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope of the rule as stated: The rule covers a manufacturer, a product placed on the market knowing it will be used without inspection, a defect, and injury to a human being. The proof standard requires intended use and a defect the plaintiff was not aware of.
  • Retailer not addressed: The judgment for the retailer was affirmed along with the rest. Plaintiff's appeal against the retailer was conditioned on reversal against the manufacturer, and the opinion says nothing about a retailer's strict liability.
  • Notice question left narrow: The court did not decide whether plaintiff's notice was actually untimely.
  • Warranty rules still available where they fit: The court did not discard every commercial warranty rule. Such rules apply to manufacturer liability only if they serve the purposes of strict liability.
  • Relied on: Escola v. Coca Cola Bottling Co. (concurring opinion), Peterson v. Lamb Rubber Co., Klein v. Duchess Sandwich Co., Gagne v. Bertran, Burr v. Sherwin Williams Co., Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors, Inc., General Motors Corp. v. Dodson, and a long list of cases extending strict liability to non-food products. Also relied on Prosser, Strict Liability to the Consumer, and Harper and James on Torts.
  • Followed on notice: La Hue v. Coca-Cola Bottling, Inc. and Chapman v. Brown, which rejected the notice requirement in consumer suits against remote manufacturers.
  • Distinguished: Jones v. Burgermeister Brewing Corp., Perry v. Thrifty Drug Co., Arata v. Tonegato, and Maecherlein v. Sealy Mattress Co. assumed notice was required in consumer-manufacturer suits. They never considered the difference between a contract warranty and one imposed on a manufacturer outside privity, so the court held they are not authority against La Hue and Chapman.
  • Overruled: The opinion expressly overrules nothing.
