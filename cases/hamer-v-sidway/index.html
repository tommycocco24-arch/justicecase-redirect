Black-Letter Rule

Consideration exists when the promisee abandons a legal right or limits his lawful freedom of action as an inducement for the promise. A court will not ask whether that forbearance actually benefited the promisor, or whether it harmed or helped the promisee. Benefit to the promisor and detriment to the promisee are alternative branches, and legal detriment alone is enough.

Facts

  • The agreement: On March 20, 1869, William E. Story promised his nephew, William E. Story, 2d, $5,000 if the nephew would refrain from drinking liquor, using tobacco, swearing, and playing cards or billiards for money until he turned 21. The nephew agreed.
  • Prior conduct: The nephew had used tobacco and occasionally drank liquor before the agreement, and he had a legal right to do so.
  • Performance: The trial court found the nephew "in all things fully performed his part of said agreement."
  • The letters: On his 21st birthday the nephew wrote that he had lived up to the contract and that $5,000 was due. The uncle replied that the nephew "shall have $5,000 as I promised you," that he had the money in the bank on the nephew's 21st birthday, and that he would not "interfere with this money in any way" until the nephew was capable of taking care of it. A postscript said: "You can consider this money on interest."
  • Nephew's assent: The trial court found the nephew consented to leave the money with the uncle on the terms of the letter.
  • Assignment: On March 1, 1877, with the uncle's knowledge and consent, the nephew assigned his rights to the $5,000 to his wife, who assigned them to the plaintiff.
  • Parties: Plaintiff is the assignee. Defendant is the executor of the uncle's estate.

Procedural Posture

The trial court (Special Term) found for the plaintiff. The General Term reversed, apparently on the view that the trust was executed during the uncle's lifetime by payment to the nephew. Plaintiff appealed to the Court of Appeals.

Issue

Does a promisee's forbearance from lawful conduct he had a right to engage in, made at the promisor's request, supply consideration for the promise even if the forbearance benefited the promisee and did not clearly benefit the promisor?

Holding

Yes. Giving up a legal right or restricting one's lawful freedom of action in exchange for a promise is sufficient consideration whether or not the promisor benefits, and the uncle's later letter, with the nephew's assent, created a trust that defeated the Statute of Limitations defense. The General Term's order was reversed and the Special Term judgment affirmed, with costs out of the estate (Parker, J., for a unanimous court: "All concur").

Reasoning

  • Defendant's argument rejected: Defendant argued that the nephew was benefited by quitting liquor and tobacco, so there was no consideration unless the uncle benefited. The court called this rule one that "could not be tolerated" and "without foundation in the law." It would leave open, case by case, whether the promisee's performance helped him too much to count.
  • Definition of consideration: The court adopted the Exchequer Chamber's 1875 definition. Consideration is either a "right, interest, profit or benefit accruing to the one party," or a "forbearance, detriment, loss or responsibility given, suffered or undertaken by the other."
  • Treatises: Anson says courts will not ask whether the consideration benefits anyone or has substantial value. Parsons says waiver of a legal right at another's request suffices. Kent says any "forbearance of a right" suffices. Pollock says the detriment branch is "the most important one."
  • Application: The nephew had a legal right to drink and smoke and gave it up for years on the strength of the uncle's promise. The court would not "speculate on the effort" this required. It was enough that he restricted his lawful freedom of action. Having fully performed, it was "of no moment" whether his performance benefited the promisor. The court added that even if benefit were relevant, nothing in the record showed the uncle was not benefited in a legal sense.
  • Statute of Frauds: The agreement fell within the statute because it could not be performed within a year and was not in writing. The promisor could waive that defense, and his letter and oral statements after the nephew's performance amounted to a waiver. Independently, the defense was unavailable because it did not appear on the face of the complaint and defendant did not plead it in the answer.
  • Statute of Limitations and trust: If the uncle remained a mere debtor, the claim was time-barred. If he became a trustee, it was not.
  • No particular words are needed to create a trust. Any language clearly showing the settlor's intention suffices if the property and its disposition are definitely stated.
  • A person in legal possession of money who acknowledges a trust, with the beneficiary's assent and on valuable consideration, becomes a trustee from that time.
  • A debtor who later agrees that a fund is in his hands and stipulates for its investment on the creditor's account creates a trust.
  • The promisor's language is read in the sense he had reason to suppose the promisee understood it.
  • Reading the letter: The uncle did not say "I will pay you at some other time" or otherwise signal a continuing debt. He said the money was in the bank, intended for the nephew, and would not be interfered with until the nephew could manage it, with interest. The court found "not lacking a single element necessary for the creation of a valid trust," and the nephew assented.
  • Deference to findings: The General Term order did not state that it reversed on the facts. So the Court of Appeals assumed the trial court's findings, which supported the judgment.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope of the rule: The forbearance must involve a legal right. The court stressed that the nephew "had a legal right" to use tobacco and liquor and that the conduct was "not forbidden by law."
  • What the court did not decide: The court said benefit to the promisor was not a proper subject of inquiry and so did not resolve whether the uncle actually benefited. It said only that the record would not permit a finding that he was not benefited.
  • Two-step structure: The contract holding establishes the debt. The trust holding is what lets the plaintiff recover despite the Statute of Limitations. Both are needed for the result.
  • Precedents relied on:
  • Shadwell v. Shadwell (9 C.B. [N.S.] 159): uncle's written promise of an annual sum on the nephew's marriage held binding and supported by good consideration.
  • Lakota v. Newton (unreported, Mass.): demurrer overruled on a promise of $100 for leaving off drinking for a year.
  • Talbott v. Stemmons (unreported, Ky.): abandoning tobacco held sufficient consideration even though it "may have saved him money or contributed to his health."
  • Lindell v. Rokes (60 Mo. 249): abstinence from liquor held good consideration for a promissory note.
  • Porter v. Wormser (94 N.Y. 431): Statute of Frauds defense not apparent on the complaint must be raised in the answer.
  • Day v. Roth (18 N.Y. 448): debtor's later agreement recognizing a fund and stipulating for its investment creates a trust.
  • White v. Hoyt (73 N.Y. 505): promisor's language is read in the sense he had reason to think the promisee understood it.
  • Precedents distinguished as "not in point":
  • Mallory v. Gillett, Belknap v. Bender, Berry v. Brown: promises to answer for another's debt, void under the Statute of Frauds.
  • Beaumont v. Reeve, Porterfield v. Butler: whether a moral obligation supports a later express promise.
  • Duvoll v. Wilson, Wilber v. Warren: enforceability of a covenant in a deed given for natural love and affection.
  • Vanderbilt v. Schreyer: a guarantee extracted by a builder who refused to finish work he was already bound to do was unenforceable. Doing what one has already contracted to do is not consideration.
  • Robinson v. Jewett: performing an act one is already legally obligated to perform is not consideration for a new contract.
  • Overruled: None stated in the opinion.
