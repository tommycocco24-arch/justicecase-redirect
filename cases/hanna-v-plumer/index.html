Black-Letter Rule

When a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure directly covers the point in dispute in a diversity case, the court does not apply the Erie outcome-determination analysis. It applies the Federal Rule, so long as the Rule is valid under the Rules Enabling Act (it "really regulates procedure") and the Constitution (it regulates a matter "rationally capable of classification" as procedural). Where no Federal Rule covers the point, the Erie choice is guided by the outcome-determination test of York, read in light of Erie's twin aims: discouraging forum-shopping and avoiding inequitable administration of the laws.

Facts

  • Parties: Petitioner, an Ohio citizen, sued respondent, a Massachusetts citizen and executor of the estate of Louise Plumer Osgood, also a Massachusetts citizen, who had died before the complaint was filed.
  • Claim: Personal injury damages over $10,000 from a South Carolina car accident allegedly caused by Osgood's negligence. Suit was filed in the District of Massachusetts on diversity jurisdiction.
  • Service: Two days after filing, process was left with respondent's wife at his residence. Everyone conceded this complied with Rule 4(d)(1).
  • State rule: Mass. Gen. Laws c. 197, § 9 bars actions against an executor unless, within one year of the executor giving bond, the writ is delivered in hand, service is accepted, or a notice of claim is filed in the probate registry.
  • Timing: The action was commenced within the one-year period, so the limitations portion of § 9 was not at issue. Only its manner-of-service requirement was.
  • Notice: Respondent's affidavit said he was not served in hand and did not accept service. It did not allege lack of actual notice.

Procedural Posture

The District Court granted respondent summary judgment, relying on Ragan and York, and held service had to satisfy § 9. The First Circuit unanimously affirmed, calling the conflict substantive, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari because the decision threatened uniformity of federal procedure.

Issue

In a diversity action in federal court, does Rule 4(d)(1), rather than a conflicting state service requirement, govern the manner of service of process?

Holding

Yes: Rule 4(d)(1) is valid under the Rules Enabling Act and the Constitution, so it controls service in a diversity action even against a conflicting state rule, and the Court of Appeals' judgment is reversed (Chief Justice Warren for the Court; Justice Black concurred in the result and Justice Harlan concurred).

Reasoning

  • Enabling Act validity. The Rules Enabling Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2072, authorizes rules of "practice and procedure" that do not "abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right." The test from Sibbach is "whether a rule really regulates procedure." Rule 4(d)(1) prescribes how a defendant is notified of suit, which plainly qualifies.
  • Incidental effects are fine. Relying on Mississippi Pub. Corp. v. Murphree, the Court said most procedural rules affect litigants' rights. The Act targets changes to the rules of decision. Incidental effects on rights do not invalidate a Rule.
  • No conflict, no question. Absent a conflicting state rule, Rule 4(d)(1) would clearly control (National Rental v. Szukhent).
  • Respondent's syllogism. Respondent argued: Erie as refined in York requires state law whenever federal law would change the outcome; applying § 9 ends the case; so Erie requires § 9. The Court rejected this on two grounds.
  • First flaw: outcome-determination is no talisman. Even with no Federal Rule, Erie probably would not require the Massachusetts procedure. Citing Byrd and York, the Court said the test is applied by reference to Erie's policies.
  • Erie's twin aims are discouraging forum-shopping and avoiding inequitable administration of the laws.
  • York spoke of "substantial" variations. Trivial variations raise no equal protection concern and do not drive forum choice.
  • At the point of litigation, every procedural variation is outcome-determinative. Example: a plaintiff who insists on state pleading deadlines in federal court will lose.
  • The proper question is ex ante: would the difference matter to the choice of forum? Here it would be of "scant, if any, relevance." The state rule would not have barred recovery. It would only have changed how process was served.
  • The Court said it is "difficult to argue" that permitting service on the wife in place of in-hand service alters the mode of enforcing state-created rights in a fashion sufficiently "substantial" to raise the equal protection problems Erie alluded to.
  • *Second, more fundamental flaw: Erie is the wrong test for a Federal Rule.*
  • Erie has never been invoked to void a Federal Rule.
  • Cases applying state law despite an argued Federal Rule held that the Rule's scope did not reach the dispute, so Erie filled the gap. Palmer v. Hoffman is the example: Rule 8(c) covers only the manner of pleading contributory negligence. The burden of proof is state law.
  • Here the clash is unavoidable. Rule 4(d)(1) says "implicitly, but with unmistakable clarity" that in-hand service is not required.
  • Validity of Federal Rules has always been judged under Sibbach, never under York (e.g., Schlagenhauf v. Holder).
  • Two lines of cases, by design. The substance/procedure line shifts with legal context (York). The Enabling Act and Erie both roughly say "state substance, federal procedure," yet they govern different decisions.
  • An Erie choice is "relatively unguided."
  • When a Federal Rule applies, the court has been instructed to apply it. It may refuse only if the Advisory Committee, the Court, and Congress erred in their prima facie judgment that the Rule is valid.
  • Constitutional power. Erie held that federal courts cannot create rules of decision without a grant of federal authority. Erie itself involved no Federal Rule and a question substantive in every traditional sense. The constitutional provision for federal courts, with the Necessary and Proper Clause, gives Congress power over practice and pleading, including matters in the uncertain area between substance and procedure that are "rationally capable of classification as either" (cf. M'Culloch v. Maryland).
  • Uniformity and housekeeping. Quoting the Fifth Circuit in Lumbermen's Mutual Casualty Co. v. Wright, the Court stressed that the Rules aim at uniformity in federal courts. Erie casts no doubt on Congress's power to make housekeeping rules that differ from state rules. Courts testing a Rule need not wholly ignore how far it makes federal litigation diverge from state litigation (Sibbach). Displacing a Rule whenever it alters the mode of enforcing state rights would "disembowel" the constitutional grant and the Enabling Act.
  • Importance of the state rule (footnote). The Court of Appeals had asked how "important" § 9 is to Massachusetts. The Court said importance matters only for the twin-aims question: would ignoring the rule unfairly discriminate against forum-state citizens, or likely lead a plaintiff to choose federal court? No plaintiff would pick federal court thinking Rule 4(d)(1) was less likely to give actual notice and more likely to yield a default.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Black concurred in the result without a written opinion.
  • Justice Harlan concurred in the judgment, writing alone.
  • He read Erie as a cornerstone of federalism. It is about more than forum-shopping and inequitable administration. Primary private conduct should not be governed by two conflicting systems of law, and federal courts should not make substantive law beyond Congress's legislative power.
  • Both the outcome test and the majority's forum-shopping test "prove too much." Litigants often pick federal court just for the Federal Rules or a preferred judge.
  • His test asks whether the choice of rule would substantially affect "primary decisions respecting human conduct" left to state regulation. If so, the state rule prevails, even against a conflicting Federal Rule.
  • He criticized the majority's "arguably procedural, ergo constitutional" test as making the Federal Rules effectively "inviolate."
  • He said Ragan, "if still good law," would require affirmance, and that Ragan was wrongly decided because Rule 3 would have had minimal effect on primary conduct. Cohen was right because the bond statute targeted strike suits and affected primary activity. He would apply the state rule there even had Rule 23 covered the issue.
  • Applied here, Rule 4(d)(1) would not substantially impair Massachusetts's policy of letting executors distribute estates safely. An executor would just check his own house or the federal courthouse as well as the probate registry. So he concurred.

Notes

  • Direct collision is the trigger. The Court said it had never before faced a case where a Federal Rule was in direct collision with state law. The Rule-based analysis applies only when the Rule covers the point in dispute.
  • Narrow ground at issue. Only the manner-of-service part of § 9 was involved. Its statute-of-limitations part was not, since the action was timely commenced.
  • Alternative ground. The Court also said that even without Rule 4(d)(1), Erie probably would not have required the Massachusetts rule. It framed this as "doubtful," not as a firm holding.
  • No overruling. The opinion overrules nothing.
  • Precedents relied on:
  • Sibbach v. Wilson & Co.: "really regulates procedure" test for Rule validity.
  • Mississippi Pub. Corp. v. Murphree: incidental effects on rights do not invalidate a Rule.
  • National Rental v. Szukhent: Rule controls absent conflict.
  • Schlagenhauf v. Holder: validity decided under Sibbach, not York.
  • Byrd v. Blue Ridge: outcome-determination is no talisman.
  • Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins (which overruled Swift v. Tyson): source of the twin aims.
  • Guaranty Trust Co. v. York: outcome-determination test, reread through the twin aims.
  • Precedents distinguished or recharacterized:
  • Palmer v. Hoffman (and, per footnote, Ragan, Woods v. Interstate Realty, Bernhardt): cases where the Federal Rule's scope did not cover the dispute, so Erie governed.
  • Cohen v. Beneficial Loan Corp.: footnote explains it on forum-shopping grounds; refusing the security-for-costs rule might push a stockholder to federal court.
  • Sibbach (footnote): the forum state barred Rule 35 exams. Sibbach predated Klaxon and assumed the tort state's law (Indiana), which permitted such orders, was the relevant law.
  • Harlan's footnote notes Byrd indicated state procedures apply where the state shows a particularly strong interest, but that this approach "may not be of constitutional proportions."
