Black-Letter Rule

Congress may use the Commerce Clause to bar racial discrimination by a motel that serves interstate travelers, because such discrimination obstructs interstate travel, and Congress may regulate local activities that have a substantial and harmful effect on interstate commerce. The test is whether the regulated activity is "commerce which concerns more States than one" and has a real and substantial relation to the national interest. A court then asks (1) whether Congress had a rational basis for finding that the activity affected commerce, and (2) whether the means chosen are reasonable and appropriate to that end.

Facts

  • Parties: Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. owned and operated the motel. The appellees were the United States and federal officials enforcing the Act.
  • The motel: 216 rooms for transient guests, near downtown Atlanta, readily accessible to interstate highways 75 and 85 and state highways 23 and 41.
  • Interstate ties: The motel solicited out-of-state patronage through national advertising, including nationally circulated magazines. It kept over 50 billboards and highway signs in Georgia and accepted out-of-state convention trade. About 75% of registered guests came from out of state.
  • Discrimination: Before the Act, the motel refused to rent rooms to Black guests and said it intended to keep doing so. The appellees proved it refused Black transients after the Act passed.
  • Statute: Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 entitles all persons to full and equal enjoyment of places of public accommodation without discrimination on the ground of race, color, religion, or national origin. Under § 201(c)(1), any establishment providing lodging to transient guests "affects commerce" per se. Excluded are owner-occupied buildings with five or fewer rooms for rent.
  • Congressional record: The Act carried no formal findings. The hearings and reports contained extensive testimony that discrimination in lodging discouraged and impaired interstate travel by Black Americans nationwide.
  • Record below: The motel offered no evidence. It submitted the case on the pleadings, admissions, and stipulated facts.

Procedural Posture

The motel sued for a declaratory judgment that Title II was unconstitutional, plus an injunction and damages. The appellees counterclaimed for enforcement under § 206(a). A three-judge district court upheld §§ 201(a), (b)(1), and (c)(1) and permanently enjoined the motel from discriminating. The motel appealed directly to the Supreme Court. The injunction stayed in effect on order of Justice Black.

Issue

Does Congress have power under the Commerce Clause to prohibit racial discrimination by a motel that serves interstate travelers, consistent with the Fifth and Thirteenth Amendments?

Holding

Yes. Title II, as applied to a motel that concededly serves interstate travelers, is a valid exercise of the commerce power, because Congress had a rational basis for finding that racial discrimination by motels burdens interstate travel and chose means reasonably adapted to removing that burden. The Fifth and Thirteenth Amendment challenges fail. Affirmed (Clark, J., for the Court).

Reasoning

  • Narrow ground of decision: Congress relied on both the Commerce Clause and § 5 and the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court found the commerce power sufficient and decided the case on that ground alone.
  • Civil Rights Cases distinguished: The Court called the Civil Rights Cases "inapposite, and without precedential value" here, for three reasons:
  • The 1875 Act covered inns, conveyances, and places of amusement without limiting coverage to businesses affecting interstate commerce. Title II is limited to enterprises with a direct and substantial relation to the interstate flow of goods and people, except where state action is involved.
  • Commerce in 1964 differs from commerce in 1875. Population mobility and interstate traffic have grown enormously, so discrimination that inhibits travel now has a far larger impact.
  • The 1875 Act was not grounded in the commerce power, and the Government did not rely on it. The 1883 Court expressly reserved cases where Congress has plenary power, such as commerce.
  • Evidence before Congress: The legislative record was "replete" with evidence that discrimination burdens interstate commerce:
  • Black travelers had to travel great distances to find lodging.
  • They often relied on friends for overnight stays.
  • A special guidebook to available lodging existed.
  • The problem was nationwide.
  • The Court identified two kinds of effect. The qualitative effect was impaired pleasure and convenience from uncertainty about lodging. The quantitative effect was that the uncertainty discouraged travel by a substantial portion of the Black community. The FAA Administrator testified that air commerce was also affected.
  • Scope of the commerce power:
  • The Court adopted Marshall's definition in Gibbons v. Ogden: commerce is "intercourse," the power is "complete in itself," and it covers commerce "which concerns more States than one."
  • The movement of persons across state lines is commerce, whether or not the transportation is commercial (Passenger Cases, Hoke, Caminetti).
  • Moral purpose is no bar: Congress has long used the commerce power against moral wrongs, including lotteries, white slavery, and criminal enterprises. That Congress saw Title II as addressing a moral problem does not weaken the commerce rationale where the burden on commerce is shown.
  • Local character irrelevant: Even assuming the motel's operation is purely local, "[i]f it is interstate commerce that feels the pinch, it does not matter how local the operation which applies the squeeze" (Women's Sportswear). Under Darby, the power reaches intrastate activities that affect interstate commerce enough to make regulating them an appropriate means to a legitimate end. Congress may therefore regulate local incidents in both origin and destination states.
  • Fifth Amendment due process: The commerce power is specific and plenary. If there is a rational basis for the finding and the means are reasonable and appropriate, the motel has no "right" to select its guests free from regulation.
  • Thirty-two states had similar laws, and no successful attack on such a law had been cited.
  • Federal commerce authority does not differ in extent from state power over intrastate commerce (Rock Royal).
  • Economic loss to members of the regulated class "has never been a barrier" (Bowles v. Willingham).
  • Public accommodations laws do not unconstitutionally interfere with personal liberty (District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co.).
  • Takings: The Act is not a taking without just compensation (Legal Tender Cases, Omnia, Central Eureka Mining).
  • Thirteenth Amendment: State public accommodations laws codify the common-law innkeeper rule, which long predated the Amendment. The Civil Rights Cases themselves noted those laws with approval. The Act's requirements are not "akin to African slavery" (Butler v. Perry).
  • Deference on means: Congress could have used other methods, but the choice of means is a policy matter within Congress's "sound and exclusive discretion." The only caveat is that the means must be reasonably adapted to a constitutionally permitted end.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Black, J., concurring (no joiners stated; the opinion also covers Katzenbach v. McClung):
  • The commerce power plus the Necessary and Proper Clause reaches local activities that burden interstate commerce, as in the Shreveport Case.
  • Courts measure the aggregate effect of many similar local acts, not one isolated event (Wickard, Darby, Fainblatt, Reliance Fuel Oil).
  • Whether particular operations affect commerce enough to fall within the power "is ultimately a judicial rather than a legislative question."
  • Remote, speculative effects are not enough, and an "isolated and remote lunchroom" serving only locals with local supplies "may possibly be beyond the reach" of the power.
  • Using due process to strip Congress of power to protect Black Americans from discrimination would be "highly ironical."
  • The Civil Rights Cases expressly left the Commerce Clause question undecided. His Bell v. Maryland dissent addressed only the Fourteenth Amendment without congressional implementation.
  • He finds no need to reach § 5.
  • Douglas, J., concurring (joins the Court's opinions; no joiners stated):
  • He is reluctant to rest solely on the Commerce Clause. Freedom from racial discrimination "occupies a more protected position" than the movement of goods.
  • He would rest on § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. That ground would cover all customers at all enumerated accommodations, whether interstate or intrastate, and would avoid litigation over whether a given establishment or customer meets the commerce definitions.
  • He reads the legislative history as showing that Congress relied on both § 5 and the Commerce Clause. He also reads state enforcement of trespass laws as state action under Shelley v. Kraemer.
  • Goldberg, J., concurring (joins the Court's opinions and judgments; no joiners stated):
  • The Act's primary purpose is vindicating human dignity, beyond economics.
  • Congress based § 201(c) on both the Commerce Clause and § 5.
  • Drawing on his Bell v. Maryland concurrence, he would hold that Congress had authority under both § 5 and the Commerce Clause.

Notes

  • Limits of the holding: The decision covers Title II "as applied" to a motel that "concededly serves interstate travelers." The motel admitted it was covered by § 201(a).
  • Expressly left open:
  • Whether Title II is also valid under § 5 or the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court said it did "not pass" on this and was not suggesting those grounds were inadequate.
  • The validity of § 201(d) and § 202, the state action provisions.
  • Distinguished, not overruled: The Civil Rights Cases, 109 U.S. 3, held to have "no relevance" to a statute resting explicitly on the commerce power. The Court also relied on that opinion's approving reference to state innkeeper laws.
  • Relied on, Commerce Clause:
  • Definition of commerce: Gibbons v. Ogden.
  • Movement of persons as commerce: Passenger Cases, Hoke v. United States, Caminetti v. United States, Morgan v. Virginia, and Hall v. DeCuir (as cited in Morgan).
  • Regulation of moral wrongs and other non-commercial ends: Lottery Case, Brooks, Darby, Jones & Laughlin, Wickard, Boynton v. Virginia, and the other cases in the opinion's list.
  • Local activity affecting interstate commerce: United States v. Women's Sportswear Mfrs. Assn. and Darby, which quotes McCulloch v. Maryland.
  • Relied on, Fifth and Thirteenth Amendments:
  • Due process: Bob-Lo Excursion Co. v. Michigan, United States v. Rock Royal Co-op., Bowles v. Willingham, and District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co.
  • Takings: Legal Tender Cases, Omnia Commercial Co., and Central Eureka Mining.
  • Thirteenth Amendment: Butler v. Perry.
  • Missing findings not fatal: The Act had no formal findings. The Court looked to the hearings, reports, and debates.
  • Companion case: All three concurrences state that they also apply to Katzenbach v. McClung (Ollie's Barbecue).
