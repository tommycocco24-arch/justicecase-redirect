Black-Letter Rule

Materials an attorney obtains or prepares in anticipation of litigation, including witness statements, memoranda, and mental impressions (the "work product of the lawyer"), fall outside the attorney-client privilege. They are still not discoverable on mere demand. The party seeking them bears the burden of showing adequate reasons, such as necessity or that denial would unduly prejudice its case or cause hardship or injustice. Under the circumstances of this case, no showing of necessity could justify forcing counsel to produce oral witness statements, whether kept as memoranda or as mental impressions.

Facts

  • Accident: The tug J. M. Taylor sank while helping tow a railroad car float across the Delaware River. Five of nine crew members drowned. The cause was unknown.
  • Retention of counsel: Three days later, the tug owners and underwriters hired a law firm, including Fortenbaugh, to defend against potential death claims and to sue the railroad.
  • Public hearing: The four survivors testified at a public hearing before the U.S. Steamboat Inspectors. The testimony was recorded and made available to all interested parties.
  • Counsel's investigation: Fortenbaugh privately interviewed the survivors and took signed statements "with an eye toward the anticipated litigation." He also interviewed others and sometimes made memoranda of what they said.
  • Suit: Four of five death claims settled. Petitioner, representing the fifth decedent, sued the tug owners and the railroad under the Jones Act.
  • Discovery demand: Petitioner's Interrogatory 38 and supplemental interrogatories asked the tug owners for exact copies of written crew statements and detailed accounts of oral statements and related memoranda.
  • Refusal: The tug owners, through Fortenbaugh, answered all other interrogatories. They admitted taking survivor statements but refused to reveal contents, claiming the request sought privileged matter prepared for litigation and "almost, the thoughts of counsel."
  • No showing of need: Petitioner made no showing of necessity or hardship. The witnesses were known and available. Petitioner's counsel admitted wanting the oral statements only to help prepare to examine witnesses and to make sure nothing was overlooked.

Procedural Posture

The District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, sitting en banc, held the materials not privileged, ordered production, and held the tug owners and Fortenbaugh in contempt and ordered them imprisoned until they complied. The Third Circuit, en banc, reversed, holding the materials were the "work product of the lawyer" and privileged from discovery; the Supreme Court granted certiorari because district courts had divided on the question.

Issue

May a party, without any showing of necessity or hardship, obtain as of right the written witness statements, private memoranda, and mental impressions that an adverse party's counsel secured in preparing for litigation after a claim arose?

Holding

No: although such materials are outside the attorney-client privilege, the discovery rules do not permit production of an attorney's work product on a naked demand as of right, the party seeking it must establish adequate reasons to justify production, and oral statements in counsel's memoranda or mind could not be reached on any showing under these circumstances; the Third Circuit's judgment is affirmed (opinion by Justice Murphy; the opinion text does not state the vote).

Reasoning

  • Proper procedural device: Petitioner proceeded mainly under Rule 33, which reaches only adverse parties. Rule 34 also reaches only parties and requires a motion showing good cause, which petitioner never filed. The correct route to Fortenbaugh's materials was his deposition under Rule 26 with a subpoena duces tecum under Rule 45.
  • Irregularity set aside: The Court declined to rest on the procedural error. It was not strongly urged and was ignored below. The rules form "integrated procedural devices," and making petitioner refile would just recreate the same question. The Court cautioned that choosing the wrong rule could be decisive in other cases.
  • Broad discovery, with limits: Discovery rules get "broad and liberal treatment," and the "fishing expedition" objection is dead. Discovery still has boundaries: bad faith, annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression (Rules 30(b), 30(d), 31(d)), irrelevance, and privilege (Rule 26(b)).
  • Rejection of the corporate-advantage argument: Petitioner argued that protecting these materials favors corporate defendants over individual plaintiffs. The Court said discovery "is not a one-way proposition" and must be analyzed across all the situations where it could be used.
  • Attorney-client privilege does not apply: The privilege does not cover information an attorney gets from a witness in anticipation of litigation, counsel's own memoranda and briefs, or writings reflecting counsel's mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal theories. The Court did not define the privilege's full scope.
  • Work-product protection rests on policy: The Court said the materials are neither privileged nor irrelevant "as those concepts are used in these rules." Unjustified inquiry into counsel's files and mental impressions "falls outside the arena of discovery" and contravenes public policy.
  • Professional-role rationale (core PR point):
  • A lawyer is an officer of the court bound to advance justice while protecting the client.
  • Proper preparation requires a degree of privacy to gather information, sift facts, form theories, and plan strategy.
  • If work product were open on demand, much would go unwritten. "An attorney's thoughts, heretofore inviolate, would not be his own."
  • Inefficiency, unfairness, and sharp practices would follow. The effect on the profession would be "demoralizing," and clients and justice would suffer.
  • When written work product may be discovered: Relevant, non-privileged facts hidden in counsel's file may be reached when production is essential to preparing one's case. The opinion's examples:
  • The materials may be admissible in evidence.
  • They may give clues to the existence or location of relevant facts.
  • They may be useful for impeachment or corroboration.
  • The witnesses are no longer available or can be reached only with difficulty.
  • Burden and discretion: The burden of establishing adequate reasons rests on the party seeking to invade counsel's preparation, and that burden is "necessarily implicit in the rules." Rule 30(b) gives the trial judge discretion over written witness statements. Petitioner offered no reason, so that discretion had nothing to work on.
  • Oral statements get stronger protection: Forcing an attorney to repeat or write out what witnesses told him creates "grave dangers of inaccuracy and untrustworthiness." The account would not qualify as evidence. Using it to impeach or corroborate would make the attorney "much less an officer of the court and much more an ordinary witness," and professional standards would suffer.
  • No facts are hidden: Petitioner can still reach the facts through searching interrogatories to Fortenbaugh and the tug owners, production of written statements on a proper showing, and direct interviews with witnesses. Wanting to prepare for examination and avoid overlooking something is insufficient.
  • Rules' original understanding: When the rules were adopted, neither the Court nor the bar contemplated opening lawyers' files and mental processes to adversaries. Absent a rule or statute saying otherwise, the Court would not read the rules to reach "so harsh and unwarranted a result."

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Jackson, concurring (joined by Justice Frankfurter): Agreed with affirmance and stressed the effect on the profession.
  • The lawyer and law office are "indispensable parts of our administration of justice," so the profession's welfare matters to society.
  • Discovery is "a two-edged sword." Historically, plaintiffs' lawyers have opposed broader discovery because defendants used it against them. The case cannot turn on helping one class of litigants.
  • The material sought is neither evidence nor a privileged attorney-client communication, so neither principle resolves the case.
  • The demand for "exact" oral statements would force counsel to create a writing not in existence, written in his own language and "permeated with his inferences."
  • Opposing counsel would then use any discrepancy to impeach the witness. That would force the lawyer onto the stand in self-defense, possibly against his own witness or client. Jackson called this the most demoralizing practice he could conceive of for the Bar.
  • A lawyer who interviews a biased or hostile witness would have to deliver that statement and could face charges of suppressing evidence for not calling the witness.
  • Petitioner had the witnesses' names and gave no reason he could not interview them or depose them under the Rules.
  • Relying on Palmer v. Hoffman, 318 U.S. 109, Jackson read procedural rules against their background custom. The literal text might allow the order, but nothing in prior discovery practice suggested it.
  • Signed or witness-written statements are governed by Rule 34 and may be produced on a showing of good cause, for example for impeachment, or where the witness is inaccessible or refuses to cooperate. Petitioner made his demand as of right and showed no cause.

Notes

  • Scope of the holding: The holding addresses a demand as of right with no showing of necessity, for witnesses who were known and available, after full interrogatory answers and with public hearing testimony on record.
  • Two tiers of protection:
  • Written witness statements can be reached on an adequate showing, within the trial court's Rule 30(b) discretion.
  • Oral statements, whether in memoranda or in counsel's mind, could not be compelled "under the circumstances of this case" on any showing.
  • Expressly left open:
  • The Court allowed for "a rare situation justifying production" of oral-statement material and said only that petitioner's case is not one.
  • It did not define the content and scope of the federal attorney-client privilege.
  • It said a failure to use the correct discovery rule could be "important or decisive" in other situations.
  • It tied its result to the rules "as now constituted," until "some rule or statute definitely prescribes otherwise."
  • Source of protection: The majority grounds protection in the policy underlying the discovery rules and the lawyer's role. It does not classify work product as a "privilege" within the rules' meaning, although the Third Circuit had called it privileged.
  • What the party cannot hide: A party cannot refuse to answer interrogatories on the ground that the information is solely within its attorney's knowledge. The underlying facts remain discoverable.
  • Authorities relied on:
  • Majority: Rules 26, 30(b), 30(d), 31(d), 33, 34, and 45. Holtzoff's article on discovery for the proper Rule 26/45 route. The 1946 Advisory Committee report and commentary noting the division among district courts.
  • Majority footnote: the broader English practice, which treats all documents prepared for actual or anticipated litigation as privileged (Odgers).
  • Jackson: Palmer v. Hoffman, 318 U.S. 109, for reading procedural rules against their historical background.
  • Overrulings: The opinion overrules no case.
