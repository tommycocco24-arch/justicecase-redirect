Black-Letter Rule

Materials an attorney obtains or prepares in anticipation of litigation (witness statements, memoranda, mental impressions) fall outside the attorney-client privilege, yet they are not discoverable on mere demand. The party seeking them bears the burden of showing necessity or justification, such as that the facts are essential to its case and otherwise unavailable. An attorney's account of oral witness statements and mental impressions receives even stronger protection, and no showing of necessity sufficed to compel it on these facts.

Facts

  • Accident: The tug "J. M. Taylor" sank while helping tow a railroad car float across the Delaware River. Five of nine crew members drowned. The cause was unknown.
  • Counsel retained: Three days later, the tug owners and underwriters hired a law firm that included Fortenbaugh to defend against potential suits and to sue the railroad.
  • Public hearing: The four survivors testified at a public hearing before the U.S. Steamboat Inspectors. That testimony was recorded and made available to all interested parties.
  • Attorney's investigation: Fortenbaugh privately interviewed the survivors and took signed statements with an eye toward litigation. He also interviewed others and sometimes made memoranda of what they said.
  • Suit: Four death claims settled. The fifth claimant (petitioner) sued the tug owners and the railroad under the Jones Act.
  • Discovery demand: Interrogatory 38 asked whether crew statements were taken and demanded exact copies of written statements and the "exact provisions" of oral ones. Supplemental interrogatories sought related records and memoranda.
  • Refusal: The tug owners answered every other interrogatory. They admitted statements were taken but refused to produce or summarize them, calling the request an attempt to obtain counsel's private files and "almost, the thoughts of counsel."
  • No showing of need: The witnesses were known and available. Petitioner's counsel admitted wanting the oral statements only to prepare for examining witnesses and to make sure nothing was overlooked.

Procedural Posture

The District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, sitting en banc, held the materials not privileged, ordered production, and held the tug owners and Fortenbaugh in contempt when they refused. The Third Circuit, en banc, reversed, holding the materials were the "work product of the lawyer," and the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Can a party, without any showing of necessity, use the federal discovery rules to compel production of witness statements, memoranda, and mental impressions an adverse party's attorney obtained or formed in preparing for litigation?

Holding

No. Such materials, though outside the attorney-client privilege, are not discoverable as of right, and the party seeking them must show adequate reasons justifying production, a showing petitioner never attempted. The Third Circuit's judgment is affirmed (opinion by Justice Murphy; the vote is not stated in the opinion text).

Reasoning

  • Procedural misstep set aside: Petitioner used the wrong vehicle. Rules 33 and 34 reach only parties, not counsel. The proper route was a Rule 26 deposition of Fortenbaugh with a Rule 45 subpoena duces tecum. The Court declined to rest on this irregularity because the rules are "integrated procedural devices" and the core question would recur under any of them.
  • Discovery is broad but bounded: The Court endorsed liberal discovery, rejecting the "fishing expedition" objection, and said either party may compel the other "to disgorge whatever facts he has." Limits still arise from bad faith or harassment (Rules 30(b), (d), 31(d)) and from irrelevance or privilege (Rule 26(b)).
  • Attorney-client privilege does not apply: The privilege does not cover information an attorney gets from a witness in anticipation of litigation, counsel's own memoranda and briefs, or writings reflecting the attorney's mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal theories. The Court declined to define the privilege's full scope.
  • Protection rests on policy: The demand fails for a reason other than privilege or irrelevance. An unjustified attempt to obtain counsel's files and mental impressions "falls outside the arena of discovery" and contravenes the policy behind the orderly prosecution and defense of claims.
  • Lawyer as officer of the court: A lawyer must work "with a certain degree of privacy" to sift facts, form legal theories, and plan strategy. If such work were open on demand, much would go unwritten, "an attorney's thoughts . . . would not be his own," and "inefficiency, unfairness and sharp practices" would follow. The effect on the profession "would be demoralizing," and clients and justice would suffer.
  • Burden on the requesting party: Written materials are not immune in all cases. Discovery may be had where relevant, non-privileged facts are hidden in the file and production is essential to preparing one's case. Examples: the documents may be admissible, may give clues to relevant facts, may be useful for impeachment or corroboration, or the witnesses may be unavailable or hard to reach. The burden of establishing adequate reasons is "necessarily implicit in the rules."
  • Written statements: Rule 30(b) gives the trial judge discretion here. Petitioner made only "a naked, general demand" as of right, leaving no room for that discretion to favor him.
  • Oral statements and mental impressions: Forcing an attorney to repeat or write out what witnesses said creates "grave dangers of inaccuracy and untrustworthiness." The account could not qualify as evidence. Using it for impeachment would make the attorney "much less an officer of the court and much more an ordinary witness," and "the standards of the profession would thereby suffer."
  • Facts remain reachable: Petitioner can still get the underlying facts through searching interrogatories, production of written statements on a proper showing, and direct interviews with witnesses.
  • Rejected policy argument: Petitioner argued that corporate defendants would hide behind claim agents and lawyers. The Court answered that discovery "is not a one-way proposition" and must be judged across all the situations in which it might be used.
  • Original understanding of the rules: When the rules were adopted, neither the Court nor the bar contemplated that lawyers' files and mental processes would be open to adversaries. Absent a rule or statute "definitely" prescribing otherwise, the Court refused that reading.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Jackson, concurring, joined by Justice Frankfurter: Agrees with affirmance and frames the case around the legal profession itself. Discovery is "a two-edged sword," and historically plaintiffs' lawyers opposed its expansion, so the case cannot turn on helping one class of litigants. What petitioner sought is neither evidence nor a privileged attorney-client communication. Demanding counsel's written account of oral statements requires creating a document that is not evidence, in the lawyer's language and "permeated with his inferences." It would be used to impeach witnesses and would force lawyers onto the stand in self-defense, sometimes against their own witness or client. A lawyer who interviews a biased or hostile witness would have to record and deliver statements the lawyer would not vouch for. Discovery was not meant to let a lawyer work "on wits borrowed from the adversary." Relying on Palmer v. Hoffman, 318 U.S. 109, Jackson reads procedural rules against the custom and practice assumed by their drafters, even where literal text might allow more. Signed witness statements fall under Rule 34 and may be ordered on "good cause," for example impeachment, inaccessibility of the witness, or the witness's refusal to talk. No such motion was made here.

Notes

  • Doctrinal source: The protection does not rest on the attorney-client privilege or on irrelevance "as those concepts are used in these rules." It rests on policy the Court found implicit in the discovery rules. The Court adopted the Third Circuit's label "work product of the lawyer," which it called apt "though roughly" termed.
  • Two tiers of protection: (1) Written witness statements are discoverable on an adequate showing of necessity or justification, at the trial judge's discretion under Rule 30(b). (2) An attorney's account of oral statements, in mental impressions or memoranda, could not be compelled by "any showing of necessity . . . under the circumstances of this case."
  • Expressly left open:
  • Whether a "rare situation" might justify producing oral statements or memoranda. The Court said only that petitioner's case "is not of that type."
  • Situations where failing to follow the proper procedural rule "would be important or decisive."
  • The full content and scope of the attorney-client privilege in federal courts.
  • Whether a future rule or statute might prescribe otherwise.
  • Distinguished: A party cannot refuse to answer interrogatories because the information is known only to the party's attorney. This case involved production of documents an attorney prepared after the claim arose.
  • Authorities relied on (majority): The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (Rules 16, 26, 30, 31, 33, 34, 45), Holtzoff's article on discovery instruments, and the Advisory Committee's 1946 Report of Proposed Amendments. A footnote describes the broader English approach, which treats all documents prepared for litigation as privileged. The majority cites no cases as controlling precedent and overrules none.
  • Authorities relied on (concurrence): Palmer v. Hoffman, 318 U.S. 109, for reading procedural measures against their historical background.
  • PR significance: The opinion grounds work-product protection in the lawyer's role as an officer of the court, the need for privacy in case preparation, and the professional harm of turning counsel into a witness.
