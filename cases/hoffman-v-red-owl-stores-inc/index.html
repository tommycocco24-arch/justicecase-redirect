Black-Letter Rule

Wisconsin adopts promissory estoppel as stated in Restatement (First) of Contracts § 90, and the promise supporting the claim need not be definite enough to constitute an offer that would ripen into a contract if accepted. The elements are (1) a promise the promisor should reasonably expect to induce definite and substantial action or forbearance by the promisee, (2) actual inducement of that action or forbearance, and (3) injustice that can be avoided only by enforcing the promise. Damages are limited to what the court finds necessary to prevent injustice and may be measured by the promisee's reliance loss instead of expectation damages.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiffs are Hoffman and his wife. Defendants are Red Owl Stores and its representative Lukowitz, who made promises and assurances to Hoffman on Red Owl's behalf.
  • Grocery store: Hoffman bought a small grocery in Wautoma to gain experience. Red Owl representatives had already said Wautoma might be too small for a Red Owl store. Hoffman sold the grocery's fixtures and inventory in June 1961 on the promise that he would be in his new store by fall.
  • Shifting price: Red Owl promised to set Hoffman up in a store for $18,000. After Hoffman sold the grocery and paid $1,000 on a lot in Chilton, the figure rose to $24,100. In November 1961, Hoffman was told the deal would go through if he added $2,000 more.
  • Bakery building: In November 1961, plaintiffs sold their Wautoma bakery building, held in joint tenancy, at defendants' urging and on the assurance that this was the last step needed. Defendants requested that Mrs. Hoffman sell her interest. The sale produced a $2,000 loss.
  • Moving and rent: Hoffman paid $125 for a month's rent on a home in Chilton. At defendants' suggestion that he get experience in a Red Owl store in the Fox River Valley, plaintiffs moved to Neenah instead, at a cost of $140.
  • Collapse: Final negotiations fell through in January 1962. The parties never agreed on the store's size, cost, design, or layout, or on lease terms such as rent, maintenance, renewal, and purchase options.
  • No bad faith: No evidence showed Lukowitz made the promises with a present intent that Red Owl would not perform.

Procedural Posture

The trial court submitted a special verdict framed on § 90, and the jury found for plaintiffs on the promissory representations, reasonable reliance, and plaintiffs' fulfillment of the required conditions, awarding damages on several items. The trial court granted a new trial on the $16,735 grocery-sale award, and the case reached the Wisconsin Supreme Court on appeal and cross appeal from that order.

Issue

Can a promisee recover under promissory estoppel when the promisor's promises were too indefinite on essential terms to form a contract?

Holding

Yes. Wisconsin adopts § 90, which does not require a promise definite enough to form an offer, and relief is limited to damages necessary to prevent injustice, so the order upholding the reliance-based awards and granting a new trial on the grocery-sale damages is affirmed (opinion by Currie, C.J.; vote not stated in the text).

Reasoning

  • Adoption of § 90: The 1933 Wisconsin Annotations to the Restatement said Wisconsin law did not follow § 90. The court's closest approach since then was dicta in Lazarus v. American Motors Corp. Here the issue was squarely presented because the trial court built the special verdict on § 90 and no other theory allowed recovery.
  • No alternative remedy: Fraud was the most comparable theory. Under Suskey v. Davidoff, fraud cannot rest on unfulfilled promises unless the promisor had a present intent not to perform, and no such evidence existed.
  • Trend and policy: Many jurisdictions have adopted promissory estoppel. The court quoted the Arkansas court in Peoples National Bank of Little Rock v. Linebarger Construction Co.: the doctrine keeps remedies "abreast of increased moral consciousness of honesty and fair representations in all business dealings." The court called it "a needed tool" to prevent injustice in a proper case.
  • The label: "Estoppel" as a basis for affirmative relief clashes with the traditional view that estoppel is a shield and not a sword (Utschig v. McClone). The court kept the term anyway because of its wide use and the lack of a better substitute, comparing it to "attractive nuisance."
  • Definiteness not required: The doctrine began as a substitute for consideration for gratuitous promises. If it required a promise definite enough to form a contract, Red Owl's promises would fail. Section 90 imposes no such requirement. Its three conditions are the only test.
  • Distinct from contract: Treating promissory estoppel as equivalent to breach of contract would be a mistake. Fluidity in applying the concept should be preserved (citing Boyer).
  • Division of labor: Elements (1) and (2) are fact questions ordinarily for the jury. Element (3), injustice, is a policy decision for the court and involves discretion.
  • Application: Ample evidence supported the jury's findings on the promises, reasonable reliance, and plaintiffs' fulfillment of conditions. Injustice would result without some relief.
  • Damages measure: Damages should be only those the court finds necessary to prevent injustice, and mechanical approaches should be avoided. The court quoted Corbin (enforcement can mean specific performance, expectation damages, reliance damages, or restitution), Seavey (the wrong is causing a detrimental change of position, so damages should not exceed that loss), and Shattuck (no rule requires contract damages in every consensual case).
  • Bakery loss ($2,000), approved in full: Ordinarily only the promisee may enforce. A third party's reliance can support relief where the promisor foresaw or had reason to foresee it (Corbin). Defendants foresaw and actually requested Mrs. Hoffman's sale of her joint interest.
  • Lot payment ($1,000), approved: The payment was part of the purchase price and gave Hoffman an equity that could not be foreclosed without a chance to pay the balance. Requiring Hoffman to invest $5,000 more to protect $1,000 would be unreasonable.
  • Rent ($125) and moving ($140), approved: Both were reasonable expenses incurred in reliance on defendants' assurances.
  • Grocery sale ($16,735), new trial affirmed: This is not a breach-of-contract action, so lost profits are not recoverable. Profit evidence is relevant only to goodwill as part of fair market value. Because Hoffman bought the grocery as a temporary experiment, justice requires no more than the difference between sale price and fair market value. The evidence did not support the large award.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Limits: The court framed promissory estoppel as distinct from a breach-of-contract action. Recovery is not automatically measured by expectation, and the court retains discretion over the injustice element and the amount of relief.
  • Third parties: The rule that only the promisee may enforce is the ordinary one. The exception applied here rested on defendants foreseeing and actually requesting Mrs. Hoffman's action.
  • Left open: The court did not set a fixed damages formula for promissory estoppel. It rejected "mechanical or rule-of-thumb approaches" and remanded the grocery item for a new trial on fair market value.
  • Contrary view acknowledged: A footnote cites a Vanderbilt Law Review note arguing that promissory estoppel cases should receive full contract damages. The court did not adopt it.
  • Relied on: Lazarus v. American Motors Corp. (prior dicta recognizing § 90's equitable considerations); Suskey v. Davidoff (fraud requires present intent not to perform); Utschig v. McClone (estoppel as shield, not sword); Flamingo v. Waukesha concurrence (criticism of "attractive nuisance" as a term); Peoples National Bank of Little Rock v. Linebarger Construction Co. (policy rationale).
  • Other jurisdictions cited as granting promissory estoppel relief: Goodman v. Dicker; Drennan v. Star Paving Co.; Van Hook v. Southern California Waiters Alliance; Chrysler Corp. v. Quimby; Lusk-Harbison-Jones, Inc. v. Universal Credit Co.; Feinberg v. Pfeiffer Co.; Schafer v. Fraser; Northwestern Engineering Co. v. Ellerman.
  • Departure from prior Wisconsin view: The court did not overrule any named case. It rejected the 1933 Wisconsin Annotations' position that Wisconsin would not approve § 90.
