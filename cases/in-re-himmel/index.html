Black-Letter Rule

Under Rule 1-103(a), a lawyer who possesses unprivileged knowledge of another lawyer's illegal conduct involving moral turpitude, or conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or misrepresentation, must report it to an authority empowered to investigate or act on it. The duty belongs to the lawyer personally. A client's own complaint, a client's instruction not to report, and the absence of a dishonest motive are no defense. Information is unprivileged if it was disclosed in the presence of non-agent third parties or was intended for disclosure to non-agent third parties.

Facts

  • Parties: Respondent James H. Himmel, an Illinois lawyer for 11 years with no prior complaints; his client Tammy Forsberg; her former lawyer, John R. Casey.
  • Underlying conversion: Casey settled Forsberg's motorcycle-injury claim for $35,000, deposited the check in his client trust account, and converted the funds. Forsberg's share was $23,233.34, and she tried unsuccessfully to collect it from 1981 to 1983.
  • Himmel's retention: In 1983 Forsberg hired Himmel to collect the money. He would receive one-third of any recovery above $23,233.34.
  • Investigation: Himmel discovered the conversion. He discussed it with the insurer, the insurer's lawyer, Casey, Forsberg, and Forsberg's mother and fiancé. Some talks with Forsberg took place with her mother and fiancé present.
  • Client's instruction: Forsberg said she wanted her money back and told Himmel to take no other action.
  • Settlement agreement: Himmel drafted an agreement under which Casey would pay $75,000. In exchange, Forsberg agreed not to bring any criminal, civil, or disciplinary action against Casey. Himmel stood to gain $17,000 or more.
  • Breach and judgment: Casey breached. Himmel sued and won a $100,000 judgment. His share would have been about $25,588 if Casey had paid.
  • Results: Forsberg recovered $10,400 through Himmel's efforts. Himmel received no fee.
  • No report: Himmel never reported Casey. His first contact with the Commission came when it asked about the lawsuit against Casey. Casey was later disbarred on consent for converting client funds, including other clients' funds after Himmel's duty to report arose.
  • Disputed client contact: Forsberg had contacted the Commission and received a complaint form. The record suggested she never completed or returned it.

Procedural Posture

The Administrator of the Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission charged Himmel with violating Rule 1-103(a). The Hearing Board found a violation and recommended a private reprimand, and the Review Board found no violation and recommended dismissal. The Illinois Supreme Court granted the Administrator leave to file exceptions to the Review Board's report.

Issue

Does a lawyer violate Rule 1-103(a) by failing to report another lawyer's conversion of client funds when the client directed the lawyer not to report it and may have contacted the Commission herself?

Holding

Yes. A lawyer with unprivileged knowledge of another lawyer's conversion of client funds must report it under Rule 1-103(a), and client instructions, a client's own complaint, and lack of financial motive are no defense. The court (Justice Stamos) rejected the Review Board's recommendation, agreed with the Hearing Board that a violation occurred, rejected the private reprimand as too lenient, and suspended Himmel for one year.

Reasoning

  • The rule's text. Rule 1-103(a) requires a lawyer with "unprivileged knowledge of a violation of Rule 1-102(a)(3) or (4)" to report it to an authority empowered to investigate or act. Rule 1-102(a)(3) bars illegal conduct involving moral turpitude. Rule 1-102(a)(4) bars conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or misrepresentation.
  • ABA guidance. The Illinois rules track the ABA Model Code, so the court found ABA Informal Opinion 1210 instructive. That opinion reads DR 1-103(a) to require reporting of any unprivileged knowledge of a lawyer's misconduct listed in DR 1-102.
  • Importance of the duty. The court relied on In re Anglin, which denied reinstatement to a petitioner who believed in a "code of silence." If the reporting rule is violated, discipline is mandated.
  • Client's complaint is irrelevant. Respondent cited no authority, and the court found none. Common sense dictates that a client's actions cannot relieve a lawyer of the lawyer's own duty under the Code. The court therefore did not resolve whether Forsberg actually reported Casey.
  • Client's instruction is no defense. A lawyer is an officer of the court bound to uphold the Code. Canon 1 says a lawyer "should assist in maintaining the integrity and competence of the legal profession." A lawyer cannot circumvent the rules by claiming the client asked him to.
  • No privilege. The court applied the Wigmore eight-part definition adopted in People v. Adam. The privilege covers communications (1) where legal advice is sought (2) from a professional legal adviser in that capacity, (3) relating to that purpose, (4) made in confidence (5) by the client, which (6) are at the client's instance permanently protected (7) from disclosure by the client or the adviser, (8) unless waived. The information failed the confidentiality element on two grounds:
  • Under People v. Williams, statements made in the presence of third parties who are not agents of client or lawyer are not privileged. Forsberg's mother and fiancé were present for some discussions.
  • Under People v. Werhollick, matters the client intends the lawyer to disclose to non-agent third parties are not privileged. With Forsberg's consent, Himmel discussed the conversion with the insurer, its lawyer, and Casey.
  • Motive is irrelevant to liability. Discipline can be appropriate without a dishonest motive (In re Weinberg; In re Clayter). Client approval does not immunize a lawyer from discipline (In re Thompson; People ex rel. Scholes v. Keithley).
  • Underlying misconduct qualifies. Respondent did not dispute it, and conversion of client funds is conduct involving moral turpitude (In re Levin; In re Stillo).
  • Sanction. The court decides sanctions itself and treats Board recommendations as advisory (Levin, citing In re Hopper; Weinberg, citing In re Winn). It measures discipline against the purposes of the disciplinary process: maintaining the integrity of the profession, protecting the administration of justice from reproach, and safeguarding the public (Levin, quoting In re Crisel and In re LaPinska).
  • Aggravating factors:
  • The failure to report interfered with the Commission's investigation of Casey and so with the administration of justice.
  • Some members of the public might have been spared Casey's misconduct had Himmel reported.
  • The court was "particularly disturbed" that Himmel drafted a settlement with Casey instead of reporting him. By doing so, Himmel and his client violated the Criminal Code's prohibition on compounding a crime (Ill. Rev. Stat. 1987, ch. 38, par. 32-1), and both stood to gain financially.
  • Mitigating factors. Fairness requires considering mitigation (In re Yamaguchi, citing In re Neff). The court weighed the $10,400 recovery, 11 years of practice without complaints, and the absence of a fee. It held these did not outweigh the seriousness of the failure to report, the interference with the investigation, and the decision to settle.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope of the duty: The rule reaches only unprivileged knowledge of violations of Rule 1-102(a)(3) or (4). The court did not address knowledge of other kinds of misconduct.
  • Privilege analysis was qualified: The court said the information was not privileged "unless the mother and fiance were agents" of the client, without deciding the agency question. It rested on Werhollick and said the result followed "probably" under Williams.
  • Left unresolved: Whether Forsberg actually reported Casey to the Commission. The court held the question irrelevant.
  • Code of Professional Responsibility setting: The opinion applies the Illinois Code, modeled on the ABA Model Code, and uses ABA Informal Op. 1210 as persuasive authority.
  • Sanction exceeded both Boards and the Administrator's request: The Administrator asked for "at least a censure." The court imposed a one-year suspension, beyond both Board recommendations.
  • Precedents relied on:
  • In re Anglin: duty to report; the "code of silence" bars reinstatement.
  • People v. Adam: Wigmore definition of privilege.
  • People v. Williams: third-party presence defeats privilege.
  • People v. Werhollick: information intended for disclosure to third parties is not privileged.
  • In re Levin and In re Stillo: conversion is moral turpitude.
  • In re Weinberg and In re Clayter: no dishonest motive is required.
  • In re Thompson and People ex rel. Scholes v. Keithley: client approval is no shield.
  • In re Yamaguchi and In re Taylor: the canons are a safe guide, and lawyers may be disciplined for breaching them.
  • Yamaguchi and In re Neff: mitigation must be considered.
  • Levin, Hopper, Weinberg, and Winn: the court's sanction authority.
  • Crisel and LaPinska: purposes of discipline.
  • No precedent overruled or distinguished in the opinion text.
