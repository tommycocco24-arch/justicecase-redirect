Black-Letter Rule

Under Rule 1-103(a) of the Illinois Code of Professional Responsibility, a lawyer who possesses unprivileged knowledge that another lawyer has engaged in illegal conduct involving moral turpitude or conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or misrepresentation must report it to an authority empowered to investigate or act on it. The duty is the lawyer's own. A client's instruction not to report, the client's own complaint to the disciplinary authority, and the lawyer's lack of a dishonest motive are not defenses. Failure to report is sanctionable, and here warranted a one-year suspension.

Facts

  • Parties: Respondent James H. Himmel, an Illinois lawyer since 1975; his client Tammy Forsberg; her former lawyer, John R. Casey.
  • Underlying misconduct: Casey negotiated a $35,000 personal injury settlement for Forsberg in 1981, deposited the check in his client trust account, and converted the funds. Forsberg's share was $23,233.34.
  • Retention: In March 1983, Forsberg hired respondent to collect her money. He would receive one-third of any recovery above $23,233.34.
  • Investigation: Respondent discovered the conversion. He discussed it with the insurer that issued the check, the insurer's lawyer, Casey, and Forsberg. Some conversations with Forsberg took place with her mother and fiancé present.
  • Settlement with Casey: Respondent drafted an agreement, executed April 11, 1983, under which Casey would pay Forsberg $75,000. Forsberg agreed not to bring any criminal, civil, or attorney disciplinary action against Casey. Respondent stood to gain $17,000 or more.
  • Enforcement suit: When Casey breached, respondent sued in February 1985 and obtained a $100,000 judgment. Respondent's share, had it been paid, would have been about $25,588.
  • Client's instruction: Forsberg wanted only her money back and told respondent to take no other action.
  • No report: Respondent never reported Casey. His first contact with the Commission came in response to its inquiry about the suit against Casey.
  • Outcome for Casey: The Administrator sought Casey's suspension in April 1985 for conversion and other moral turpitude unrelated to Forsberg. Casey was disbarred on consent in November 1985. The Administrator noted Casey converted many clients' funds after respondent's duty to report arose.
  • Mitigation: Forsberg recovered an additional $10,400 through respondent's efforts. Respondent took no fee and had no prior complaints in 11 years of practice.

Procedural Posture

The Administrator of the Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission charged respondent with violating Rule 1-103(a). The Hearing Board found a violation and recommended a private reprimand; on the Administrator's exceptions, the Review Board found no violation and recommended dismissal, and the Illinois Supreme Court granted the Administrator leave to file exceptions to that report.

Issue

Did respondent violate Rule 1-103(a) by failing to report Casey's conversion of client funds when his client had instructed him not to and may herself have contacted the Commission?

Holding

Yes. A lawyer with unprivileged knowledge of another lawyer's conversion of client funds must report it under Rule 1-103(a), and neither the client's wishes nor the client's own contact with the Commission excuses the failure; the court rejected the Review Board's recommendation and suspended respondent for one year (opinion by Justice Stamos; vote not stated in the text).

Reasoning

  • Client's report is no defense. Respondent cited no authority, and the court found none. A lawyer's duty under the Code is personal, so a client's actions cannot relieve it. Whether Forsberg actually informed the Commission was therefore irrelevant. The only question was whether respondent violated the Code.
  • Client's instruction is no defense. A lawyer, as an officer of the court, must uphold the Code. Canon 1 says a lawyer "should assist in maintaining the integrity and competence of the legal profession." A lawyer cannot circumvent the rules by claiming the client asked him to.
  • Text of the rules. Rule 1-103(a) requires reporting "unprivileged knowledge" of a violation of Rule 1-102(a)(3) (illegal conduct involving moral turpitude) or (a)(4) (dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or misrepresentation).
  • ABA Model Code as guidance. The Illinois rules track the ABA Model Code, so the court treated ABA Informal Opinion 1210 as instructive. That opinion reads DR 1-103(a) to require reporting any unprivileged knowledge of a lawyer's misconduct listed in DR 1-102.
  • Duty to report is serious. Relying on In re Anglin, where a "code of silence" showed a petitioner unfit for reinstatement, the court said discipline is mandated if the reporting rule was breached.
  • Information was unprivileged. Applying the eight-part Wigmore definition adopted in People v. Adam, privilege attaches where:
  • legal advice of any kind is sought
  • from a professional legal adviser in that capacity;
  • the communications relate to that purpose,
  • are made in confidence
  • by the client,
  • and are, at the client's instance, permanently protected
  • from disclosure by the client or the adviser,
  • unless the protection is waived.
  • Confidentiality element failed in two ways.
  • Under People v. Williams, disclosures made in the presence of third parties who are not agents of the client or lawyer are not privileged. Forsberg's mother and fiancé were present, so the information was unprivileged unless they were her agents.
  • Under People v. Werhollick, matters the client intends the lawyer to disclose to non-agent third parties are not privileged. With Forsberg's consent, respondent discussed the conversion with the insurer, its lawyer, and Casey. The court rested on Werhollick "and probably Williams."
  • Motive is irrelevant. Discipline may be appropriate without a dishonest motive (In re Weinberg; In re Clayter). Client approval does not immunize a lawyer from discipline (In re Thompson; People ex rel. Scholes v. Keithley).
  • Conversion qualifies. Respondent did not dispute that Casey's conduct fell within Rule 1-102(a)(3) or (a)(4). Conversion of client funds is conduct involving moral turpitude (In re Levin; In re Stillo).
  • Sanction.
  • The court, not the Boards, decides the sanction, though it considers their recommendations (Levin, citing In re Hopper; Weinberg, citing In re Winn).
  • Discipline serves to maintain the integrity of the profession, protect the administration of justice from reproach, and safeguard the public (Levin, quoting In re Crisel and In re LaPinska).
  • Public discipline was needed. Failure to report interfered with the Commission's investigation of Casey. Some members of the public might have been spared Casey's misconduct had respondent reported.
  • The court was "particularly disturbed" that respondent drafted a settlement in exchange for a promise not to report or prosecute. Both he and his client thereby "ran afoul" of the Criminal Code's compounding-a-crime provision, and both stood to gain financially.
  • Mitigation must be considered (In re Yamaguchi, citing In re Neff). Forsberg's recovery, respondent's clean record, and his waived fee did not outweigh the seriousness of the failure to report, the interference with the investigation, and the choice to settle instead of reporting.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Scope of the rule. The reporting duty covers unprivileged knowledge of violations of Rule 1-102(a)(3) or (a)(4). Privileged information is exempt from the rule.
  • Privilege analysis left partly open. The court did not decide whether Forsberg's mother and fiancé were her agents. It conditioned the Williams ground on that point and rested more firmly on Werhollick.
  • Client's report. The court held the parties' factual dispute over whether Forsberg reported Casey irrelevant. It did not resolve that dispute.
  • Compounding a crime. The Criminal Code violation was used as an aggravating factor in setting the sanction. Respondent was not charged with it in this proceeding.
  • Boards' recommendations. The court agreed with the Hearing Board on liability, rejected its private-reprimand recommendation as too lenient, and rejected the Review Board's view that respondent's conduct rectified a wrong without injuring the bar, public, or administration of justice. The Administrator had sought at least a censure. The court imposed a one-year suspension.
  • Precedents relied on:
  • In re Yamaguchi, In re Taylor: Code canons are a safe guide to conduct, and lawyers may be disciplined for not observing them.
  • In re Anglin: duty to report other lawyers' misconduct; "code of silence" shows unfitness.
  • People v. Adam (adopting Wigmore): definition of privilege.
  • People v. Williams: third-party presence defeats privilege.
  • People v. Werhollick: information intended for disclosure to third parties is not privileged.
  • In re Weinberg, In re Clayter: no dishonest motive required for discipline.
  • In re Thompson, People ex rel. Scholes v. Keithley: client approval does not immunize.
  • In re Levin, In re Stillo: conversion of client funds is moral turpitude.
  • In re Levin, In re Hopper, In re Weinberg, In re Winn: the court bears final responsibility for sanctions.
  • In re LaPinska, In re Crisel: purposes of discipline.
  • In re Yamaguchi, In re Neff: mitigating factors must be considered.
  • ABA Informal Opinion 1210: persuasive reading of Model Code DR 1-103(a).
  • Overruled or distinguished: The opinion overrules and distinguishes no precedent.
