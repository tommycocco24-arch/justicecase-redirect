Black-Letter Rule

Under the Uniform Parentage Act, a woman's motherhood may be established "under this part" (Fam. Code § 7610), and by parity of reasoning from the artificial insemination statute (Fam. Code § 7613), a married couple who consent to a medical procedure that results in a child's birth are the child's lawful parents even when neither has a biological tie to the child. Applying Johnson v. Calvert's reasoning to the circumstances here, where the intended mother and another woman could each establish motherhood under the Act, the tie is broken in favor of the intended mother. Parents cannot by agreement limit or abrogate a child's right to support.

Facts

  • Arrangement: Luanne and John Buzzanca, a married couple, agreed to have an embryo genetically unrelated to either of them implanted in a surrogate, who would carry and give birth to the child for them.
  • Agreement: The agreement was oral before implantation. The written surrogacy agreement, signed about two weeks after implantation, memorialized that oral contract. John's attorney conceded this at oral argument.
  • Split: John filed for dissolution before the birth, alleging no children of the marriage. Jaycee was born six days after Luanne's response.
  • Surrogate: The trial court accepted a stipulation that the surrogate and the surrogate's husband were not the "biological parents." John's attorney did not object. The surrogate made no claim to the child.
  • Genetic donors: The egg and sperm donors were unknown to the court.
  • John's "best facts": John's only proffered defense was testimony that Luanne promised to assume all responsibility for the child. The court assumed arguendo that a trial court would believe this.
  • Custody: Luanne has had physical custody of Jaycee since infancy.

Procedural Posture

In the consolidated dissolution and parentage proceeding, the trial court ruled on stipulated facts and offers of proof that Luanne was not the legal mother, John was not the legal father, and John owed no child support, and it declined to apply estoppel. Luanne appealed and petitioned for a writ of supersedeas; the Court of Appeal stayed the judgment, keeping the support order in place, and consolidated the writ with the appeal.

Issue

Are a married couple who consent to a gestational surrogacy using an embryo genetically unrelated to either of them the lawful parents of the resulting child?

Holding

Yes. Because their consent to the medical procedure caused the child's conception and birth, Luanne and John are Jaycee's lawful parents under Fam. Code § 7610 and § 7613 applied by parity of reasoning, and John's paternity and support duty stand regardless of any promise by Luanne to assume sole responsibility. The court reversed the rulings that Luanne was not the lawful mother, that John was not the lawful father, and that no support was due. It remanded with directions to enter a new judgment declaring both lawful parents, to make all appropriate orders ensuring Luanne's legal custody, to amend the birth certificate to reflect Luanne as mother and John as lawful father, and to make an appropriate permanent child support order; until then, the temporary support order remains in effect. Opinion by Sills, P.J., with Wallin, J., and Crosby, J., concurring (unanimous panel).

Reasoning

  • Text of § 7610: The statute says motherhood "may" be established by giving birth "or under this part." It does not say "shall," and it contains no reference to genetics. John's reading, that birth and genetics are the only two routes, misreads the statute.
  • *Parity of reasoning from Johnson: Johnson v. Calvert held genetics established maternity "under this part" by borrowing Evidence Code provisions that on their face applied only to paternity. Confining women to motherhood-specific texts, as John urged, would have reversed Johnson*'s own result.
  • Nonbiological routes to paternity: The Act lets a man become a father without biology, by marriage-based provisions in § 7611 and by consenting to his wife's artificial insemination under § 7613.
  • § 7613 applies by analogy: The Legislature may not have contemplated gestational surrogacy with unknown donors, but courts must construe statutes in unanticipated settings (Johnson). Artificial insemination and this surrogacy are "exactly analogous": each procreates a child through the consent to a medical procedure of someone who intends to raise the child but has no biological tie. § 7613 is the clearest statement of legislative intent where the Legislature did contemplate a nonbiological parent who caused a child's birth.
  • No reason to distinguish husband and wife: Both are equally situated as consenters. That Luanne did not give birth is irrelevant under a statute whose core idea is consent-based estoppel.
  • Estoppel as foundation: § 7613 codifies the common law rule of People v. Sorensen, where a husband who consented to his wife's insemination was "directly responsible" for the child's existence and liable for support. Clevenger v. Clevenger also grounded paternity in estoppel. Estoppel applies here, though the court did not rest on it alone.
  • Tie-breaking by intent: Once Luanne can establish motherhood under the Act, the case looks like Johnson, where the tie between two women was broken by intent. Here it is not close. Neither the surrogate nor the unknown egg donor could prevail against Luanne.
  • Johnson's dicta governs: Johnson said that where neither the gestator nor the ovum provider will take the child, recognizing the intending parents best promotes certainty and stability. Read in context, that "considered dicta" covers any child who would not exist "but for the efforts of the intended parents," including intended parents without a genetic tie.
  • Rejecting the "adoption default" model: Treating nonbiological intended parents as strangers who must adopt (1) contradicts Fam. Code § 7570(a)'s "compelling state interest in establishing paternity for all children," which necessarily extends to maternity; (2) contradicts Johnson; and (3) ignores that dependency law, not adoption screening, protects children from their own parents. The model is circular because it assumes the child is someone else's from the start.
  • Enforcement versus consequences: The court is not enforcing the surrogacy contract or "transferring" parenthood. It treats the agreements as acts that caused a child's birth.
  • John's promise defense fails: Parents cannot contract away a child's right to support (In re Marriage of Ayo). Stephen K. v. Roni L. refused a man's fraud claim over a false birth control assurance. A fortiori, one who expressly consents to a procedure intended to produce a child is responsible: "a deliberate procreator is as responsible as a casual inseminator."
  • Policy: Intent to parent correlates with the child's best interests (Johnson), unlike a rule that makes a child a legal orphan.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Limited to married couples: The court reasoned from § 7613, which addresses a married man. Whether § 7613 applies by parity to a nonmarried couple was expressly left open.
  • Surrogate-only scenario left open: Because the surrogate was dismissed by stipulation, the opinion does not address who is responsible for a child when only the surrogate is available.
  • Gender interchangeability left open: The court reserved whether other paternity provisions of the Act can apply to women. It held only that § 7613 "most certainly" can.
  • Contract enforceability not decided: The court disclaimed ruling on whether surrogacy contracts are enforceable.
  • Custody and visitation: The ruling is without prejudice to John in future custody and visitation proceedings. As lawful father, John has a right to visitation consistent with Jaycee's best interest.
  • No waiver shortcut: The court refused to bar John's surrogate-is-mother argument on waiver grounds, calling that an "intellectual cheat" in a case of first impression.
  • Best interest alternative: In a footnote, the court said Luanne would prevail even under Justice Kennard's best-interest approach from the Johnson dissent.
  • Call to the Legislature: The court urged the Legislature to set rules for artificial reproduction.
  • Subsequent history: John's petition for review by the Supreme Court was denied June 10, 1998.
  • Relied on:
  • Johnson v. Calvert: "under this part" includes genetics; parity of reasoning; intent tiebreaker; dicta on intending parents.
  • People v. Sorensen: consent-based paternity and support liability.
  • Jaycee B. v. Superior Court: this case's prior appeal; John caused conception.
  • Clevenger v. Clevenger: estoppel-based paternity.
  • In re Marriage of Ayo and Stephen K. v. Roni L.: no contracting away support.
  • Moss v. Superior Court: legislative priority on child support.
  • Out-of-state artificial insemination cases assigning support based on consent: In re Baby Doe, Gursky, Anonymous v. Anonymous, K.S. v. G.S., In re Marriage of Adams, K.B. v. N.B., Levin v. Levin, and Karin T. v. Michael T.
  • In re Marriage of Witbeck-Wildhagen: no support duty absent actual consent, confirming that consent is the key.
  • Distinguished:
  • In re Marriage of Moschetta, a traditional surrogacy case. The surrogate there was both genetic and birth mother and sought custody, and the intended mother had abandoned any claim, so there was no tie to break. Moschetta never considered whether the intended mother's participation made her a parent. Its refusal to apply the § 7540 and § 7611(d) presumptions to a woman rested on those presumptions being rebuttable evidentiary devices from the "old law of illegitimacy," not a rule that sex-specific provisions never cross over.
  • Nancy S. v. Michele G. and West v. Superior Court: involved nonmarried couples and custody or visitation, with no support issue.
