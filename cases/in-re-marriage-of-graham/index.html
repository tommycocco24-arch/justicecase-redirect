Black-Letter Rule

Under the Uniform Dissolution of Marriage Act's definition of "marital property" (§ 14-10-113(2), C.R.S. 1973), an educational degree such as an M.B.A. earned during the marriage is not property subject to division on dissolution. It lacks the usual attributes of property: exchange value, transferability, and inheritability. A spouse who financially supported the other's education may still have that contribution considered when the court divides whatever marital property exists, or as a relevant factor in awarding maintenance if maintenance is sought and need is shown.

Facts

  • Parties: Anne P. Graham (wife, petitioner) and Dennis J. Graham (husband, respondent) married on August 5, 1968, in Denver. The opinion calls it a six-year marriage, although the dates it gives (marriage in August 1968, joint dissolution petition in February 1974) span about five and a half years.
  • Wife's role: Anne worked full-time as an airline stewardess throughout the marriage. The trial court found she provided 70% of the couple's financial support, which went to family expenses and to the husband's education. She also did most of the housework and cooking.
  • Husband's role: Dennis worked part-time for most of the marriage, but his main pursuit was school. Over about three and a half years he earned a B.S. in engineering physics and an M.B.A. from the University of Colorado. After graduating he took a job as an executive assistant at a large corporation at $14,000 per year.
  • Joint activity: The couple together managed an apartment house.
  • Assets and children: The couple accumulated no marital assets and had no children.
  • Dissolution: The parties jointly petitioned for dissolution on February 4, 1974, in Boulder County District Court. Anne made no claim for maintenance or attorney fees.

Procedural Posture

The trial court held as a matter of law that an education obtained during marriage is jointly owned property. It valued the future earnings value of the M.B.A. at $82,836 and awarded the wife $33,134, payable at $100 per month. The Colorado Court of Appeals reversed, holding that an education is not divisible "property" under the Act, though it may be one factor in setting maintenance or making an equitable property division. The wife sought review in the Colorado Supreme Court.

Issue

Is an educational degree acquired by one spouse during the marriage "marital property" subject to division under § 14-10-113(2) of the Uniform Dissolution of Marriage Act?

Holding

No. An M.B.A. degree is not "property" within the Act's definition of marital property because it has no exchange value, is personal to the holder, terminates at death, and cannot be transferred; the court of appeals' judgment is affirmed (opinion by Justice Lee, with Chief Justice Pringle and Justices Groves and Carrigan dissenting).

Reasoning

  • Standard of review: Property division is committed to the trial court's sound discretion, and no rigid mathematical formula applies (Carlson v. Carlson; Greer v. Greer). Appellate courts disturb a division only for abuse of discretion. The supreme court, however, may always interpret Colorado statutes, and the question here is statutory meaning.
  • Purpose of division: The goal is to allocate to each spouse what equitably belongs to that spouse (citing H. Clark, Domestic Relations § 14.8).
  • Statutory text read broadly: The legislature's use of "all property" signals a broadly inclusive meaning. Prior Colorado cases defined property comprehensively as "anything and everything which may belong to a man and in the ownership of which he has a right to be protected by law" (Las Animas County High School District v. Raye).
  • Limits on "property": There are necessary limits on what counts as property, and the court finds no indication in the Act that the legislature used the concept in any sense other than the one usually understood to be embodied in the term. It cites Black's as "one helpful definition": "everything that has an exchangeable value or which goes to make up wealth or estate."
  • *Tests borrowed from Ellis:*
  • Ellis v. Ellis (Colo.) held military retirement pay was not property because it had none of these elements: cash surrender value, loan value, redemption value, lump sum value, or value realizable after death.
  • In re Marriage of Ellis (Colo. App.) also asked whether the item can be assigned, sold, transferred, conveyed, or pledged, and whether it terminates on the owner's death.
  • Applying those factors to a degree:
  • It has no exchange value or objective transferable value on an open market.
  • It is personal to the holder.
  • It terminates on the holder's death and cannot be inherited.
  • It cannot be assigned, sold, transferred, conveyed, or pledged.
  • It is the cumulative product of years of prior education, diligence, and hard work, and money alone cannot buy it.
  • It is an intellectual achievement that may potentially help the holder acquire property in the future.
  • It therefore has "none of the attributes of property in the usual sense."
  • Other jurisdictions: The court found no decision, even from community property states, holding that one spouse's education is divisible marital property.
  • Todd v. Todd (Cal. App.): a law degree is not a divisible community asset, partly because it "cannot have monetary value placed upon it."
  • Stern v. Stern (N.J.): earning capacity, even when enhanced by a law degree the other spouse financed, "should not be recognized as a separate, particular item of property."
  • Daniels v. Daniels (Ohio App.): education is a factor in awarding alimony, which differs from dividing property.
  • Nail v. Nail (Tex.): goodwill of a medical practice was not marital property because it rested on the husband's personal skill, reputation, and experience. The court notes Mueller v. Mueller (Cal. App.) as contra.
  • *Distinguishing Greer: The trial court relied on Greer v. Greer, where the wife taught while the husband earned a medical degree, the parties accumulated marital property, and the wife received $150 per month for four years. The court of appeals upheld that award as either a property adjustment reflecting her financial contribution or alimony in gross, "in lieu of" her rights in the husband's property. Greer* did not hold the medical education itself was divisible property. It is also distinguishable on two grounds: the Grahams accumulated no marital property, and the wife here did not seek maintenance.
  • Remaining remedies for the supporting spouse:
  • Where marital property exists, the court may consider the supporting spouse's contribution to the other's education in dividing it (Greer; see Carlson).
  • If maintenance is sought and need is shown, the court may award it based on all relevant factors under § 14-10-114(2). The supporting spouse's contribution to the other's education is "certainly" among those factors.
  • The court twice stresses that neither route was available here: no marital property existed, and the wife sought no maintenance.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Dissent (Justice Carrigan, joined by Chief Justice Pringle and Justice Groves): Would affirm the trial court's award.
  • The asset is earning capacity: As a matter of economic reality, the most valuable asset either party acquired was the husband's increased earning capacity. The wife's earnings were "invested" in his education because she was the breadwinner who gave him the time and funds to study. The majority misframes the question by focusing on the degree. The relevant asset is the increased earning power that came with the degree, which the economist valued at a discounted present value of about $82,000.
  • Equity: The case fits a familiar pattern: a wife works to educate her husband and is divorced shortly after he graduates. Equity demands extraordinary remedies to prevent extraordinary injustice, and narrow concepts of "property" should not leave courts powerless.
  • Timing problem: Had the marriage lasted long enough to accumulate property, precedent would let the court account for her contribution in dividing it (Greer; In re Marriage of Vanet (Mo. App.)).
  • Future earnings already considered elsewhere: Courts consider future earning potential, sometimes through professional goodwill, in property division and alimony (Todd; Golden v. Golden; Mueller; In re Marriage of Goger; In re Marriage of Lukens). Colorado has considered a wife's contributions to the husband's earning capacity in alimony (Kraus v. Kraus; Shapiro v. Shapiro).
  • Maintenance is an empty remedy here: § 14-10-114 limits maintenance to a spouse unable to self-support, so the statute "would seem to preclude" maintenance for this wife. The majority's emphasis on her failure to request it therefore carries little weight.
  • Analogy to tort and wrongful death: The law treats future earning capacity as a compensable asset when someone wrongfully destroys or impairs it (Nemer v. Anderson). A widow recovers lost future support based partly on the present value of her husband's anticipated earnings, including probable increases (United States v. Sommers; Good v. Chance).
  • Single action: The wife might have a separate claim for implied debt, quasi-contract, or unjust enrichment (see Dass v. Epplen). The law favors resolving all aspects of a dispute in one action, so the court should uphold the award in the dissolution case.

Notes

  • Scope of the holding: The ruling interprets "marital property" under § 14-10-113(2). It addresses an educational degree, specifically an M.B.A. The majority does not separately analyze enhanced earning capacity as a distinct asset, which is the dissent's framing. It cites Stern approvingly for the point that earning capacity is not a separate item of property.
  • Facts that shaped the result: The majority repeatedly notes that the parties accumulated no marital property and that the wife sought no maintenance. Both points ground its distinction of Greer and its discussion of remaining remedies.
  • Contribution remains relevant: The holding does not make the supporting spouse's contribution irrelevant. That contribution may be weighed in dividing existing marital property and in a maintenance award under § 14-10-114(2).
  • Not addressed by the majority: Separate equitable or restitution-type claims (implied debt, quasi-contract, unjust enrichment). Only the dissent raises them, and it does so tentatively ("Perhaps").
  • Relied on:
  • Las Animas County High School District v. Raye (broad definition of property).
  • Ellis v. Ellis and In re Marriage of Ellis (factors for what counts as property).
  • Black's Law Dictionary (cited as "one helpful definition": exchangeable value).
  • Carlson v. Carlson and Greer v. Greer (trial court discretion in division).
  • Todd v. Todd and Stern v. Stern (out-of-state support).
  • H. Clark, Domestic Relations § 14.8 (purpose of division).
  • Distinguished:
  • Greer v. Greer: marital property existed, and the award was framed as a property adjustment or alimony in gross; the court did not treat the education as divisible property.
  • Daniels v. Daniels: an alimony case, which differs from property division.
  • Nail v. Nail: addressed professional goodwill, a related issue (with Mueller noted as contra).
  • Overruled: The opinion overrules no case.
  • Same case, two uses: Todd v. Todd appears on both sides. The majority cites it for the point that a law degree is not divisible. The dissent cites it for the point that goodwill reflecting future earning potential may be considered.

  7. Suppose the wife had sought maintenance but earned enough to support herself. Would § 14-10-114, as the dissent reads it, allow an award that reflects her contribution to the husband's education?
  8. The dissent reframes the asset as enhanced earning capacity and points out that tort law compensates its loss. Should the definition of marital property track what the law protects elsewhere, or should it stay tied to transferability and exchange value?
