Black-Letter Rule

A builder whose deviation from the contract is both trivial and innocent may recover the contract price despite less than full performance, less an allowance for the defect. Whether a promise is a condition requiring literal fulfillment turns on considerations of justice and presumed intention, weighing the purpose to be served, the desire to be gratified, the excuse for deviation, and the cruelty of enforced adherence. The allowance is ordinarily the cost of replacement, and it becomes the difference in value when the cost of completion is grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff builder, Jacob & Youngs, built a country residence for defendant owner, Kent, at a cost of upwards of $77,000. It sued for an unpaid balance of $3,483.46.
  • Specification: The plumbing specifications required "standard pipe" of Reading manufacture.
  • Deviation: Some of the installed pipe was made by other factories. The omission resulted from the oversight and inattention of the plaintiff's subcontractor. The evidence sustained a finding that it was neither fraudulent nor willful.
  • Indistinguishable pipe: Reading pipe differs from other brands only by the manufacturer's name stamped on it every six to seven feet. The defendant's architect inspected the pipe on arrival and failed to notice the discrepancy.
  • Timing: Construction ceased in June 1914 and the defendant moved in. No one complained about performance until March 1915.
  • Demand to redo: The architect directed the plaintiff to redo the work. By then the plumbing was encased within the walls except in a few places. Compliance would have meant demolishing substantial parts of the completed structure at great expense.
  • Refusal and suit: The plaintiff left the work untouched and asked for the certificate that final payment was due. The certificate was refused, and the plaintiff sued.
  • Excluded evidence: The plaintiff offered to prove that the installed brands matched Reading pipe in quality, appearance, market value, and cost. The trial court excluded the evidence.

Procedural Posture

The trial court excluded the plaintiff's evidence of equivalence and directed a verdict for the defendant. The Appellate Division reversed and granted a new trial, and the defendant appealed to the Court of Appeals.

Issue

Can a builder whose deviation from a construction specification was trivial and innocent recover the contract balance, subject to an allowance for the defect, so that the trial court erred in excluding evidence that the deviation was insignificant and in directing a verdict for the owner?

Holding

Yes. An omission that is both trivial and innocent does not necessarily breach a condition causing forfeiture, and here the allowance is measured by the difference in value because replacement cost would be grossly disproportionate. The Appellate Division's order was affirmed and judgment absolute was directed for the plaintiff on the stipulation, with costs in all courts (Cardozo, J., joined by Hiscock, Ch. J., Hogan and Crane, JJ.; 4-3).

Reasoning

  • Full performance remains the duty. The court said a contracting party never fills the measure of the duty by less than full performance. A trivial and innocent omission can still sometimes be atoned for by an allowance for damages, so it need not operate as the breach of a condition followed by forfeiture.
  • Promises versus conditions. The court framed the issue in terms of dependent and independent promises. Some promises are plainly independent and some plainly dependent. A third group is dependent, and so conditions, when the departure is substantial, and is treated as independent and collateral when the departure is insignificant.
  • Justice and presumed intention. Classification depends partly on justice and partly on presumable intention. Parties are presumed to contemplate the reasonable and probable. If they intend something else, they must say so. The court will not assume a purpose "to visit venial faults with oppressive retribution."
  • Context matters.
  • The margin of acceptable departure differs between a sale of common chattels and the construction of a mansion or skyscraper.
  • Implying a condition is harsh when the work is united to the land and cannot be surrendered. It is reasonable when defective goods can be returned.
  • Substituting equivalents may matter more in fields of art than in fields of mere utility.
  • Liberal view over strict certainty. The court acknowledged that consistency and certainty favor a stricter standard. It held that New York decisions commit the state to the liberal view, which weighs equity and fairness more heavily.
  • No formula; a question of degree. The line between important and trivial cannot be settled by formula. It is a question of degree for the triers of fact if there is doubt, and for the judges if the inferences are certain.
  • Outer limits.
  • No change is tolerated if it is so dominant or pervasive that it frustrates the purpose of the contract in any real or substantial measure.
  • There is no general license to install whatever the builder thinks is "just as good."
  • Four factors. The court weighs the purpose to be served, the desire to be gratified, the excuse for deviation from the letter, and the cruelty of enforced adherence. Only after that weighing can it decide whether the law implies literal fulfillment as a condition.
  • Willfulness bars relief. A willful transgressor must accept the penalty, and the rigor of implied conditions is not mitigated for that party. A transgressor whose default is unintentional and trivial may hope for mercy if it offers atonement.
  • Measure of the allowance.
  • Cost of replacement is the usual measure, because the owner is entitled to the money that will permit completion.
  • The measure shifts to the difference in value when completion cost is grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained.
  • Here replacement cost would be great and the difference in value would be nominal or nothing.
  • The court illustrated with Vermont granite specified and New Hampshire granite of the same quality installed by a subcontractor's blunder. The allowance in that case would not be the cost of reconstruction.
  • Remedy as an instrument of justice. The substantial-performance rule was developed as an instrument of justice. The measure of the allowance must be shaped to the same end.
  • Offer of proof. Because the trial court excluded the evidence of insignificance, the plaintiff had no occasion to make an offer of proof on the allowance. An offer directed to difference in value would not necessarily have been defective.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • McLaughlin, J., dissenting (joined by Pound and Andrews, JJ.):
  • Bad faith. The plaintiff did not perform. Its failure was either intentional or due to gross neglect, which on the uncontradicted facts amounted to the same thing.
  • Scale of the deviation. The contract called for 2,000 to 2,500 feet of Reading pipe. Only about two-fifths, or 1,000 feet at most, was shown to comply. After the first delivery, no one examined the pipe. Of about 900 exposed feet later uncovered, all but 70 feet came from other manufacturers.
  • Burden of proof. The plaintiff bore the burden of proving that more compliant pipe was used. It gave no explanation for the substitution and offered no proof of the cost of compliance where compliance was possible.
  • Good faith governs. Substantial performance depends in no small degree on the contractor's good faith. Recovery less damages is available for minor omissions due to inadvertence, citing Woodward v. Fuller and Nolan v. Whitney, and this case did not fit that category.
  • Owner's right to get what was bargained for. The owner's reason for wanting Reading pipe, even a mere whim, is irrelevant. Payment was conditioned on that pipe, and "just as good" is no answer. The dissent quoted Smith v. Brady: the specifications are the law between the parties, and a contrary rule would make a new contract and encourage breach.
  • Disposition. The dissent would have reversed the Appellate Division and affirmed the trial court's directed verdict.

Notes

  • Expressly left open: Parties may, by "apt and certain words," make performance of every term a condition of recovery. The court said "That question is not here." Its rule applies when the parties are silent.
  • Limits of the holding:
  • Relief requires a default that is both unintentional and trivial.
  • The willful transgressor gets no mitigation.
  • Deviations that frustrate the contract's purpose in any real or substantial measure are never tolerated.
  • Damages caveat: The court noted that some exposed sections might have been replaced at moderate expense. The defendant did not limit his demand to those sections and treated the plumbing as a unit to be corrected throughout.
  • Factual dispute between the opinions: The majority treats the evidence as sustaining a finding of innocent oversight. The dissent characterizes the same conduct as intentional or gross neglect and stresses the quantity of nonconforming pipe.
  • Precedents relied on:
  • Spence v. Ham, 163 N.Y. 220: a trivial and innocent omission may be atoned for by damages; cost of replacement is the usual measure; the unintentional transgressor may hope for mercy.
  • Woodward v. Fuller, 80 N.Y. 312; Glacius v. Black, 67 N.Y. 563; Bowen v. Kimbell, 203 Mass. 364: support for substantial performance with an allowance.
  • Rosenthal Paper Co. v. Nat. Folding Box & Paper Co., 226 N.Y. 313; Bogardus v. N.Y. Life Ins. Co., 101 N.Y. 328: some promises are plainly independent.
  • Eastern Forge Co. v. Corbin, 182 Mass. 590; Robinson v. Mollett, L.R. 7 Eng. & Ir. App. 802; Miller v. Benjamin, 142 N.Y. 613: dependent promises are treated as independent when the departure is insignificant.
  • Dakin & Co. v. Lee, [1916] 1 K.B. 566: the liberal view is spreading to jurisdictions slow to welcome it.
  • Crouch v. Gutmann, 134 N.Y. 45: no tolerance for deviations that frustrate the purpose; doubtful questions of degree go to the triers of fact.
  • Easthampton L. & C. Co. v. Worthington, 186 N.Y. 407: no general license to install "just as good"; judges decide when the inferences are certain.
  • Schultze v. Goodstein, 180 N.Y. 248; Desmond-Dunne Co. v. Friedman-Doscher Co., 162 N.Y. 486: the willful transgressor must accept the penalty.
  • Handy v. Bliss, 204 Mass. 513 (quoted), with cf. cites to Foeller v. Heintz, 137 Wis. 169, and Oberlies v. Bullinger, 132 N.Y. 598: omissions that cannot be cured without taking down the building may barely affect its value.
  • Secondary sources: Anson on Contracts (Corbin's ed.) § 367; 2 Williston on Contracts §§ 805, 841, 842.
  • Overruled or distinguished: The majority does not expressly overrule or distinguish any precedent. The dissent relies on Smith v. Brady, 17 N.Y. 173, and also cites Spence v. Ham and Crouch v. Gutmann for its view that the substantial-performance rule does not apply here.
