Black-Letter Rule

A warranty of habitability, measured by the standards of the D.C. Housing Regulations, is implied by operation of law into leases of urban dwelling units covered by those Regulations. The warranty cannot be waived, and breach gives the tenant the usual contract remedies. Because the tenant's duty to pay rent depends on the landlord's performance, the tenant may raise housing code violations as a defense in an eviction for nonpayment.

Facts

  • Parties: Tenants (appellants) each rented an apartment under separate written leases in Clifton Terrace, a three-building complex in Northwest Washington. First National Realty Corp. was the landlord.
  • Nonpayment: The tenants admitted they did not pay April rent. The landlord sued for possession only, not a money judgment.
  • Defense: The tenants alleged about 1,500 Housing Regulations violations in the complex, some affecting their units directly and others indirectly. They raised these as an equitable defense or recoupment/set-off equal to the rent claimed.
  • Timing of violations: The tenants conceded the offer of proof covered only violations that arose after the lease term began. There was no allegation that the apartments were in poor condition or violated the code when the leases started.
  • Rent escrow: The tenants offered to pay rent into the court registry while the case was pending.

Procedural Posture

The Landlord and Tenant Branch of the D.C. Court of General Sessions excluded the tenants' proof of code violations and entered judgment for the landlord. The D.C. Court of Appeals affirmed in Saunders v. First National Realty Corp., rejecting the argument that the landlord had a contractual duty to comply with the Housing Regulations. The D.C. Circuit granted leave to appeal.

Issue

Do housing code violations arising during the lease term affect the tenant's obligation to pay rent, so that the tenant may prove them as a defense to an eviction for nonpayment?

Holding

Yes. A warranty of habitability measured by the Housing Regulations is implied into leases of covered urban dwellings, the tenant's rent obligation depends on the landlord's performance of that warranty, and the tenant must be allowed to prove violations. Reversed and remanded (opinion by Judge J. Skelly Wright; Judge Robb concurred only in the result and in Parts IV-B and V; the opinion does not state a vote count).

Reasoning

  • Lease as contract (Part II): The common law treated a lease as a conveyance of an interest in land, a view rooted in feudal, agrarian conditions. The urban apartment dweller wants a "package of goods and services": heat, light, ventilation, plumbing, secure doors and windows, sanitation, and maintenance. Leases of urban dwelling units should be "interpreted and construed like any other contract."
  • Implied warranties elsewhere (Part III): Contract law implies warranties of fitness and merchantability in sales of goods, chattel rentals, and services, and courts have begun applying implied warranties to new home sales. The court found no convincing reason to keep leases outside this trend.
  • Part IV-A, common law ground. Three separate considerations compel abandoning the no-repair rule:
  • Outdated factual assumptions. The rule assumed the land mattered most and that a "jack-of-all-trades" tenant farmer could make repairs. Urban tenants care only about "a house suitable for occupation." They lack repair skills, are mobile, live in complex buildings where repairs need landlord-controlled equipment and areas, and cannot finance major repairs.
  • Consumer protection principles. The landlord sells housing as a commercial business and has greater opportunity, incentive, and capacity to inspect and maintain. The tenant relies on the landlord much like a car buyer relies on the manufacturer (Henningsen). The tenant may legitimately expect the unit to stay fit for the lease term.
  • Urban housing market. Unequal bargaining power, discrimination, standardized form leases, a housing shortage, and the social costs of slums all support protecting tenant expectations. Caveat emptor is rejected because tenants cannot inspect hidden defects and, given the shortage, may have no real choice but to accept obvious ones.
  • Part IV-B, housing code ground (independent). Even apart from the common law, the Housing Regulations require implying the warranty:
  • The Regulations set detailed standards, assign duties to lessor or lessee, and impose inspections and criminal penalties. They are silent on private remedies.
  • Prior D.C. Circuit cases (Whetzel, Kanelos) held the code imposes a repair duty on landlords enforceable in tort. Brown v. Southall Realty held a lease of premises in known code violation at the outset void as an illegal contract.
  • Section 2501 expressly covers maintenance during the lease. If serious noncompliance at the start voids the lease, the section must also affect the contract after signing.
  • Following Schiro v. W.E. Gould, existing law is read into the contract. Footnote 56 concedes this may have little factual support in these parties' actual intent, but warranties are implied by operation of law as public policy.
  • Duties the code places on the lessor cannot be waived or shifted by agreement. Official enforcement has been ineffective. Under Pines v. Perssion, keeping the no-warranty rule would be inconsistent with legislative housing policy.
  • Part V, remedy. Under contract principles, the rent obligation depends on the landlord's performance. The fact-finder makes two findings:
  • Whether the alleged violations existed during the period for which rent is claimed.
  • What portion of the rent obligation, if any or all, the breach suspended.
  • Outcomes under Part V:
  • No suspension: judgment for possession issues.
  • Total suspension: the possession action for nonpayment fails.
  • Partial suspension: no possession judgment if the tenant pays the partial rent found due. Possession may be entered if the tenant refuses.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Judge Robb: Concurred in the result and joined only Parts IV-B (housing code ground) and V (remedy). He did not join the common law reasoning in Parts II, III, and IV-A. The text contains no written separate opinion.

Notes

  • Scope: The holding covers leases of urban dwelling units covered by the D.C. Housing Regulations. The court said the agrarian conveyance model "may continue to be reasonable" for some farming or commercial leases.
  • Oral and written leases: The warranty applies to oral and written leases for all types of tenancies (fn. 29).
  • No waiver: Lease repair provisions are irrelevant because the warranty cannot be excluded (fn. 49). Any agreement shifting code duties placed on the lessor is illegal and unenforceable (fn. 58).
  • Contract law between the parties only: Contract law governs the parties' rights and obligations as between themselves. The court intended no change to "real property" definitions for recordation, descent, conveyancing, or creditors' rights (fn. 13).
  • Relevant violations: Violations must affect the tenant's unit or common areas the tenant uses (fn. 62).
  • The landlord may defend by proving the tenant wrongfully caused the damage.
  • Disintegration from normal use is not assignable to the tenant.
  • No official inspection or finding is required (Diamond Housing Corp. v. Robinson).
  • De minimis: One or two minor violations that do not affect habitability do not reduce rent (fn. 63).
  • Limits on the holding: Rent becomes due again once repairs are made. The holding affects only eviction for nonpayment. The landlord may still evict at lease termination or on any other legal ground (fn. 64).
  • Remedies: All contract remedies are available, including specific performance of the warranty (fn. 61).
  • Escrow: The trial court may require the tenant to pay future rent into the court registry while in possession, with apportionment after trial. Either party may amend before trial to allege a change in the unit's condition (fn. 67).
  • Expressly left open: Validity of the lease clause waiving the statutory 30-day notice to quit (fn. 2). The tenants have not yet claimed money damages, and Rule 4(c) bars a counterclaim when the landlord seeks only possession (fn. 3).
  • Overruled: Bowles v. Mahoney was effectively overruled by Kanelos and Whetzel on the basis of the housing code (fn. 52).
  • Abandoned: The common law no-repair rule (traced to the Middle Ages; see Paradine v. Jane for the related rule requiring rent after the building is destroyed).
  • Relied on, landlord-tenant: Lemle v. Breeden (Haw.) and Reste Realty Corp. v. Cooper (N.J.), described as "recent and well reasoned." Also Pines v. Perssion (Wis.).
  • Relied on, D.C. housing code cases: Whetzel v. Jess Fisher Management Co., Altz v. Lieberson (Cardozo), Kanelos v. Kettler, Brown v. Southall Realty Co., Edwards v. Habib.
  • Relied on, contract and products cases: Schiro v. W.E. Gould & Co. and Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors.
  • Earlier exceptions to the no-repair rule: Graves v. Berdan (upper-story tenant not liable for rent after fire) and Ingalls v. Hobbs (short-term furnished dwelling). The court also cited quiet enjoyment and constructive eviction cases.
  • Criticized: Kearse v. Spaulding, Susskind v. 1136 Tenants Corp., and Rubinger v. Del Monte refused to imply warranties into apartment leases. The court said they offered "no convincing explanation" and relied without discussion on the old rule.
