Black-Letter Rule

Under California's Uniform Parentage Act, proof of genetic consanguinity (through blood testing) and proof of having given birth are both means of establishing a mother and child relationship, and California recognizes only one natural mother per child. When the two means do not coincide in one woman, the natural mother is the woman who intended to procreate the child, meaning she who intended to bring about the birth of a child she intended to raise as her own. A gestational surrogacy contract is not, on its face, inconsistent with public policy, and declaring the gestational surrogate not to be the legal mother does not violate the state or federal Constitution.

Facts

  • Parties: Mark and Crispina Calvert, a married couple, wanted a child. Crispina had a hysterectomy, but her ovaries could still produce eggs. Anna Johnson offered to act as their surrogate.
  • Contract: All three signed a contract providing that an embryo made from Mark's sperm and Crispina's egg would be implanted in Anna. The child would be taken into the Calverts' home "as their child," and Anna would relinquish "all parental rights."
  • Payment: The Calverts agreed to pay Anna $10,000 in installments, the last due six weeks after birth, and to pay for a $200,000 life insurance policy on her life.
  • Breakdown: Relations deteriorated during the pregnancy. Anna sent a letter demanding the balance of her payments or she would refuse to give up the child. Both sides then sued for declarations of parentage.
  • Genetics: Blood tests after the birth excluded Anna as the genetic mother. At trial the parties stipulated that Mark and Crispina were the genetic parents.
  • Anna's background: Anna was a licensed vocational nurse who had done well in school and had previously borne a child. The court relied on these facts to find she could make an informed decision to enter the contract.
  • Child's home: By agreement, the child lived with the Calverts after birth, and Anna had visitation.

Procedural Posture

The trial court ruled that Mark and Crispina were the child's "genetic, biological and natural" parents, that Anna had no parental rights, and that the surrogacy contract was legal and enforceable against Anna's claims. It also terminated Anna's visitation. The Court of Appeal affirmed, and the California Supreme Court granted review.

Issue

Under California's Uniform Parentage Act, when a married couple's embryo is gestated and delivered by another woman under a surrogacy agreement, is the genetic mother who intended to raise the child the child's natural mother?

Holding

Yes. When genetic consanguinity and giving birth are split between two women, the woman who intended to procreate and raise the child is the natural mother. That result does not offend the Constitution or public policy. The Court of Appeal's judgment was affirmed in an opinion by Panelli, J., joined by Lucas, C.J., and Mosk, Baxter, and George, JJ. Arabian, J., concurred separately, and Kennard, J., dissented.

Reasoning

  • The UPA governs. The Act was passed in 1975 to abolish the legitimate/illegitimate distinction and was not written with surrogacy in mind. It still facially applies to any parentage determination. The court declined the invitation to decide the case on constitutional or policy grounds outside the Act.
  • It relied on People v. Salemme for the principle that statutes are often properly applied to facts the legislature did not contemplate.
  • Both women qualify under the Act.
  • Civ. Code § 7003(1) lets a natural mother establish the relationship "by proof of her having given birth to the child, or under [the Act]."
  • Under § 7015, the paternity provisions apply to maternity "insofar as practicable." Blood-test evidence can therefore establish maternity, through Evid. Code §§ 892, 895, and 895.5.
  • Anna proved she gave birth, and Crispina proved a genetic relationship.
  • The § 7004 presumptions don't apply. Those presumptions help identify a father when the evidence points to a particular man. Here each claimant's identity and factual basis were obvious, so the question was purely legal.
  • The statute shows no preference. "May" and the disjunctive "or" in § 7003 make birth and blood-test evidence alternative methods. The Act does not resolve which controls when they conflict.
  • Intent breaks the tie.
  • The Calverts affirmatively intended the child's birth and took the steps to cause it. But for their acted-on intention, the child would not exist.
  • The parties aimed to bring the Calverts' child into the world. They did not set out to donate a zygote to Anna.
  • Anna would not have been given the chance to gestate the child had she declared, before implantation, an intent to be its mother. Her later change of heart does not undo Crispina's status.
  • Support from commentators. The court relied on three sources:
  • Hill: intended parents are the "first cause, or the prime movers" of the procreative relationship.
  • Shultz: "voluntarily chosen, deliberate, express and bargained-for" intentions should presumptively determine parenthood in assisted reproduction.
  • A Yale L.J. note: the originators of the "mental concept" of the child merit credit as conceivers.
  • Child welfare and certainty.
  • Children's interests are unlikely to run against those of the adults who chose to bring them into being.
  • Anna's rule would make a gestator the mother, against her expectations, if the intended mother refused the child.
  • An intent rule promotes certainty and stability for the child.
  • Public policy: the contract can be considered.
  • Vetoed legislation. S.B. 937 found surrogacy contracts consistent with public policy, but the Governor vetoed it with reservations. The court gave the bill no weight either way.
  • Adoption statutes. Penal Code § 273 bars paying a parent for consent to adoption. Gestational surrogacy differs from adoption, so the statute and the adoption laws don't apply:
  • Anna agreed before conception, so she was not vulnerable to financial pressure to give up her own expected child.
  • She was not the genetic mother.
  • The payments compensated her for gestation and labor, and installments were due both during the pregnancy and after birth.
  • Termination of parental rights. The same reasons mean the contract doesn't implicate the policies behind termination statutes (Welf. & Inst. Code § 202).
  • Involuntary servitude. The court found no potential for involuntary servitude and no evidence of coercion or duress (Pollock v. Williams).
  • Exploitation and commodification. The court found no proof that surrogacy exploits poor women more than economic necessity generally does. It found no evidence that surrogacy fosters treating children as commodities.
  • Women's capacity to agree. The argument that women cannot knowingly agree to gestate echoes reasoning that long denied women equal economic and professional status.
  • Proper forum. The court acknowledged that the Legislature is the proper forum to study these issues.
  • Constitution.
  • Procedural due process. Anna identified no defect in the notice or hearing she received.
  • Equal protection. No such claim was made. A gestator who agrees to carry a couple's genetic child is not similarly situated to the wife who provides the ovum intending to raise the child.
  • Substantive due process. Anna relied on the unwed-father cases (Stanley, Quilloin, Caban, Lehr). The court said those cases don't support rights for a gestational surrogate. Language in Lehr and Adoption of Kelsey S. actually stresses the importance of the genetic connection.
  • The threshold question. Anna's argument assumes she is the mother. The real question is whether declaring she is not the mother is constitutional.
  • Michael H. v. Gerald D. This case doesn't help Anna. Tradition does not protect a gestator who carries another couple's zygote, because the arrangement is too new. To the extent tradition matters, it favors the couple forming their own family. Recognizing a liberty interest in Anna would necessarily infringe on Mark's and Crispina's interests.
  • State privacy. California's privacy right is broader than the federal right (Committee to Defend Reproductive Rights v. Myers, compared with Harris v. McRae), but it still doesn't cover Anna's claim. A gestational surrogate is not making her own procreative choice. She agrees to provide a service with no expectation of raising the child.
  • Donor analogy. The Calverts were not genetic "donors." Civ. Code § 7005 (artificial insemination) does not apply.
  • Legislative silence on IVF. Silence does not mean the Legislature disapproved of IVF. Courts should not restrict reproductive technology when the Legislature has not done so.
  • Rejection of the dissent's best-interests test (footnote).
  • It invites governmental interference in private matters.
  • It confuses parentage with custody. Parentage must come first and should not be dictated by custody outcomes.
  • The dependency laws already protect children.
  • It breeds instability during litigation and could split custody between the father and the gestator.
  • By contracting away her rights, the gestator arguably concedes that the child's best interests are not with her.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Arabian, J. (concurring): Joins the holding that Crispina is the natural mother under the UPA, and says that holding resolves the case. Refuses to join the majority's public-policy discussion, which he calls dictum. Surrogacy goes beyond contract principles and needs nonadversarial, legislative study. He quotes Satz v. Perlmutter (Fla.) and Matter of Conroy (N.J.) on why legislatures are better suited to such questions. The Legislature's failure to act should "chasten" the court, not embolden it.
  • Kennard, J. (dissenting):
  • The statute. Both women have substantial and equal claims under the UPA, and the Act provides no tiebreaker. Intent is not found in the statute.
  • The majority's four rationales:
  • But-for causation fails because the gestator is equally a but-for and substantial-factor cause.
  • The "originators of the concept" rationale is borrowed from intellectual property, and children are not property.
  • Bargained-for expectations invoke contract law, but courts don't specifically enforce personal service contracts, still less the delivery of a child.
  • The child-welfare rationale has merit but doesn't support a rigid rule.
  • Devaluing gestation. The intent rule ignores the gestator's physical, psychological, and emotional commitment. She cites Lehr's statement that a mother's parental relationship is clear because she "carries and bears the child," and Burgess v. Superior Court.
  • Missing safeguards. The rule validates unregulated surrogacy without the protections in the model Uniform Status of Children of Assisted Conception Act: court approval before conception, counsel, a showing of medical need, counseling, and fitness standards.
  • Proposed test. She would decide maternity by the best interests of the child, noting that the UPA itself uses that standard elsewhere (§ 7017(d)(2)).
  • Disposition. She would reverse and remand for a best-interests determination, and urges legislative action.
  • Baby M. She distinguishes Matter of Baby M. as a traditional surrogacy case, where the birth mother was also the genetic mother.

Notes

  • Scope: The tiebreaker applies only when genetics and gestation are split between two women. The opinion addresses a married couple whose own gametes formed the embryo.
  • Egg donation (footnote): The court states that under its analysis, in a true egg donation case, the birth mother who intends to raise the child is the natural mother.
  • Two mothers rejected (footnote): The court declined the ACLU's request to recognize two mothers. Doing so here would diminish Crispina's role, and the court saw no compelling reason for it.
  • Left open:
  • The validity of a surrogacy contract purporting to deprive the gestator of her freedom to terminate the pregnancy. This contract acknowledged her absolute right to choose, so the court did not reach the question.
  • The broader surrogacy questions, which the court said belong with the Legislature.
  • Public-policy discussion: The majority found the contract "not, on its face, inconsistent with public policy." Arabian, J., treats that discussion as dictum.
  • Court of Appeal reasoning disapproved (footnote): The Court of Appeal read § 7003(1) to require a woman to first prove genetic "natural" motherhood before birth counts. The Supreme Court rejected that reading. "Natural" in § 7003 just means non-adoptive.
  • Statutory update (footnote): Civ. Code §§ 7000-7021 and the cited Evidence Code provisions were repealed effective January 1, 1994 and replaced with equivalent Family Code provisions (Fam. Code §§ 7600-7650; 7500-7501, 7550-7557).
  • Precedents as the opinion uses them:
  • People v. Salemme: relied on. Statutes apply to facts the legislature did not foresee.
  • Levy v. Louisiana; Glona v. American Guarantee Co.: background for the UPA's purpose of equal treatment regardless of legitimacy.
  • Stanley, Quilloin, Caban, Lehr; Santosky, Lassiter, Smith v. Organization of Foster Families: cited by Anna. Held not to support rights for a gestational surrogate.
  • Lehr; Adoption of Kelsey S.: relied on for the importance of the genetic connection.
  • Michael H. v. Gerald D.: distinguished. Tradition does not protect gestational surrogates.
  • Committee to Defend Reproductive Rights v. Myers; Harris v. McRae: show the state privacy right is broader, but it still doesn't reach Anna's claim.
  • Pollock v. Williams: involuntary servitude arises in cases of criminal punishment for refusal to work, and nothing like that is present here.
  • Smith v. Organization of Foster Families (footnote): cited in describing the trial court's analogy of Anna to a foster mother.
  • Statutes held inapplicable:
  • Civ. Code § 7005 (artificial insemination).
  • Civ. Code § 7004 paternity presumptions.
  • Penal Code § 273 and the adoption statutes.
