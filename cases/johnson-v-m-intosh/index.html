Black-Letter Rule

Under the discovery principle adopted by the United States, the discovering European sovereign, and the United States as its successor, holds the ultimate title to land in America, subject only to the Indian right of occupancy, and holds the exclusive right to extinguish that occupancy by purchase or conquest. Indian tribes may not convey the absolute title to private individuals, so a title resting solely on a direct purchase from a tribe cannot be sustained in U.S. courts.

Facts

  • Plaintiffs' claim: Plaintiffs claimed the land under two grants, one made in 1773 and one in 1775, by chiefs of certain tribes of the Illinois and Piankeshaw nations.
  • Agreed facts: The case agreed showed that the chiefs had authority from their own people to convey, and that their tribes were in rightful possession of the land they sold.
  • Location: The land lay within Virginia's chartered limits. Virginia later ceded the territory northwest of the Ohio River to the United States.
  • Royal Proclamation of 1763: The crown reserved the lands west of the sources of the rivers flowing into the Atlantic for the use of the Indians and forbade British subjects from purchasing or settling them.
  • Virginia Act of 1779: Virginia declared its exclusive right of pre-emption from the Indians within its chartered limits and purported to annul deeds made by Indians to individuals.
  • Later cession: The Illinois and Piankeshaw nations, after war with the United States, ceded the country containing the disputed land to the United States by treaty, without reserving the plaintiffs' title. They had used the land in common with their other lands from the date of the deeds until the cession.

Procedural Posture

The district court of Illinois rendered judgment against the plaintiffs. They sought review in the Supreme Court.

Issue

Can a title to land acquired by private individuals through a direct purchase from Indian tribes be recognized in the courts of the United States?

Holding

No. Discovery vested the ultimate title and the exclusive right to extinguish Indian occupancy in the discovering sovereign and its successor, so the tribes could not convey a title the courts will sustain, and the judgment was affirmed with costs (opinion by Chief Justice Marshall; vote not stated).

Reasoning

  • Title depends on the law of the sovereign. Title to land depends entirely on the law of the nation where it lies. The Court therefore examined the principles "our own government has adopted," in addition to abstract justice.
  • The discovery principle. To avoid conflict among themselves, European nations agreed that discovery gave title to the discovering government against all other Europeans, which title possession could consummate. Discovery gave that nation the sole right to acquire the soil from the natives.
  • What Indian rights survived. The tribes were "the rightful occupants of the soil," with a legal and just claim to possess and use it. Their sovereignty was diminished, and their power to dispose of the soil to whomever they pleased was denied.
  • Universal practice. Spain, Portugal, France, Holland, and especially England all claimed and granted land by discovery while it was occupied by Indians. Cabot's 1496 commission, and the Gilbert and Raleigh charters, authorized discovering and taking possession of lands not already possessed by Christian people. Later crown grants (the 1606 and 1609 Virginia charters, the Plymouth Company, the Duke of York, the Carolinas) were made while the Indians occupied the land and "purport to convey the soil as well as the right of dominion." These patents "cannot be considered as nullities," and the Court noted that the Plymouth Company's grants, and in some instances the proprietors' title to the soil, were respected.
  • Treaties among nations. By the treaty of Utrecht, France ceded Nova Scotia or Acadie to Great Britain, a great part of which was in Indian possession. The treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle did not resolve that controversy, but the commissioners' arguments showed that each side relied on the title discovery gave to lands still in Indian possession. In the treaty of Paris (1763), France ceded the whole country on the English side of the dividing line, though much of it was occupied by Indians, and Spain ceded Florida, great part of which was in Indian possession. France ceded Louisiana to Spain, which later retroceded it; it was occupied chiefly by Indians at both times. From this history the Court concluded that all European nations asserted and recognized the discoverer's exclusive right to appropriate lands occupied by Indians.
  • The United States adopted the principle. By the Revolutionary treaty, Britain's government powers and right to the soil passed to the states. The states and the United States took clear title subject only to Indian occupancy. Virginia's 1779 act and its land office for Kentucky affirmed that the exclusive right to purchase from the Indians lay in the government. State cessions to the Union conveyed the soil as well as jurisdiction.
  • Absolute title is exclusive. "An absolute title to lands cannot exist, at the same time, in different persons, or in different governments." Recognizing the crown's absolute title, subject to occupancy, is incompatible with complete title in the Indians.
  • Conquest and the limits of judicial review. "Conquest gives a title which the courts of the conqueror cannot deny." The British claims were established by the sword, and title to much of the land held in the country originates in them. Courts may not question that title or sustain one incompatible with it.
  • Why the ordinary law of conquest did not apply. Normally the conquered keep their property and are incorporated into the conquering nation. The Court reasoned that incorporation was impracticable here, so a different rule was unavoidable. However extravagant the pretension of converting discovery into conquest, if that principle was asserted, sustained, and became the source of most property titles, "it becomes the law of the land, and cannot be questioned." The concomitant principle, that Indians are mere occupants incapable of transferring the absolute title, may be "opposed to natural right," but if indispensable to the system under which the country was settled, it "certainly cannot be rejected by courts of justice."
  • Alternative ground: the purchaser holds under tribal law. Even if an individual could buy the Indian title, he acquires only that title. He "incorporates himself" with the tribe as to that land and holds it under their laws. If the tribe annuls the grant, no U.S. court can set the annulment aside. Here the tribes ceded the land to the United States without reserving the plaintiffs' title, a "fair presumption" that they treated the grants as void.
  • The 1763 Proclamation as an additional objection. All vacant lands vested in the crown under the British constitution, and the crown could grant them or reserve them for the Indians. The prerogative over relations with foreign peoples included restraining encroachments on Indian lands. The proclamation's authority on this continent "has never been denied."

Notes

  • Indian title is a real property interest. "It has never been contended, that the Indian title amounted to nothing. Their right of possession has never been questioned." The government's claim is to the ultimate title "charged with this right of possession."
  • The Court disclaimed a moral defense. It refused to decide whether agriculturists may, on abstract principles, expel hunters. It did "not mean to engage in the defence of" the European principles. Its holding rests on what U.S. courts may recognize given the established system of title.
  • Virginia's 1779 act. The Court relied on it only as an affirmance of the general principle. It expressly did not ascribe to the act "the power of annulling vested rights." It also left aside the marginal note in the statutory revisals stating that the prohibition had been repealed.
  • Crown confirmation can cure. The Rhode Island charter and the related royal letter "sanction a previous unauthorized purchase from Indians, under the circumstances attending that particular purchase." They do not show that an Indian title is valid against a crown title or without crown confirmation.
  • Eastern-state titles under Indian deeds. Titles held under the Indians in the eastern states were "sanctioned by length of possession," but the Court knew of no judicial decision in their favor.
  • *Relied on: Fletcher v. Peck.* That case held the Indian title, though entitled to respect until legitimately extinguished, was not "absolutely repugnant to a seisin in fee" in the state. The Court said this conforms to the discovery principle. It compared the Indian right to a lease for years: compatible with seisin in fee, yet able to bar an ejectment.
  • *Distinguished: Campbell v. Hall (Cowper).* That case held illegal the part of a proclamation imposing a tax on a conquered province after it had received a government. The Court accepted the decision but held it inapplicable. Taxing by proclamation is not a royal prerogative, while granting vacant lands and restraining encroachments on Indians always have been.
  • Distinguished: the Connecticut–Mohegan controversy. That dispute turned on the scope of a grant and reservations to the colony. It did not assert that individuals could obtain a complete title from the Indians.
  • Distinguished: Pratt and Yorke opinion. The law officers' opinion that Indian grants needed no royal patent likely concerned East Indian "princes or governments" and the question whether subjects carry the common law with them. It was therefore inapplicable to purchases in America.
  • Colonial prohibitions on purchases. Plaintiffs argued these showed Indian deeds were otherwise valid. The Court called that inference "at most, equivocal" and read the acts as evidence that such purchases opposed sound policy.
