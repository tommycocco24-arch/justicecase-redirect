Black-Letter Rule

A criminal defendant has no constitutional right to compel appointed appellate counsel to raise every nonfrivolous issue the defendant requests, if counsel, as a matter of professional judgment, decides not to present those points. The accused keeps ultimate authority over certain fundamental decisions: whether to plead guilty, waive a jury, testify in their own behalf, or take an appeal. Outside those decisions, strategic and tactical choices such as which issues to press on appeal belong to counsel, after consultation with the client.

Facts

  • The crime: Richard Butts was robbed at knifepoint and beaten by four men. Butts told a detective he recognized one attacker as "Froggy." The next day police arrested respondent David Barnes, who is known as "Froggy."
  • Trial: The prosecution rested mainly on Butts' testimony and identification. The trial judge sua sponte cut off defense questioning about Butts' psychiatric treatment and refused a requested accessorial-liability instruction. The jury convicted Barnes of first- and second-degree robbery and second-degree assault.
  • Appointed appellate counsel: The Appellate Division assigned Michael Melinger. Barnes sent a letter listing claims to raise and a pro se brief. Melinger accepted some claims and rejected most, saying they would not help win a new trial and were not based on record evidence. Melinger listed seven possible issues and invited Barnes' "reflections and suggestions." The record shows no response.
  • The brief: Melinger's brief focused on three of the seven issues (exclusion of psychiatric evidence, failure to suppress the identification, the judge's cross-examination of Barnes). Melinger also submitted Barnes' pro se brief. Barnes later filed two more pro se briefs raising three more of the seven issues. At oral argument Melinger argued only the three issues in counsel's own brief.
  • Assumed insistence: The record was ambiguous about what Barnes requested. The Court assumed, as the Second Circuit found, that Barnes insisted Melinger raise the issues.

Procedural Posture

The Appellate Division affirmed the conviction and the New York Court of Appeals denied leave to appeal; after an unsuccessful first federal habeas petition and further state filings, Barnes brought a second federal habeas petition claiming ineffective assistance of appellate counsel, which the District Court dismissed. A divided Second Circuit panel reversed, holding under Anders v. California that counsel must argue additional colorable points the appellant requests, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does defense counsel assigned to prosecute an appeal from a criminal conviction have a constitutional duty to raise every nonfrivolous issue the defendant requests?

Holding

No; neither Anders nor any other decision gives an indigent defendant a constitutional right to compel appointed counsel to press nonfrivolous points that counsel, in professional judgment, decides not to present, so the Second Circuit's judgment is reversed (opinion by Chief Justice Burger).

Reasoning

  • The Second Circuit's per se rule: The court below held that because Anders bars counsel from abandoning a nonfrivolous appeal, it also bars counsel from abandoning a nonfrivolous issue, and that no showing of likely success was needed. The Court rejected this as a "new per se rule."
  • Framework of defendant's rights: There is "no constitutional right to an appeal," but Griffin v. Illinois and Douglas v. California require that an appeal open to those who can pay be provided to indigents. The accused has ultimate authority over certain fundamental decisions (plead guilty, waive jury, testify, take an appeal), and under Faretta v. California may, with some limits, represent themselves. No decision extends client control to issue selection by appointed counsel.
  • Purpose of appointed counsel: Douglas rested on the superior ability of trained counsel in examining the record, researching the law, and marshalling arguments. A rule letting the client dictate issues undermines counsel's ability to present the case by professional evaluation.
  • Advocacy wisdom on winnowing: Experienced advocates stress cutting weaker arguments and focusing on one or a few key issues. The Court quoted Justice Jackson ("multiplying assignments of error will dilute and weaken a good case and will not save a bad one") and Stern's appellate practice treatise. Strict oral-argument time limits and brief page limits make issue selection more important. Even without such limits, raising every colorable issue risks burying the arguments that "go for the jugular" (John W. Davis).
  • **Anders cuts the other way:* Anders requires counsel to support the client's appeal "to the best of his ability." Melinger did that. Requiring counsel to raise every colorable claim the client suggests would disserve the goal of vigorous, effective advocacy that underlies Anders*.
  • Professional standards (footnotes): Proposed Model Rule 1.2(a) requires the lawyer to abide by the client's decisions on the objectives of representation and on plea, jury waiver, and testifying, and to consult with the client on means. Outside those decisions, counsel takes professional responsibility for the conduct of the case after consulting the client. ABA Standard 21-3.2 seems to say counsel should yield to a client's insistence on an appellate contention, but Defense Function Standard 4-5.2 assigns strategic and tactical decisions to counsel. Either way, an ABA recognition that a practice is desirable does not make it constitutionally required.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Blackmun, concurring in the judgment: Declines to join the majority because the case does not require deciding whether there is a constitutional right to a first appeal, and because, as an ethical matter, Blackmun agrees with Justice Brennan and the ABA that a lawyer should argue all nonfrivolous claims the client insists on, after giving the client the lawyer's best advice. That ideal allocation is not a constitutional requirement where counsel's performance is "within the range of competence demanded of attorneys in criminal cases" (McMann v. Richardson) and gives the indigent defendant "an adequate opportunity to present his claims fairly" (Ross v. Moffitt). Both were met here. Blackmun adds that counsel's refusal cannot foreclose review forever: failing to raise insisted-upon nonfrivolous constitutional claims should constitute "cause and prejudice" for any resulting state procedural default under Wainwright v. Sykes, with habeas as the remedy.
  • Justice Brennan, joined by Justice Marshall, dissenting: The Sixth Amendment right to the "assistance of counsel" includes a personal right of the defendant to decide which nonfrivolous issues to present on appeal, even against counsel's advice. Points:
  • Faretta shows the right to counsel protects the defendant's dignity and autonomy and is more than a right to competent presentation. Anders reflects the same view by requiring a brief so the client may "raise any points that he chooses."
  • The right is not all-or-nothing. Defendants may want to argue innocence, press political arguments, or protect third parties. Counsel should assist within the limits of conscience, law, and duties to the court.
  • ABA Standard 21-3.2's commentary says the decision to press a particular contention on appeal is the client's. The majority wrongly relies on trial standards; trial decisions must be made fast, while appellate issue choices can be made deliberately.
  • The majority's advocacy advice is good, but the Constitution does not require clients to be wise.
  • Indigent clients often mistrust appointed counsel, and lawyers' interests (reputation, flat fees, low appointment fees) may diverge from clients'. The majority's rule deepens that mistrust.
  • The majority overstates how much appellate outcomes turn on presentation; good claims usually win regardless.
  • Would remand for a hearing on whether Barnes actually and timely insisted on briefing the nonfrivolous issues. If not, or if Barnes was content with the pro se brief, there was no deprivation.
  • The statement that there is "no constitutional right to an appeal" was unnecessary and "quite arguably wrong."

Notes

  • Narrow question: The only question decided was whether a criminal defendant has a constitutional right to have appellate counsel raise every nonfrivolous issue the defendant requests. The holding is constitutional. It does not resolve what professional ethics rules require.
  • Expressly left open: Whether federal habeas is available to review claims counsel declined to raise, and whether counsel's refusal to raise requested claims constitutes "cause" for procedural default under Wainwright v. Sykes (citing Engle v. Isaac). Justice Blackmun's concurrence answers that question for himself only.
  • Factual assumption: The Court assumed, without deciding, that Barnes insisted on the issues and did not simply accept counsel's choice. The dissent would have remanded on this point.
  • Relied on: Griffin v. Illinois and Douglas v. California (indigent appeal and counsel); Wainwright v. Sykes (Burger, C.J., concurring) and ABA Standards for the defendant's fundamental decisions; Faretta v. California (self-representation); proposed Model Rule 1.2(a) and ABA Defense Function Standard 4-5.2.
  • Distinguished / read narrowly: Anders v. California. The Court read Anders as barring abandonment of a nonfrivolous appeal and requiring best-effort advocacy, and held it does not extend to every nonfrivolous issue. The Court treated Anders as supporting its result.
  • Discounted: ABA Standard 21-3.2, which respondent cited and which appears to favor client control over appellate contentions. The Court noted that Defense Function Standard 4-5.2 instead assigns strategic and tactical decisions to counsel, and said that in any event an ABA recognition that a practice is desirable does not make it constitutionally required.
  • Overruled: No prior decision of the Court. The Second Circuit's per se rule was rejected.
  • PR takeaway: The opinion separates the constitutional floor from ethical ideals. Blackmun and the dissent both read professional standards as favoring client control over appellate issues; the majority holds those standards do not set the Sixth Amendment's content.
