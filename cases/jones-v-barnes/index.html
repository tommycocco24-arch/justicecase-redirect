Black-Letter Rule

An indigent criminal defendant has no constitutional right to compel appointed appellate counsel to raise every nonfrivolous issue the defendant requests, if counsel, as a matter of professional judgment, decides not to present those points. The accused holds ultimate authority over certain fundamental decisions: whether to plead guilty, waive a jury, testify, or take an appeal. Selecting which issues to press on appeal belongs to counsel's professional judgment after consulting the client.

Facts

  • Crime and arrest: Butts was robbed at knifepoint and beaten by four men. He told a detective he recognized one assailant as "Froggy." The next day police arrested respondent Barnes, known as "Froggy."
  • Trial: The prosecution rested mainly on Butts' testimony and identification. The trial judge sua sponte told Butts not to answer a defense question about psychiatric treatment, and no offer of proof followed. The judge refused a defense-requested instruction on accessorial liability. The jury convicted Barnes of first- and second-degree robbery and second-degree assault.
  • Appointed appellate counsel: The Appellate Division assigned Melinger to the appeal. Barnes sent a letter listing claims to raise, including suppression of the identification, exclusion of psychiatric evidence, and ineffective trial counsel, plus a pro se brief.
  • Counsel's response: Melinger accepted some claims and rejected most, saying they would not help win a new trial and could not be raised because they were not based on the record. He listed seven potential claims and invited Barnes' "reflections and suggestions." The record shows no reply.
  • The briefs: Melinger's brief focused on three of the seven points. He also submitted Barnes' pro se brief. Barnes later filed two more pro se briefs raising three more of the seven issues. At oral argument Melinger argued only his own three points.
  • Assumption on review: The record was ambiguous about what Barnes requested. The Court assumed, as the Second Circuit concluded, that Barnes insisted Melinger raise the issues.

Procedural Posture

After the state appeal and an earlier habeas petition failed, Barnes filed a second federal habeas petition claiming ineffective assistance of appellate counsel, which the District Court dismissed. A divided Second Circuit panel reversed, holding that counsel must argue all colorable points the client requests, and ordered the writ granted unless the State provided new counsel and a new appeal. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does defense counsel assigned to prosecute an appeal from a criminal conviction have a constitutional duty to raise every nonfrivolous issue requested by the defendant?

Holding

No. Neither Anders nor any other decision gives an indigent defendant a constitutional right to compel appointed counsel to press nonfrivolous points that counsel, in professional judgment, decides not to present. Reversed (Chief Justice Burger for the Court).

Reasoning

  • Framework of client authority: There is no constitutional right to an appeal. Under Griffin and Douglas, though, if an appeal is open to those who can pay, it must be provided to indigents. The accused controls certain fundamental decisions (plea, jury waiver, testifying, taking an appeal), citing Wainwright v. Sykes (Burger, C.J., concurring) and ABA Standards. Under Faretta, a defendant may, with some limitations, represent himself. None of these authorities gives the client control over issue selection.
  • Counsel's superior skill: Douglas recognized trained counsel's superior ability in examining the record, researching the law, and marshalling arguments. A per se rule letting the client pick the issues undermines counsel's ability to present the case on professional evaluation.
  • Winnowing is good advocacy: The Court relied heavily on advocacy literature. Justice Jackson: legal contentions "depreciate through over-issue," and multiplying assignments of error "will dilute and weaken a good case and will not save a bad one." Stern's appellate practice treatise and a Second Circuit practice manual say the same.
  • Practical limits: Strict oral argument time limits and brief page limits make issue selection more important. Even without such limits, the Second Circuit's rule "is contrary to all experience and logic" because raising every colorable issue risks burying the arguments that "go for the jugular."
  • **Anders cuts the other way:* Anders requires counsel to support the client's appeal "to the best of his ability." Melinger did that. Forcing counsel to raise every colorable claim would "disserve the very goal of vigorous and effective advocacy that underlies Anders*."
  • Ethics rules are not constitutional mandates (footnote): Proposed Model Rule 1.2(a) has the lawyer abide by the client's decisions on objectives and on plea, jury waiver, and testifying, and consult on means. Outside those fundamental decisions, the attorney takes professional responsibility for the conduct of the case after consulting the client. ABA Defense Function Standard 4-5.2 puts strategic and tactical decisions in counsel's exclusive province. Barnes cited ABA Criminal Appeals Standard 21-3.2, which appears to favor client control over contentions. The Court answered that ABA recognition of a practice as desirable does not make it constitutionally required.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Blackmun, concurring in the judgment: Did not join the opinion because he saw no need to decide whether a constitutional right to a first appeal exists. As an ethical matter, he agreed with Justice Brennan and the ABA that the lawyer should give his best advice and then acquiesce in the client's choice of nonfrivolous claims. That ideal is not constitutional where counsel's performance falls "within the range of competence demanded of attorneys in criminal cases" (McMann v. Richardson) and gives the indigent "an adequate opportunity to present his claims fairly" (Ross v. Moffitt). Both were met here. He added his view, on a question the majority did not reach, that counsel's failure to raise nonfrivolous constitutional claims the client insisted on must constitute "cause and prejudice" for any resulting state procedural default under Wainwright v. Sykes, with habeas as the remedy.
  • Justice Brennan, joined by Justice Marshall, dissenting: The Sixth Amendment right to the "assistance of counsel" includes a personal right of the defendant to decide which nonfrivolous issues to raise on appeal, even against counsel's advice. Faretta holds that counsel exists to protect the defendant's dignity and autonomy by helping him make his own choices. Anders requires a brief so the client may "raise any points that he chooses." The right is not all-or-nothing: clients may want to press innocence, pursue political aims, or protect third parties. ABA Standard 21-3.2 commentary says "Counsel's role, however, is to advise. The decision is made by the client." The majority's reliance on trial-stage Standard 4-5.2 is misplaced because trial decisions are rushed while appellate issue selection can be deliberate. Policy: indigent clients often mistrust appointed lawyers, whose interests (reputation, flat fees) may diverge from theirs, and the majority's rule deepens that mistrust. Appellate judges can spot good claims amid weak ones. Client choices should be respected unless they would require lawyers to violate conscience, the law, or duties to the court. He would remand for a hearing on whether Barnes actually and timely insisted on the issues. He also called the majority's statement that there is no constitutional right to appeal unnecessary and "quite arguably wrong."

Notes

  • Narrow question: The Court framed the only question as whether a defendant has a constitutional right to have appellate counsel raise every nonfrivolous issue requested. The holding is constitutional. It does not settle the ethical allocation of authority, which the Court addressed only to say ABA standards do not set constitutional requirements.
  • Expressly left open: Whether federal habeas is available to review claims counsel declined to raise, and whether counsel's refusal to raise requested claims is "cause" for a procedural default under Wainwright v. Sykes (also citing Engle v. Isaac). Justice Blackmun answered the cause question for himself only.
  • Factual assumption: The Court assumed Barnes insisted on the issues. The dissent would have remanded to find out.
  • Rejected below: The Second Circuit's per se rule, and its holding that an appellant need not show likelihood of success on the omitted claims, were reversed. The Second Circuit also held that Barnes' pro se briefs did not cure the omission. The Supreme Court did not separately address that point.
  • Relied on: Douglas v. California (counsel's superior skill; right to counsel on first appeal as of right); Griffin v. Illinois (equal access to appeal); Wainwright v. Sykes, n.1 (Burger, C.J., concurring) (fundamental decisions belong to the accused); Faretta v. California (self-representation, with limits).
  • Distinguished: Anders v. California. The Second Circuit read Anders' bar on abandoning a nonfrivolous appeal as also barring abandonment of a nonfrivolous issue. The Court read Anders as requiring vigorous advocacy, which counsel provided here.
  • Professional rules discussed: Proposed Model Rule 1.2(a) (Final Draft 1982); ABA Standards 4-5.2 and 21-3.2. The dissent also cites Model Code EC 7-7 and EC 7-8 and notes that Rule 1.2's drafters were chiefly concerned with insurer-insured representation.
  • No precedent overruled.
