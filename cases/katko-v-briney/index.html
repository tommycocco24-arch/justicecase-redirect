Black-Letter Rule

A property owner has no privilege to use force calculated to cause death or serious bodily injury to protect land or chattels unless the intruder also threatens the owner's personal safety enough to justify self-defense. An owner cannot do indirectly by a mechanical device, such as a spring gun, what the owner could not do in person. A spring gun or similar device is justified only against a trespasser committing a felony of violence or a felony punishable by death, or endangering human life. Breaking and entering an unoccupied house to steal does not qualify, and the trespasser's own illegal conduct does not change the rule.

Facts

  • Property: Bertha Briney inherited farmland in 1957 that included an old farmhouse. No one lived there afterward. The Brineys' own home was several miles away.
  • Repeated intrusions: From 1957 to 1967 the house was repeatedly broken into, with household items taken, windows broken, and the property "messed up." The Brineys boarded up windows and doors and posted "no trespass" signs. The nearest sign was 35 feet from the house.
  • The trap: On June 11, 1967, the Brineys wired a 20-gauge shotgun to an iron bed in the north bedroom so it would fire when the door opened. Mr. Briney first aimed it at stomach height and then, at Mrs. Briney's suggestion, lowered it to hit the legs. He said he acted "because I was mad and tired of being tormented" but did not intend to injure anyone. He offered no explanation for using a loaded shell aimed at a person already inside the house. The gun could not be seen from outside, and no warning was posted.
  • Plaintiff's entry: Katko and a companion had taken old bottles and fruit jars from the house on an earlier trip. On July 16, 1967, around 9:30 p.m., they returned and entered by removing a board from a porch window. Katko knew the house had long been uninhabited and that he had no right to enter and steal.
  • Injury: When Katko opened the bedroom door, the gun fired and blew away much of his right leg, including part of the tibia. He spent 40 days in the hospital, wore a cast for about a year and a brace for another year, and has a permanent deformity and a shortened leg.
  • Criminal case: Katko pleaded guilty to larceny in the nighttime of property worth less than $20. He was fined $50 and paroled from a 60-day sentence.

Procedural Posture

At the Brineys' request, a jury of local residents tried Katko's damages action and returned a verdict of $20,000 actual and $10,000 punitive damages. The trial court denied the Brineys' motions for judgment notwithstanding the verdict and for a new trial, and the Brineys appealed.

Issue

May an owner use a spring gun capable of inflicting death or serious injury to protect personal property in an unoccupied, boarded-up farmhouse against trespassers and thieves?

Holding

No. An owner may not use deadly or seriously injurious force, including by a spring gun, to protect property where no human life is threatened, so the trial court's instructions were correct and the judgment was affirmed (opinion by Moore, C.J.; all justices concurred except Larson, J., who dissented).

Reasoning

  • Standard of review: Review was for correction of errors at law. Jury findings bind the court if supported by substantial evidence.
  • Narrow framing: The court said it was "not here concerned with a man's right to protect his home and members of his family." The house was unoccupied and the owners lived miles away.
  • Instructions approved: The Brineys argued that the law permits a spring gun in a dwelling or warehouse to stop a burglar or thief. The court rejected that argument and approved Instructions 2, 5, and 6:
  • Instruction 2: Spring guns are prohibited except to prevent felonies of violence and where human life is in danger, and breaking and entering is not a felony of violence.
  • Instruction 5: An owner may use reasonable force to protect property, but not force that will take life or inflict great bodily injury, even against a trespasser who is breaking the law.
  • Instruction 6: Setting spring guns likely to kill or seriously injure, for the purpose of harming trespassers, is prohibited. The device is justified only when the trespasser is committing a felony of violence or a felony punishable by death, or is endangering human life.
  • Elements of the claim (Instruction 7, unobjected to): To recover compensatory damages, the plaintiff had to prove by a preponderance of the evidence:
  • Defendants set a shotgun trap in a vacant house, known only to them, to protect household goods from trespassers and thieves.
  • The force used exceeded the force reasonably necessary, which persons are entitled to use in protecting property.
  • Plaintiff was injured, and the amount of the damages.
  • The injuries resulted directly from the discharge of the trap the defendants set.
  • Human safety over property: Relying on Prosser, the court reasoned that the law values human safety above "mere rights in property." Spring guns are not justified against "a mere trespasser, or even a petty thief." They are privileged only against those whom the landowner, if present in person, could injure in the same way.
  • Mechanical device rule: Quoting Restatement of Torts § 85, the court held that an owner cannot gain by a mechanical device a privilege to inflict harm the owner could not inflict directly, even after a request to leave. Harper and James likewise say the owner is liable "even to a thief" injured by such a device.
  • Precedent: The court followed Hooker v. Miller, its own case holding a vineyard owner liable to a grape thief shot by a spring gun. It also relied on decisions from other states and United Zinc & Chemical Co. v. Britt (see Notes).
  • Punitive damages, preserved-error rule: The Brineys never challenged in the trial court whether punitive damages were allowable. Under the settled rule that the court will not consider a contention raised for the first time on appeal, the issue was not before it. The jury's finding of malice and wanton and reckless disregard was supported by substantial evidence and was binding.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Larson, J. (dissenting, writing alone):
  • Intent: The majority wrongly assumed the Brineys intended to shoot intruders. Intent was a disputed fact question, since both defendants testified the gun was set to scare and was aimed "low so it couldn't kill anybody."
  • Proposed rule: There should be no absolute liability to a criminal intruder. An owner of buildings housing valuable property may use devices intended to repel but not seriously injure. Liability should require an intent to kill or seriously injure, or else intentional, reckless, or grossly negligent setting of the device, following Allison v. Fiscus. Negligent installation could still create liability.
  • Three categories of intruder: He distinguished (1) the mere trespasser, where he agreed absolute liability is proper because a child or innocent person may be hurt; (2) the criminal invader who poses no threat to life, where he thought the law was unsettled; and (3) the intruder into a dwelling who threatens life, where he said most authorities recognize the privilege.
  • First impression: He read Hooker as covering only a mere trespasser in a vineyard, so this case was one of first impression.
  • Instructions: He found Instructions 2, 5, and 6 confusing. Instruction 6's phrase "for the purpose of harming trespassers" injected intent without clarifying whether intent was required, so he could not tell the basis of the jury's verdict. He would reverse and remand for a new trial.
  • Punitive damages: A plaintiff engaged in serious criminal conduct should not receive a punitive windfall. Doing so lets a criminal profit from his crime, interferes with criminal law policy, and ignores the plaintiff's unclean hands. He would treat the trial court as lacking jurisdiction to submit the issue and hold the $10,000 award void, despite the lack of objection. He criticized the majority for resting on "a mere technical rule of civil procedure."
  • Legislature: If his rule is not desirable policy, the legislature is the body best suited to set a different one.

Notes

  • Limits of the holding:
  • The court expressly set aside the right to protect one's home and family. The facts involve an unoccupied house miles from the owners' residence.
  • The court expressly declined to decide whether punitive damages are allowable against an owner who injures a criminal trespasser with a spring gun. The opinion "is not to be taken or construed as authority that the allowance of punitive damages is or is not proper." The award stood only because the issue was not preserved.
  • The court did not review the disposition of Katko's criminal charge.
  • Relied on: treatises: Prosser on Torts; Restatement of Torts § 85 (cross-referencing § 79); Harper and James § 27.3; Am. Jur.; C.J.S.; and the 44 A.L.R.2d 383 annotation.
  • Relied on: Iowa precedent: Hooker v. Miller (vineyard owner liable to grape thief; "trespassers and other inconsiderable violators of the law are not to be visited by barbarous punishments"). State v. Vance, as quoted in Hooker, holds that a mere trespass against property other than a dwelling does not justify a deadly weapon.
  • Relied on: other jurisdictions:
  • Allison v. Fiscus (Ohio): facts "very similar." It recognized recovery by a felonious warehouse intruder injured by dynamite, held that justification was a jury question, and recognized punitive damages.
  • Starkey v. Dameron (Colo.): compensatory and punitive damages for a spring gun hidden in a gas pump.
  • Wilder v. Gardner (Ga.): judgment for a plaintiff injured by a spring gun.
  • Phelps v. Hamlett (Tex.): reversed on an instruction error, but relied on for its statement that considerations of humanity bar traps dangerous to trespassers whose presence may be anticipated.
  • United Zinc & Chemical Co. v. Britt (U.S.): liability arises because the defendant expected the trespasser and prepared an injury no more justified than firing the gun himself.
  • Criminal liability cases cited: State v. Childers, Pierce v. Commonwealth, State v. Marfaudille, State v. Beckham, State v. Green (manslaughter conviction for a spring gun in a vacant house), and State v. Barr (murder conviction for a spring gun in a boarded-up cabin).
  • Statutes noted: Wisconsin, Oregon, and England ban spring guns by statute.
  • Procedural authorities: Ke-Wash Co. v. Stauffer Chemical Co., Cole v. City of Osceola, and others, for the rule against raising issues for the first time on appeal. Sebastian v. Wood, for the rule that punitive damages are not a matter of right and may be awarded on a showing of malice or wanton and reckless disregard.
  • Nothing overruled: The majority overrules no precedent.
