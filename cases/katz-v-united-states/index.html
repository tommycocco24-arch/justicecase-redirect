Black-Letter Rule

The Fourth Amendment "protects people, not places," so government electronic eavesdropping on a conversation a person justifiably relies on as private is a "search and seizure" even without physical intrusion into the enclosure. Searches conducted without prior approval by a judge or magistrate are per se unreasonable, subject only to a few specifically established and well-delineated exceptions. Justice Harlan's concurrence frames the inquiry as a twofold test: (1) the person exhibited an actual, subjective expectation of privacy, and (2) society is prepared to recognize that expectation as reasonable.

Facts

  • Charge: Katz was indicted on eight counts of transmitting wagering information by telephone from Los Angeles to Miami and Boston, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1084.
  • Surveillance: FBI agents attached an electronic listening and recording device to the outside of the public telephone booth Katz used. The device did not penetrate the booth.
  • The booth: The booth was partly glass, so Katz remained visible inside it.
  • Scope of the surveillance: Agents began only after investigation showed a strong probability Katz was using that phone to send gambling information interstate. Based on earlier visual observation, they listened only during the predicted period each morning when he used the booth. They obtained six recordings averaging about three minutes each, capturing only Katz's end of conversations about bets and wagering information. When they inadvertently picked up another person's statements once, they stopped listening.
  • No warrant: The agents never sought authorization from a magistrate.
  • Evidence at trial: Over Katz's objection, the government introduced the recordings of his end of the calls.

Procedural Posture

Katz was convicted in the District Court for the Southern District of California. The Court of Appeals affirmed, rejecting the Fourth Amendment claim because there was "no physical entrance into the area occupied by" Katz, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does the government's warrantless use of an electronic device attached to the outside of a public telephone booth to listen to and record a caller's conversations violate the Fourth Amendment, even though the device did not physically penetrate the booth?

Holding

Yes. Electronically listening to and recording Katz's words violated the privacy he justifiably relied on in the booth and was a Fourth Amendment search and seizure regardless of physical penetration, and because the agents did not obtain prior judicial authorization, a constitutional precondition for this kind of electronic surveillance, the conviction is reversed (opinion by Justice Stewart; Justice Marshall took no part).

Reasoning

  • Reframing the question: The Court refused to adopt the petitioner's framing. Labeling a place a "constitutionally protected area" does not solve Fourth Amendment problems. The Amendment is also not a general constitutional "right to privacy." It protects privacy against certain kinds of governmental intrusion. Other constitutional provisions protect other aspects of privacy, and the general right to be let alone is left largely to state law.
  • People, not places: What a person knowingly exposes to the public, even at home or in an office, is unprotected (Lewis, Lee). What a person seeks to preserve as private, even in an area accessible to the public, may be protected (Rios, Ex parte Jackson).
  • The glass booth: Visibility did not matter. Katz sought to exclude "the uninvited ear," not the intruding eye. One who occupies a booth, shuts the door, and pays the toll is entitled to assume his words will not be broadcast to the world. A narrower reading would ignore the vital role of the public telephone in private communication.
  • Trespass doctrine abandoned: Olmstead and Goldman treated the absence of physical penetration as ending the inquiry because the Amendment was thought to reach only tangible property. Warden v. Hayden discredited the premise that property interests control. Silverman held that the Amendment reaches recorded oral statements overheard without a technical trespass under local property law. The underpinnings of Olmstead and Goldman have been so eroded that their "trespass" doctrine "can no longer be regarded as controlling."
  • A warrant could have issued: The surveillance was narrow enough that a properly informed magistrate could have authorized it with appropriate safeguards, as in Osborn and as described in Berger. A footnote explains that lack of advance notice to the suspect does not defeat such an order (Ker; Fed. R. Crim. P. 41(d)).
  • Self-restraint is not enough: The government asked the Court to retroactively validate the agents' conduct because they relied on Olmstead and Goldman and did no more than a magistrate could have approved. The Court refused. The agents' restraint was self-imposed. No neutral magistrate reviewed probable cause beforehand, set limits during the search, or received a report afterward. Warrantless searches are unlawful even with facts unquestionably showing probable cause (Agnello), because a judicial officer must be interposed between citizen and police (Wong Sun; Jeffers).
  • No exception applies:
  • Incident to arrest: Surveillance even contemporaneous with arrest could hardly be an "incident" of it.
  • Hot pursuit: Electronic surveillance is unlikely to be realistic in such urgent situations.
  • Consent: The nature of electronic surveillance precludes consent.
  • No new phone-booth exception: Skipping advance authorization substitutes unreliable after-the-fact justification for objective predetermination of probable cause (Beck v. Ohio) and leaves people secure only "in the discretion of the police." The same considerations apply in a booth as in a home, office, or hotel room.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Douglas, J. (joined by Brennan, J.), concurring: Joins the Court and responds to White. The President and Attorney General are interested adversaries in national security cases, not neutral magistrates. The Fourth Amendment draws no lines between types of crimes, and spies and saboteurs are as entitled to its protection as suspected gamblers.
  • Harlan, J., concurring: Reads the majority to hold only that (a) an enclosed phone booth, like a home (Weeks) and unlike a field (Hester), is a place where a person has a constitutionally protected reasonable expectation of privacy; (b) electronic as well as physical intrusion can violate the Amendment; and (c) invasion of such an area is presumptively unreasonable without a warrant. States the twofold test: an actual, subjective expectation of privacy that society is prepared to recognize as reasonable. Things exposed to the "plain view" of outsiders fail the first prong; conversations in the open fail the second. The booth is "a temporarily private place" for its momentary occupant. Would expressly overrule Goldman ("bad physics as well as bad law") and recognize Olmstead as overruled. Does not read the majority to bar every warrantless interception of a booth conversation; exceptions can wait for an appropriate case.
  • White, J., concurring: Agrees the surveillance was unreasonable without a warrant. Relying on the national security footnote, would not require a warrant where the President or Attorney General has authorized surveillance as reasonable for national security. Notes that Hoffa, Lopez, Osborn, and On Lee (undercover agents, informants wearing recorders, transmissions from an agent) remain undisturbed: a speaker assumes the risk that the listener will repeat, record, or transmit what is said. Katz differs because he sought to exclude the uninvited ear.
  • Black, J., dissenting: The Amendment's text ("persons, houses, papers, and effects"; "particularly describing . . . things to be seized") covers tangible things already in existence, and a conversation can be neither searched nor seized, nor can a future conversation be particularly described. The Framers knew of eavesdropping and did not address it. Olmstead and Goldman rested on the text, not on trespass. Silverman turned on physical intrusion and the supervisory exclusionary rule, and Warden v. Hayden involved tangible clothing, so neither "eroded" the earlier cases. The Court is rewriting the Amendment into a general privacy guarantee and acting as a continuing constitutional convention. Also disputes Harlan's claim that the Court "has long held" this.

Notes

  • Narrow framing: The Court holds that prior judicial authorization is a precondition for "the kind of electronic surveillance involved in this case." It does not adopt a general constitutional right to privacy.
  • Source of the test: The "reasonable expectation of privacy" two-part test appears in Harlan's concurrence. The majority speaks of the privacy on which Katz "justifiably relied" and of what a person "seeks to preserve as private."
  • Expressly left open:
  • Whether safeguards other than prior magistrate authorization would satisfy the Fourth Amendment in a national security case.
  • The validity of the warrant used to search Katz's premises (not reached).
  • The law of detention or arrest under the Fourth Amendment.
  • Collateral ruling: Rejected Katz's argument that his immunized grand jury testimony after the court of appeals' affirmance required dismissal. The immunity statute protects against future prosecution, not punishment under a prior adjudication of guilt (Counselman; Brown; cf. Reina).
  • Displaced: Olmstead and Goldman: the majority says their trespass doctrine "can no longer be regarded as controlling." Harlan would state that both are overruled.
  • Relied on: Silverman (oral statements covered without technical trespass); Warden v. Hayden (property interests do not control); Osborn and Berger (judicially authorized electronic surveillance); Agnello, Wong Sun, Jeffers, Beck v. Ohio (warrant requirement and neutral magistrate); Lewis, Lee (knowing exposure); Rios, Ex parte Jackson (privacy in public-accessible settings); Ker (no-notice entries).
  • "Protected area" cases recast: Footnotes note common ground that a home is protected (Weeks) and an open field is not (Hester), and that Silverman, Lopez, and Berger used "protected area" language without treating it as a talismanic solution.
