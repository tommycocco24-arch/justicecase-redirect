Black-Letter Rule

Under the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause, "public use" means "public purpose," and courts give broad deference to legislative judgments about what public needs justify a taking. A city's taking of private, non-blighted property to carry out a carefully considered, integrated economic development plan satisfies the public use requirement, even when the land will be transferred to private parties. The government may not take A's property for the sole purpose of transferring it to private party B. It also may not take property under a mere pretext of public purpose when its actual purpose is to confer a private benefit.

Facts

  • Distressed city: A state agency designated New London a "distressed municipality" in 1990. The federal government closed the Naval Undersea Warfare Center in Fort Trumbull in 1996, eliminating over 1,500 jobs. By 1998, the city's unemployment rate was nearly double the state's, and its population was at its lowest since 1920.
  • The plan: The New London Development Corporation (NLDC), a private nonprofit, prepared an integrated plan for 90 acres of Fort Trumbull after Pfizer announced a research facility next to the area. The plan had seven parcels, including a hotel and "urban village," new residences, a Coast Guard Museum site, research and office space, marinas, a riverwalk, and park or marina support. Its goals were jobs, tax revenue, downtown revitalization, and waterfront recreation.
  • Deliberation: State agencies reviewed the project. Consultants evaluated six alternative proposals. The city council approved the plan in January 2000 and authorized the NLDC to acquire property by eminent domain in the city's name under a state statute.
  • Petitioners: Nine owners of 15 properties. Four were in parcel 3 (office space) and 11 in parcel 4A (park or marina support). Ten were occupied by the owner or a family member. Five were investment properties. Susette Kelo had made extensive improvements to her house. Wilhelmina Dery had lived in her house since her birth there in 1918.
  • No blight: No one alleged that any petitioner's property was blighted or in poor condition. The properties were condemned only because they were located in the development area.
  • Private beneficiaries: The NLDC was negotiating a 99-year ground lease at a nominal rent with Corcoran Jennison, a developer selected from a group of applicants. No lease had been signed. The identities of the private parties were not known when the plan was adopted.
  • No illegitimate purpose: The trial judge and every member of the Connecticut Supreme Court agreed that the record showed no evidence of an illegitimate purpose.

Procedural Posture

After a 7-day bench trial, the New London Superior Court enjoined the takings in parcel 4A and denied relief as to parcel 3. Both sides appealed. The Connecticut Supreme Court held all the takings valid over a three-justice dissent, which would have required clear and convincing evidence that the plan's economic benefits would actually occur. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does a city's decision to take private property for the purpose of economic development satisfy the "public use" requirement of the Fifth Amendment?

Holding

Yes. Because the city's comprehensive development plan serves a public purpose, and the Court has long read "public use" broadly as "public purpose" with deference to legislative judgment, the takings satisfy the Fifth Amendment. The judgment of the Connecticut Supreme Court is affirmed (opinion by Stevens, J.).

Reasoning

  • Two polar propositions frame the case. The sovereign may not take A's property for the sole purpose of transferring it to private party B, even with just compensation. It may transfer property between private parties when future "use by the public" is the purpose, as with a common-carrier railroad. Neither proposition decides this case.
  • No pretext here. Taking land to benefit a particular private party, or under a pretext of public purpose, would be forbidden. This plan was "carefully considered." No court below found an illegitimate purpose, and the private beneficiaries were unknown when the plan was adopted. Like the statute in Midkiff, the plan was not adopted "to benefit a particular class of identifiable individuals."
  • "Use by the public" was abandoned long ago. That test was difficult to administer and impractical given society's evolving needs. Since the late 19th century, the Court has read public use as "public purpose." The Court cites Fallbrook Irrigation Dist. v. Bradley, Strickley v. Highland Boy Gold Mining Co., Clark v. Nash, Mt. Vernon-Woodberry Cotton Duck Co., and Ruckelshaus v. Monsanto Co.
  • Public purpose is defined broadly and with deference.
  • Berman v. Parker: The Court upheld a blighted-area redevelopment plan, including the taking of a non-blighted department store, and deferred to the judgment that the area had to be planned as a whole.
  • Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff: The Court upheld transfers of fee title from lessors to lessees to break up a land oligopoly. "[I]t is only the taking's purpose, and not its mechanics," that matters.
  • Monsanto: The Court upheld data-sharing provisions even though the "most direct beneficiaries" were private applicants.
  • Federalism themes (Hairston) counsel respect for state and local judgments about local needs.
  • Application to the plan. The city found the area sufficiently distressed to justify economic rejuvenation, and that finding gets deference. The plan coordinates commercial, residential, and recreational uses and is authorized by a state statute. Given the plan's comprehensive character, the thorough deliberation behind it, and the limited scope of review, the Court evaluates the challenges in light of the entire plan, as in Berman.
  • Rejected: a bright-line rule against economic development. Promoting economic development is a traditional government function. There is no principled way to distinguish it from purposes already upheld: mining and agriculture (Strickley), a "well-balanced" community (Berman), breaking up a land oligopoly (Midkiff), and lowering market entry barriers (Monsanto).
  • Rejected: the claim that private benefit blurs public and private takings. Pursuing a public purpose often benefits private parties. Berman already rejected the "one businessman for the benefit of another" objection. "The public end may be as well or better served through an agency of private enterprise than through a department of government."
  • Hypothetical one-to-one transfers. A transfer from A to B solely because B will pay more taxes, executed outside an integrated plan, is not presented. Such a transfer would raise suspicion of a private purpose, and those cases "can be confronted if and when they arise."
  • Rejected: a "reasonable certainty" requirement. Under Midkiff, empirical debates over the wisdom of takings do not belong in federal court when the purpose is legitimate and the means are not irrational. The Court invokes Lingle v. Chevron (rejecting the "substantially advances" formula) for the same concern about judicial second-guessing. Delaying condemnation until success is assured would impede comprehensive plans, which need all parties' rights settled before construction.
  • Deference on the size and boundaries of the taking. Under Berman, once public purpose is established, the amount and character of land taken rests with the legislative branch.
  • States may go further. Nothing prevents states from imposing stricter public use limits by state constitution or statute. The Court's authority extends only to the federal baseline.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Kennedy, J. (concurring; joins the Court's opinion in full):
  • Public use review is a rational-basis standard, similar to economic regulation review under Due Process and Equal Protection. That standard still has teeth. A court should strike down a taking that, by a clear showing, is intended to favor a particular private party with only incidental or pretextual public benefits.
  • Faced with a plausible accusation of favoritism, a court should review the record seriously, while presuming the government acted reasonably and for a public purpose.
  • The trial court's inquiry here satisfied this review. It considered testimony from officials and corporate officers, communications between the parties, the city's real economic distress, the state's commitment of public funds before most beneficiaries were known, the selection of a developer from a group of applicants, and the fact that other beneficiaries were still unknown.
  • Kennedy rejects a per se rule or presumption of invalidity for economic development takings. He leaves open that a narrower category of takings, where the risk of undetected favoritism is acute, might warrant a presumption of invalidity. He declines to say what that category is.
  • Facts that kept this case under Berman and Midkiff: a comprehensive plan addressing citywide depression, benefits that were not de minimis, mostly unknown private beneficiaries, and elaborate procedures that allow review of the city's purposes.
  • O'Connor, J. (dissenting; joined by the Chief Justice, Scalia, J., and Thomas, J.):
  • The majority effectively deletes "for public use" from the Takings Clause.
  • Three categories of permissible takings exist: transfer to public ownership, transfer to private parties who make the property available for public use, and, in certain circumstances, public purpose takings for later private use.
  • Berman and Midkiff fit the third category only because the existing property use inflicted affirmative harm on society (blight in Berman, oligopoly in Midkiff), and eliminating that use directly achieved a public benefit. Kelo's and Dery's well-maintained homes caused no harm.
  • Nearly any lawful use generates some incidental public benefit, so allowing secondary benefits to justify a private-to-private transfer leaves no real limit.
  • The "coterminous with the police power" language in Berman and Midkiff was errant and unnecessary to those holdings.
  • The majority's pretext inquiry is unworkable because private and public benefits merge in economic development. It is also theoretically flawed, since motive has no bearing on whether public benefits result.
  • An "upgrade only" limit is meaningless. The case-specific facts (careful process, integrated plan) have no legal significance in limiting the rule.
  • Leaving the matter to the states is an abdication. The burdens will fall on those with fewer resources.
  • She would hold the takings in both parcels unconstitutional, reverse, and remand.
  • Thomas, J. (dissenting; also joins O'Connor's dissent):
  • Under its original meaning, the Public Use Clause permits a taking only if the government owns the property or the public has a legal right to use it.
  • Text: founding-era dictionaries define "use" as employment. The Constitution uses "use" elsewhere in this narrow sense, and "public use" contrasts with "general Welfare."
  • Common-law background: Blackstone and Kent treat nuisance law, which requires no compensation, as distinct from eminent domain.
  • Structure: a public purpose reading duplicates the Necessary and Proper Clause inquiry and renders the clause surplusage.
  • Early practice: takings for roads, canals, and common-carrier mills fit the actual-use test, and state courts were divided over broader uses.
  • Precedent: the public purpose line (Bradley, Clark, Strickley) rests on unnecessary dicta. The deference line traces to dictum in Gettysburg Electric. Berman and Midkiff wrongly equated eminent domain with the police power. Deference here is anomalous compared with other Bill of Rights protections and with the protection given to the home in search cases.
  • Policy: the losses will fall disproportionately on poor and minority communities, as with urban renewal and the Berman project itself.
  • He would reconsider the public use cases and reverse.

Notes

  • Scope of holding: The holding rests on a comprehensive, integrated, carefully deliberated plan, authorized by a state statute, with no evidence of illegitimate purpose and with private beneficiaries unknown at adoption. The Court evaluated the takings in light of the whole plan and declined to review them lot by lot.
  • Still forbidden: Purely private takings, and takings under a mere pretext of public purpose where the actual purpose is to benefit a particular private party.
  • Expressly left open:
  • One-to-one transfers outside an integrated plan, made because the new owner will pay more taxes. These would "raise a suspicion" and can be addressed if and when they arise. The Court notes (n. 17) that such takings may implicate other guarantees (Village of Willowbrook v. Olech).
  • Fairness of the measure of just compensation (n. 21), which was not before the Court.
  • Under Kennedy's concurrence, whether some narrower category of takings warrants a presumption of invalidity.
  • Federal floor only: States may impose stricter public use limits. The Court cites County of Wayne v. Hathcock (state constitutional law) and California's statute limiting economic development takings to blighted areas.
  • Precedents relied on: Berman v. Parker; Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff; Ruckelshaus v. Monsanto Co.; Fallbrook Irrigation Dist. v. Bradley; Strickley v. Highland Boy Gold Mining Co.; Clark v. Nash; Mt. Vernon-Woodberry Cotton Duck Co. v. Alabama Interstate Power Co.; Hairston v. Danville & Western R. Co.; O'Neill v. Leamer; National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Boston & Maine Corp.; Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. (by analogy, rejecting predictive judicial review); Missouri Pacific R. Co. v. Nebraska and Calder v. Bull (for the bar on A-to-B transfers). Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. is cited "cf." for urban planning.
  • *Reading of Berman (n. 13): The majority rejects petitioners' reading that Berman* upheld only blight removal. The upheld purpose included redesigning the area to prevent future blight, which is how the non-blighted store could be taken.
  • Response to O'Connor (n. 16): The majority rejects the "harmful property use" theory. The store in Berman, the lands in the mining and irrigation cases, and the trade secrets in Monsanto were not harmful, and the Court's cases focus on future use. Suggesting a public purpose cannot be achieved through private parties confuses purpose with mechanics.
  • Nothing overruled. The majority applies existing precedent. Thomas would reconsider the public purpose cases. O'Connor would treat the police-power language of Berman and Midkiff as unnecessary to their holdings.
  • Property course link: Kelo sits on the eminent domain side of takings doctrine. It is a public use case, distinct from regulatory takings (Lingle) and from the just compensation question, which the Court set aside. Both dissents stress the line between the police power and nuisance abatement, which require no compensation, and eminent domain.
