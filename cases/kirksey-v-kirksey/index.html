Kirksey v. Kirksey

Black-Letter Rule

A promise to furnish a house and land to cultivate is a mere gratuity, and no action lies for its breach, even though the promisee suffered loss and inconvenience by breaking up her home and moving to the promisor's place in reliance on it. The promisee's moving costs do not supply consideration to make the promise enforceable.

Facts

  • Parties: The plaintiff is a woman with a family to raise. The defendant is the man who made the promise.
  • The promise: The defendant promised to furnish the plaintiff with a house and land to cultivate until she could raise her family.
  • Plaintiff's reliance: The plaintiff broke up her home and moved sixty miles to the defendant's, suffering loss and inconvenience in doing so.
  • Breach: The plaintiff sued for breach of the promise.

Procedural Posture

The plaintiff sued for breach of the promise and obtained a judgment in the court below. That outcome is implied by the reversal on the ground that no action lies. On appeal, the Alabama Supreme Court reversed, pursuant to the agreement of the parties.

Issue

Does the loss and inconvenience a promisee suffers in moving to accept a promised house and land supply sufficient consideration to make the promise enforceable?

Holding

No. The defendant's promise was a mere gratuity, and an action will not lie for its breach. The judgment below is reversed. Ormond, J., delivered the opinion but states that he personally disagrees with the result. The opinion does not give a vote count.

Reasoning

  • The majority's view: Ormond's "brothers" on the court concluded that the defendant's promise was "a mere gratuity." Because it was a gratuity, its breach is not actionable.
  • No stated analysis: The opinion gives no reasoning for the majority's conclusion. It cites no statute, precedent, or policy. It states the result.
  • Ormond's contrary inclination: Ormond would have found the plaintiff's loss and inconvenience in moving sixty miles "a sufficient consideration to support the promise." He yields to the majority.
  • Disposition by agreement: The reversal is entered "pursuant to the agreement of the parties." The opinion does not describe the terms of that agreement.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text. Ormond's disagreement appears inside the court's own opinion and is covered under Notes.

Notes

  • Author disagrees with result: The opinion's author would have ruled for the plaintiff. The holding reflects the view of his colleagues, which he reports without adopting.
  • Conclusion without analysis: The court does not explain why the plaintiff's move fails as consideration. It does not discuss whether the defendant sought the move in exchange for the promise. Students should not attribute a bargain-based rationale to the opinion itself.
  • No precedent cited: The opinion relies on, distinguishes, and overrules no prior cases.
  • Reliance theory not addressed: The court addresses only whether consideration supports the promise. It does not discuss any other basis for enforcing a promise.
  • Agreement of the parties: The reversal follows an agreement between the parties, and the opinion leaves that agreement's terms unstated.
