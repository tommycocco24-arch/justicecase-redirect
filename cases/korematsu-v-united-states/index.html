Black-Letter Rule

All legal restrictions that curtail the civil rights of a single racial group are immediately suspect and must be subjected to "the most rigid scrutiny." Pressing public necessity may sometimes justify such a restriction, and racial antagonism never can. Applying that standard, the Court upheld a wartime military order excluding all persons of Japanese ancestry from a West Coast military area as within the war power of Congress and the Executive, judged as of the time it was made and violated.

Elements of the test as the majority applied it:

  1. A restriction targeting a single racial group is suspect and gets the most rigid scrutiny.
  2. Only "apprehension by the proper military authorities of the gravest imminent danger to the public safety" can justify it.
  3. The measure must have "a definite and close relationship" to preventing the danger (here, espionage and sabotage).
  4. The Court will not reject as unfounded the judgment of the military and Congress that disloyal members of the group could not be "precisely and quickly ascertained" and separated from the loyal.
  5. Validity is assessed as of the time the order was made and violated.

Facts

  • Parties: Petitioner Korematsu, an American citizen of Japanese descent, was prosecuted by the United States. No question was raised as to his loyalty.
  • Statute: An Act of Congress of March 21, 1942 made it a misdemeanor to knowingly remain in a military area contrary to the order of a designated military commander.
  • Executive and military orders: Executive Order No. 9066, issued after war with Japan began, sought protection against espionage and sabotage. Military orders issued under it included a curfew order (upheld in Hirabayashi) and Civilian Exclusion Order No. 34.
  • Exclusion order: Order No. 34, issued May 3, 1942, excluded all persons of Japanese ancestry from an area including San Leandro, California, after May 9, 1942.
  • Violation: Korematsu knowingly and admittedly remained in the area. He was charged with remaining there on May 30, 1942, and stipulated at trial that he violated the order knowing of its existence.
  • Related orders: A March 27, 1942 proclamation barred persons of Japanese ancestry from leaving the area "until and to the extent that a future proclamation or order should so permit or direct." Separate orders required evacuees to report to assembly centers and provided for detention in assembly or relocation centers.
  • Post-exclusion evidence: The majority noted that later investigations found about five thousand citizens of Japanese ancestry refused to swear unqualified allegiance, and several thousand evacuees requested repatriation to Japan.

Procedural Posture

Korematsu was convicted in federal district court of violating the 1942 Act by remaining in the military area, and the Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari because of the importance of the constitutional question.

Issue

Was it within the war power of Congress and the Executive to exclude all persons of Japanese ancestry, including citizens, from the West Coast military area, so that Korematsu's conviction for remaining there could stand?

Holding

Yes. Applying the principles of Hirabayashi, the exclusion order was within the war power because exclusion had a definite and close relationship to preventing espionage and sabotage and the Court could not reject the military's judgment that the disloyal could not be quickly separated from the loyal; the conviction was affirmed in an opinion by Justice Black.

Reasoning

  • Standard announced: Racial restrictions are "immediately suspect" and get "the most rigid scrutiny." That does not make them all unconstitutional. Public necessity may justify them. Racial antagonism cannot.
  • Hirabayashi controls the framework: Both convictions rested on the same 1942 Act and the same executive and military orders, all aimed at espionage and sabotage. Hirabayashi had already rejected delegation, war-power, and race-discrimination challenges to the curfew.
  • Exclusion vs. curfew: The Court conceded exclusion from one's home is "a far greater deprivation" than a nighttime curfew. Either requires the gravest imminent danger. Exclusion, like curfew, has a definite and close relationship to preventing espionage and sabotage. The military found curfew inadequate and ordered exclusion, under congressional authority to decide who may remain in threatened areas.
  • Deference to military judgment: Quoting Hirabayashi, the Court could not reject the judgment of the military and Congress that an unascertained number of disloyal persons existed and could not be readily isolated in a critical hour. The Court rejected the argument that the danger of invasion had passed by May 1942.
  • Group-wide application: Because immediate segregation of the disloyal from the loyal was judged impossible, the order could apply to the whole group. That military judgment "answers the contention" that exclusion was group punishment based on antagonism.
  • Time-of-order rule: The Court upheld the order "as of the time it was made and when the petitioner violated it," citing Chastleton and Block v. Hirsh.
  • Hardship acknowledged: Hardships are part of war. Compulsory exclusion of large groups of citizens is inconsistent with basic governmental institutions except under "direst emergency and peril," but when shores are threatened, "the power to protect must be commensurate with the threatened danger."
  • No contradictory orders: A person cannot be convicted for doing what it is a crime not to do. The March 27 order barring departure lasted only until a future order directed otherwise. The May 3 exclusion order was that future order. On May 30, the only operative order was the one barring him from remaining.
  • Exclusion severed from detention: The orders imposed three distinct duties: (1) depart the area; (2) report to and temporarily remain in an assembly center; (3) go under military control to a relocation center for an indeterminate period. By analogy to Blockburger, violations of each are separate offenses. Some who reported to assembly centers were released, showing the steps pose different problems. Korematsu was convicted only of remaining in the area, so the Court would not decide the validity of the detention provisions.
  • Alternative view within the Court: Some members viewed evacuation and assembly-center detention as inseparable, reasoning that the power to exclude includes the power to do so by force, which entails some restraint. Under either view, the order Korematsu violated was valid.
  • Race-prejudice characterization rejected: The Court refused to treat the case as imprisonment in a "concentration camp" for racial prejudice. It said Korematsu was excluded because of the war, the military's fear of invasion, its judgment of military urgency, and Congress's grant of power to the military. The Court would not use "the calm perspective of hindsight" to call the actions unjustified.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Frankfurter, J. (concurring): Joins the Court's opinion. Reads the orders as a clear, noncontradictory code requiring Korematsu to leave by reporting to the Assembly Center. The war power is "the power to wage war successfully" and is as much part of the Constitution as peacetime powers. Wartime action must be judged in the context of war and is not lawless because similar peacetime action would be. A military order within the means appropriate for conducting war is constitutional, and Congress may make its violation a crime triable in civil courts. Finding the measures constitutional is not approval of them. "That is their business."
  • Roberts, J. (dissenting): The facts show a clear constitutional violation. The case is unlike Hirabayashi or a temporary safety exclusion. It is a conviction for refusing to submit to imprisonment in a concentration camp based solely on ancestry, without inquiry into loyalty. The majority wrongly divides a single, indivisible program. Proclamation No. 4 made leaving a crime and Order No. 34 made staying a crime, so the only lawful path was an Assembly Center. Punishing a person bound by two contradictory orders denies due process. He need not surrender his liberty and then seek habeas, as in Endo. In a footnote, he insists courts may inquire whether an emergency existed and continued, citing Chastleton. Would reverse.
  • Murphy, J. (dissenting): The exclusion goes over "the very brink of constitutional power" and into "the ugly abyss of racism." Military judgments deserve great respect, but limits of military discretion are judicial questions (Sterling v. Constantin). His test: the deprivation must be reasonably related to a danger so "immediate, imminent, and impending" that ordinary constitutional processes cannot intervene (United States v. Russell, Mitchell v. Harmony, Raymond v. Thomas). The order fails because it rests on an assumption of group racial disloyalty. General DeWitt's Final Report relied on racial and sociological claims and unverified incidents, which deserve no military deference. Individual disloyalty cannot justify group punishment. Individual hearings were feasible, as done for persons of German and Italian ancestry, given the months that passed and the absence of martial law. Finds violations of equal protection under the Fifth Amendment, liberty of movement and residence, and procedural due process.
  • Jackson, J. (dissenting): Guilt is personal and not inheritable. Korematsu's presence was criminal only because of his parents' ancestry. Courts cannot hold military commands to constitutional standards and should not try, but a reasonable military order is not thereby constitutional. Courts cannot meaningfully evaluate military necessity; here the Court had only DeWitt's unsworn, untested report. Judicial approval is worse than the order itself: the principle "lies about like a loaded weapon" for any authority with a plausible claim of urgent need. The Court expanded Hirabayashi despite that opinion's express limits to the curfew. A civil court cannot be made to enforce an order that violates constitutional limits even if militarily reasonable. The real check on military power is political and historical judgment. Would reverse and discharge the prisoner.

Notes

  • Narrow ground: The holding covers only the exclusion order Korematsu was convicted of violating. The Court expressly declined to decide the validity of the orders requiring reporting to and detention in assembly or relocation centers, reserving those "serious constitutional issues" until such an order "is applied or is certain to be applied" to a petitioner.
  • Temporal limit: The order was upheld as of the time it was made and violated, not for all time.
  • Majority split on severability: Some Justices in the majority treated exclusion and assembly-center detention as inseparable, but reached the same result.
  • Relied on: Hirabayashi v. United States (curfew upheld under same Act and orders; framework and quoted deference language). Chastleton Corp. v. Sinclair and Block v. Hirsh (cf., validity judged at time of the measure). Ex parte Kawato (cf., hardship). Blockburger v. United States (cf., separate duties as separate offenses).
  • Distinguished: The Endo case, decided the same term, is cited as illustrating the difference between the validity of an exclusion order and of detention after exclusion.
  • Dissents' use of precedent: Roberts distinguishes Hirabayashi as a curfew case. Jackson quotes Hirabayashi's own limiting language to argue the majority extended it beyond what it decided. Murphy draws his test from Sterling, Russell, Mitchell, and Raymond.
  • No overrulings: The opinion overrules no prior case.
