Black-Letter Rule

Due process bars convicting a person under a registration law that punishes wholly passive conduct when the person had no actual knowledge of the duty to register and the prosecution makes no showing of the probability of such knowledge. Conviction under such a law requires actual knowledge of the duty, or proof of its probable knowledge, followed by a failure to comply.

Facts

  • Ordinance: Los Angeles Municipal Code § 52.39 made it unlawful for any "convicted person" to be or remain in the city more than five days without registering with the Chief of Police. "Convicted person" covered anyone convicted of an offense punishable as a felony in California, or of an out-of-state offense that would be a California felony.
  • Continuing offense: § 52.43(b) made each day's failure to register a separate offense.
  • No mental element: The ordinance contained no willfulness element, and the California court did not read one into it.
  • Appellant: She had lived in Los Angeles for over seven years. During that time she was convicted in Los Angeles of forgery, a felony under California law. She had not registered.
  • Arrest: She was arrested on suspicion of another offense and then charged with failing to register.
  • Excluded defense: She offered proof that she did not know of the duty to register. The trial court refused it, so the Court assumed she lacked actual knowledge.
  • Sentence: The court fined her $250 and placed her on probation for three years.

Procedural Posture

A jury convicted appellant after the trial court rejected her due process objection, and the trial court denied her motion for arrest of judgment and a new trial. The Appellate Department of the Superior Court affirmed, and the case came to the Supreme Court on appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1257(2), where it was argued and reargued.

Issue

Does a registration ordinance violate Fourteenth Amendment due process when applied to a person who had no actual knowledge of the duty to register, with no showing of the probability of such knowledge?

Holding

Yes; the registration provisions, as applied to a person without actual knowledge of the duty to register and without proof of the probability of such knowledge, violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, so the conviction is reversed (Douglas, J., for the Court, with Justice Burton dissenting separately and Justice Frankfurter, joined by Justices Harlan and Whittaker, dissenting).

Reasoning

  • Legislative latitude acknowledged: The Court rejected Blackstone's view that a "vicious will" is always necessary. Lawmakers have wide latitude to define offenses without knowledge or diligence elements (Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co. v. United States).
  • Ignorance-of-law rule acknowledged: "Ignorance of the law will not excuse" is deep in our law (Shevlin-Carpenter Co. v. Minnesota). The police power is "one of the least limitable" (District of Columbia v. Brooke).
  • Wholly passive conduct: The ordinance punishes mere failure to register. That differs from committing acts, or failing to act under circumstances that should alert the actor to the consequences (cf. Shevlin-Carpenter, United States v. Balint, United States v. Dotterweich).
  • Notice is part of due process: Notice is required before property is disturbed, assessments are made, or penalties are imposed for failure to act (Mullane v. Central Hanover Trust Co., Covey v. Town of Somers, Walker v. Hutchinson City). Those were civil property cases. The Court held the principle applies equally when a wholly passive, unaware person faces criminal condemnation.
  • This ordinance differs from ordinary registration laws: Many registration laws resemble licensing of business activity (cf. Bryant v. Zimmerman, United States v. Harriss, United States v. Kahriger). Here, violation involves no activity at all. Mere presence in the city is the test. Nothing in the circumstances would prompt a person to ask whether registration was required.
  • Weak law enforcement interest: At most the ordinance is a convenience for police, compiling a list of convictions already publicly recorded.
  • No chance to comply: On first learning of the duty, appellant had no opportunity to comply and avoid the penalty, though her default was entirely innocent.
  • Blameworthiness: The Court quoted Holmes, The Common Law: a law punishing conduct "which would not be blameworthy in the average member of the community would be too severe for that community to bear." The severity lies in the lack of any opportunity to avoid the law's consequences or defend against prosecution.
  • Fine-print analogy: Convicting without knowledge here would be as bad as a law "written in print too fine to read or in a language foreign to the community."

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Burton, J. (dissent, no opinion): Stated only that, as applied to appellant, the ordinance does not violate her constitutional rights.
  • Frankfurter, J., joined by Harlan and Whittaker, JJ. (dissent):
  • Federal and state law is "thick with" provisions punishing people who had no awareness of the law's requirements, and courts have sustained them, including many registration laws (Balint).
  • In fairness terms, a five-year narcotics sentence for an unaware defendant is no different from appellant's probation and $250 fine.
  • Courts can address hardship by reading a mental element into a statute as a matter of construction. A grossly disproportionate sanction may violate the Eighth Amendment or Fourteenth Amendment due process.
  • The majority's line between doing and not doing revives the old feasance/nonfeasance distinction, which cannot mark the boundary of constitutionality.
  • The Holmes passage, read in context, does not support the majority. Holmes would not have drawn this line.
  • If applied to its full scope, the decision would impair a vast body of legislation. Frankfurter predicted it would be "an isolated deviation" and "a derelict on the waters of the law."
  • On the facts, appellant was convicted in California, in Los Angeles itself, and lived there for over seven years. She faced no hard question about whether an out-of-state offense counted as a California felony.

Notes

  • As-applied holding: The Court held the provisions unconstitutional "as sought to be applied here." It did not strike down registration laws generally.
  • Two-part limit: The bar applies where there is no actual knowledge of the duty and no proof of the probability of such knowledge. Either one can support a conviction, if followed by failure to comply.
  • Key distinguishing features stressed by the Court: wholly passive conduct, no activity beyond presence in the city, no circumstances prompting inquiry, and no opportunity to comply after learning of the duty.
  • Legislative latitude preserved: The Court reaffirmed that legislatures may generally omit knowledge and diligence elements and that ignorance of the law is ordinarily no excuse.
  • Relied on: Mullane, Covey, and Walker (notice principle, extended from civil property cases to criminal prosecution); Holmes, The Common Law (blameworthiness).
  • Distinguished (cited "cf."): Shevlin-Carpenter, Balint, and Dotterweich, as involving acts or failures to act under circumstances that should alert the actor; Bryant, Harriss, and Kahriger, as registration laws of a different, often business-related, character.
  • Cited for general principles: Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co. (latitude to exclude knowledge elements); District of Columbia v. Brooke (breadth of police power).
  • Overruled: None.
