Black-Letter Rule

The liberty protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment includes the right of adults to engage in private, consensual sexual conduct without government intervention. A state statute making such conduct between same-sex adults a crime "furthers no legitimate state interest which can justify its intrusion into the personal and private life of the individual." Bowers v. Hardwick is overruled.

Facts

  • Entry: Harris County officers responded to a reported weapons disturbance and entered the Houston apartment where petitioner John Geddes Lawrence lived. The Court noted that the right of the police to enter did not seem to have been questioned.
  • Conduct: Officers observed Lawrence and Tyron Garner engaging in a sexual act. Both were adults, and the conduct was private and consensual.
  • Statute: Tex. Penal Code § 21.06(a) made it a crime to engage in "deviate sexual intercourse with another individual of the same sex." The definition covered oral and anal contact and penetration with an object. The same conduct between different-sex partners was not covered.
  • Charge and penalty: Both men were arrested, held overnight, and charged with "deviate sexual intercourse, namely anal sex, with a member of the same sex (man)." After pleading nolo contendere, each was fined $200 plus $141.25 in court costs.
  • Consequences: The offense was a class C misdemeanor. The Court stressed that the convictions would stay on petitioners' records, would trigger sex-offender registration in at least four other States, and would carry collateral consequences such as notations on job applications.
  • Enforcement history: Texas admitted in 1994 that it had not prosecuted anyone for private, consensual adult conduct under the statute.

Procedural Posture

Petitioners were convicted before a Justice of the Peace. On trial de novo, the Harris County Criminal Court rejected their federal and state equal protection challenges. The Texas Court of Appeals for the Fourteenth District, sitting en banc in a divided opinion, rejected their equal protection and due process arguments and affirmed, treating Bowers as controlling on due process. The Supreme Court granted certiorari on three questions: equal protection, due process, and whether Bowers should be overruled.

Issue

Does a state statute that criminalizes private, consensual sexual conduct between adults of the same sex violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment?

Holding

Yes. In an opinion by Justice Kennedy, the Court held that adults have a liberty interest under the Due Process Clause in engaging in private, consensual sexual conduct, and that the Texas statute furthers no legitimate state interest that can justify its intrusion into that private life; it overruled Bowers, reversed the Texas Court of Appeals, and remanded for further proceedings not inconsistent with the opinion.

Reasoning

  • Choice of ground. The Court resolved the case under due process. It called petitioners' equal protection argument under Romer v. Evans "tenable" but declined to rest on it. An equal protection holding would leave open whether a ban drawn to cover both same-sex and different-sex conduct would be valid. Leaving Bowers in place would also leave the stigma of criminalization intact even if this statute fell.
  • Link between the two clauses. Equality of treatment and due process liberty "are linked in important respects," and a ruling on liberty advances both. Criminalizing homosexual conduct is itself "an invitation to subject homosexual persons to discrimination both in the public and in the private spheres."
  • The liberty line of cases. The Court traced substantive liberty from Pierce and Meyer through Griswold (privacy in the marital bedroom), Eisenstadt (extending decisions about sexual conduct to unmarried persons), Roe (a substantive due process right with "real and substantial protection"), and Carey (invalidating a contraceptive ban for minors under 16). Together these showed Griswold could not be confined to married adults.
  • Bowers misframed the claim. Bowers asked whether there is a "fundamental right upon homosexuals to engage in sodomy." Framing it that way "demeans the claim," much as calling marriage simply a right to have sexual intercourse would demean a married couple. These statutes reach "the most private human conduct, sexual behavior, and in the most private of places, the home," and seek to control a personal relationship that adults may choose without being punished as criminals.
  • Bowers's history was overstated.
  • No longstanding American history of laws aimed at homosexual conduct as a distinct matter. Early sodomy laws, derived from the 1533 English statute, covered relations between men and women too and targeted nonprocreative sex generally.
  • Sodomy laws do not seem to have been enforced against consenting adults in private. Surviving prosecutions mostly involved minors, force, status disparity, or animals.
  • Nineteenth-century evidence rules barred conviction on a consenting partner's testimony, which helps explain infrequent prosecutions.
  • Laws singling out same-sex conduct appeared only in the 1970s, in nine States, and several have since abandoned them.
  • Morality alone does not answer the question. Religious and moral condemnation of homosexual conduct reflects sincere convictions. The issue is whether the majority may use the criminal law to enforce those views on the whole society. "Our obligation is to define the liberty of all, not to mandate our own moral code." (Casey.)
  • Recent tradition controls. "[O]ur laws and traditions in the past half century are of most relevance here." They show "an emerging awareness that liberty gives substantial protection to adult persons in deciding how to conduct their private lives in matters pertaining to sex." History and tradition are "the starting point but not in all cases the ending point" of the inquiry (County of Sacramento v. Lewis, Kennedy, J., concurring).
  • Evidence of that awareness.
  • The 1955 Model Penal Code declined to criminalize private consensual relations. Its reasons: such laws undermine respect for law, regulate conduct not harmful to others, and invite arbitrary enforcement and blackmail.
  • The British Wolfenden Report and the Sexual Offences Act 1967.
  • Dudgeon v. United Kingdom (European Court of Human Rights, 1981), which contradicts Bowers's premise that the claim was insubstantial in Western civilization.
  • States with such laws fell from the 25 referenced in Bowers to 13, with a pattern of nonenforcement against consenting adults in private.
  • Erosion by later cases. Casey reaffirmed constitutional protection for personal decisions about marriage, procreation, contraception, family relationships, child rearing, and education. Persons in homosexual relationships "may seek autonomy for these purposes, just as heterosexual persons do." Romer struck down class-based legislation directed at homosexuals as born of animosity and lacking a rational relation to a legitimate purpose.
  • Stare decisis. Stare decisis "is not an inexorable command" (Payne v. Tennessee). Under Casey, reliance on an existing liberty counsels strongly against overruling. The Court gave four reasons that test favored overruling Bowers:
  • Bowers induced no individual or societal reliance of that sort.
  • Its foundations were eroded by Casey and Romer.
  • It faced substantial and continuing criticism, including from five state courts that declined to follow it under their own constitutions and from the European Court of Human Rights.
  • It created uncertainty because precedents before and after it contradicted its central holding.
  • Adoption of the Bowers dissent. Justice Stevens's Bowers dissent "should have been controlling in Bowers and should control here." Its two propositions: a majority's traditional view that a practice is immoral is not a sufficient reason to uphold a law prohibiting it, and intimate choices by married and unmarried persons are protected liberty.
  • Conclusion. "Bowers was not correct when it was decided, and it is not correct today." The State "cannot demean their existence or control their destiny by making their private sexual conduct a crime." The Framers did not presume to know liberty's "manifold possibilities," and each generation can invoke its principles.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice O'Connor, concurring in the judgment. She joined Bowers and declined to join in overruling it. She found the statute unconstitutional under the Equal Protection Clause.
  • Texas treats the same conduct differently based solely on the participants.
  • Where a law exhibits a "bare ... desire to harm a politically unpopular group," the Court applies "a more searching form of rational basis review," especially where the law inhibits personal relationships (Moreno, Eisenstadt, Cleburne, Romer).
  • Moral disapproval of a group, standing alone, is not a legitimate state interest under equal protection.
  • The statute targets conduct "closely correlated with being homosexual," so it is directed at gay persons as a class.
  • She distinguished Bowers as a due process case that never held moral disapproval a rational basis for a classification punishing homosexual but not heterosexual sodomy.
  • She left open whether a sodomy law neutral in effect and application would violate substantive due process.
  • She stated that other laws distinguishing homosexuals and heterosexuals would not necessarily fail, naming national security and preserving the traditional institution of marriage as legitimate interests Texas did not assert here.
  • Justice Scalia, dissenting, joined by the Chief Justice and Justice Thomas.
  • Stare decisis: The majority abandoned Casey's approach. Its three factors (erosion, criticism, lack of reliance) apply equally to Roe. Societal reliance on Bowers's validation of morals legislation was "overwhelming."
  • Substantive due process: Under Glucksberg, only rights "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" get heightened scrutiny. The majority never calls homosexual sodomy a fundamental right and never applies strict scrutiny, so Bowers's fundamental-rights conclusion stands untouched. Bowers's history was accurate, since sodomy was criminalized whether or not laws targeted same-sex conduct specifically. An "emerging awareness" cannot create a fundamental right, and foreign views are "meaningless dicta."
  • Rational basis: The majority actually applied rational-basis review. Holding that majoritarian sexual morality is not a legitimate interest "effectively decrees the end of all morals legislation," calling into question laws against bigamy, same-sex marriage, adult incest, prostitution, adultery, fornication, bestiality, and obscenity.
  • Equal protection: The statute applies equally to all persons. Distinguishing by the partner's sex mirrors marriage laws. Loving used heightened scrutiny only because of racial purpose. O'Connor's "more searching" review lacks support and leaves opposite-sex marriage laws on "pretty shaky grounds."
  • He calls the majority's disclaimer about formal recognition of relationships unreliable. He argues the matter belongs to democratic change.
  • Justice Thomas, dissenting. He joined Justice Scalia's dissent. He called the law "uncommonly silly" and said he would vote to repeal it as a legislator. He wrote that he can find no general right of privacy in the Bill of Rights or any other part of the Constitution.

Notes

  • What the case does not involve. The Court expressly excluded:
  • minors;
  • persons who might be injured or coerced, or who are in relationships where consent might not easily be refused;
  • public conduct;
  • prostitution;
  • whether the government must give formal recognition to any relationship homosexual persons seek to enter.
  • Level of scrutiny. The majority opinion does not label the right "fundamental" and does not name a tier of scrutiny. It holds that the statute "furthers no legitimate state interest" that can justify the intrusion. Justice Scalia reads this as rational-basis review. Students should be ready to defend a reading of the standard from the opinion's own language.
  • Equal protection not reached. The Court called the Romer-based equal protection argument "tenable" but did not decide it. Only Justice O'Connor rested on equal protection.
  • Left open by the concurrence. Justice O'Connor did not decide whether a sex-neutral sodomy law would violate substantive due process.
  • Overruled. Bowers v. Hardwick, 478 U.S. 186 (1986).
  • Relied on.
  • Pierce v. Society of Sisters, Meyer v. Nebraska, Griswold v. Connecticut, Eisenstadt v. Baird, Roe v. Wade, Carey v. Population Services Int'l: the substantive liberty line.
  • Planned Parenthood v. Casey: personal autonomy; liberty over moral code; stare decisis and reliance.
  • Romer v. Evans: erosion of Bowers; animus.
  • County of Sacramento v. Lewis (Kennedy, J., concurring): history as starting point only.
  • Payne v. Tennessee: stare decisis not inexorable.
  • Justice Stevens's dissent in Bowers: adopted as controlling.
  • Dudgeon v. United Kingdom and later European Court of Human Rights decisions: persuasive authority.
  • Distinguished. Bowers's Georgia statute applied regardless of the participants' sex. The Texas statute applied only to same-sex participants.
  • Disposition. Reversed and remanded for further proceedings not inconsistent with the opinion.
