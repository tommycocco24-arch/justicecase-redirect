Black-Letter Rule

An advertisement is ordinarily an invitation to negotiate and is not an offer. It becomes an offer only when it is clear, definite, and explicit and leaves nothing open for negotiation, or when it offers a reward for performing a specified act. Whether an offer was made turns on how an objective, reasonable person would understand the communication, and a statement evidently made in jest is not an offer.

Facts

  • Parties: Leonard, a Seattle resident, sued PepsiCo, which makes and distributes Pepsi and Diet Pepsi.
  • Promotion: "Pepsi Stuff" let consumers collect Pepsi Points from marked packages and redeem them for Pepsi-logo merchandise. PepsiCo tested the promotion in the Pacific Northwest, including Washington, from October 1995 to March 1996.
  • Commercial: A teenager shows off a T-shirt (75 points), leather jacket (1450 points), and shades (175 points). A Harrier Jet then lands at a school, the wind strips a teacher to his underwear, and the teen says "Sure beats the bus." The screen reads "HARRIER FIGHTER 7,000,000 PEPSI POINTS." A footnote records an on-screen line: "Offer not available in all areas. See details on specially marked packages."
  • Catalog: The commercial referred viewers to the Pepsi Stuff Catalog. Its Order Form listed 53 items, from 15 points to 3300 points (a mountain bike). No Harrier Jet appeared. Orders could be placed "only" on the original Order Form. Extra points could be bought for ten cents each if at least 15 original points came with the order.
  • Leonard's order: Leonard raised about $700,000 and, on or about March 27, 1996, sent an Order Form with "1 Harrier Jet" written in, 15 original points, and a check for $700,008.50. Leonard appears to have had counsel at the time.
  • Rejection: PepsiCo's fulfillment house returned the check, calling the jet "fanciful" and included to make the ad humorous. BBDO, the ad agency, called the jet "clearly a joke."
  • Jet's cost: A Harrier costs roughly $28 million, and Leonard knew that.
  • Later ads: PepsiCo later changed the commercial to 700,000,000 points, then added "(Just Kidding)."

Procedural Posture

PepsiCo sued in S.D.N.Y. for a declaratory judgment, and Leonard sued in Florida state court; the Florida case was removed and transferred to S.D.N.Y., and the declaratory action was dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction. After years of procedural disputes, Leonard consented to the court's jurisdiction, and PepsiCo moved for summary judgment under Rule 56 on the contract and fraud claims.

Issue

Did the Pepsi Stuff commercial constitute an offer that Leonard could accept by submitting an Order Form and 7,000,000 Pepsi Points, forming an enforceable contract for a Harrier Jet?

Holding

No. The commercial was an advertisement and not a unilateral offer, no objective, reasonable person would have read it as a serious offer of a fighter jet, and any alleged contract fails the Statute of Frauds, so Judge Kimba M. Wood granted PepsiCo's motion for summary judgment on the contract and fraud claims.

Reasoning

  • Summary judgment fits contract formation. Formation can be decided on summary judgment when the words and conduct are so clear that reasonable people could not differ over their meaning (Krumme, quoting Bourque; Wards Co.).
  • Choice of law. As a transferred case, Florida choice-of-law rules apply (Ferens). Florida looks to where the last act necessary to complete the contract occurred. That depends on whether the commercial was an offer (acceptance in Washington) or a solicitation (PepsiCo's acceptance in New York). The court resolved the offer question under general common-law principles. Having found no offer, it applied New York law to the Statute of Frauds.
  • Ground 1: Advertisements are not offers.
  • General rule from Restatement (Second) § 26 & cmt. b, Corbin, Farnsworth, Williston, and New York cases (Lovett, Geismar, Gimbel Bros.). Ads need "some language of commitment or some invitation to take action without further communication."
  • Filling out an order form does not convert an ad into an offer (Mesaros; Foremost Pro Color; Alligood). Leonard's Order Form was the offer. A contract would arise only if PepsiCo accepted it and cashed the check.
  • Exception: an ad that is "clear, definite, and explicit, and leaves nothing open for negotiation" is an offer (Lefkowitz).
  • Lefkowitz distinguished on two grounds. (1) The commercial reserved the details to a separate writing, the Catalog, and said nothing about how to accept. (2) Even if the Catalog had listed the jet, there were no words of limitation like "first come, first served," so the alleged offer would be too indefinite (Mesaros; Farnsworth on the risk of acceptances exceeding inventory).
  • Payne v. Lautz Bros. distinguished in a footnote: that ad was "absolutely unrestricted," while this commercial expressly reserved details to the Catalog.
  • Reward cases do not apply.
  • Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball and similar "prove me wrong" cases (Barnes, Newman, James v. Turilli, Las Vegas Hacienda, Grove, Mears) involve offers that seek performance of a specified act.
  • The commercial sought a reciprocal promise through the Order Form. It did not say that anyone who showed up with 7,000,000 points would get a jet.
  • Carbolic Smoke Ball itself separates reward offers from ordinary ads, which are "offers to negotiate — offers to receive offers — offers to chaffer" (Bowen, L.J.).
  • Leonard noticed the jet was missing from the Catalog and said this did not affect Leonard's understanding. The court: "It should have."
  • Ground 2: Objective reasonable person standard.
  • Neither side's subjective intent counts. The test is what an objective, reasonable person would understand (Kay-R Elec.; Mesaros).
  • Acts "evidently done in jest" create no power of acceptance (Corbin § 1.11; Graves). If the jest is not apparent and a reasonable hearer would believe an offer was made, a contract may form (Barnes; Lucy v. Zehmer).
  • Judge, not jury. Gallagher v. Delaney rested on the subtle workplace dynamics of a sexual harassment claim. Deciding how a reasonable person would read a commercial is a routine judicial task on summary judgment.
  • Why the ad was a joke (five reasons):
  • It makes exaggerated, transformative claims typical of ads, which reasonable viewers treat as puffery (Hubbard; Lovett).
  • The teenage pilot is wildly improbable: preening, helmetless, and casual about flying a fighter through a residential area.
  • Flying a jet to school is an exaggerated adolescent fantasy (the gaping classmates, the defrocked teacher, the bike rack).
  • A Harrier is a military attack aircraft, so showing it as a commute is not serious, even if a demilitarized version could be acquired.
  • Getting a $28 million jet for about $700,000 is "a deal too good to be true." Drinking 7,000,000 Pepsis would take roughly 190 a day for 100 years.
  • Same visual treatment of all items and a press release with no mention of humor show only that the humor was tongue-in-cheek. Humor is not limited to the "rough and boisterous joke" (Murphy v. Steeplechase, Cardozo).
  • In footnotes, the court contrasted the seriousness signals in Carbolic Smoke Ball (£1000 deposited in a bank) and Barnes (a statement made before a state commission, later reaffirmed).
  • Discovery denied.
  • Later ad changes are not probative. They may reflect concern about unreasonable people threatening frivolous litigation, and the question is whether the ad Leonard saw was an offer.
  • PepsiCo's internal understanding goes only to subjective intent, which is irrelevant.
  • Whether others also read the ad as an offer does not make that reading more or less reasonable.
  • Per a footnote, any discovery would bear only on Ground 2 and would leave Grounds 1 and 3 untouched.
  • Ground 3: Statute of Frauds (N.Y.U.C.C. § 2-201(1)).
  • A sale of goods for $500 or more requires a writing sufficient to show a contract, signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought.
  • Under Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden, signed and unsigned writings may be combined if they clearly refer to the same transaction. Horn & Hardart sets two strict threshold requirements:
  • The signed writing must itself establish a contractual relationship.
  • The unsigned writing must on its face refer to the same transaction.
  • Neither is met. The commercial is not a writing. The Order Form lacks PepsiCo's signature. Leonard is not a party to or beneficiary of PepsiCo's contracts with its advertisers.
  • Fraud claim.
  • The elements are a representation of a material existing fact, falsity, scienter, deception, and injury (NYU v. Continental).
  • Alleging that a defendant entered a contract with no intent to perform is insufficient (Rocanova; Grappo). The misrepresentation must be collateral or extraneous to the contract (Bridgestone/Firestone; Deerfield). In Stewart v. Jackson & Nash, misstatements of present fact supported fraud.
  • Leonard alleged only that PepsiCo never intended to honor the "offer." Judgment for PepsiCo.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Three independent grounds. Each of the three grounds (no offer, evident jest, Statute of Frauds) alone supports summary judgment. The court said discovery could affect only the jest ground.
  • Narrow framing. The question was whether the specific commercial Leonard saw and relied on was an offer. The later 700,000,000-point and "(Just Kidding)" versions were outside the question.
  • Offer/acceptance mechanics. The consumer's completed order form is the offer, and the advertiser's acceptance (processing payment) forms the contract. This drove the choice-of-law analysis.
  • Relied on: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 26 & cmt. b; Corbin §§ 1.11, 2.4; Farnsworth § 3.10; Williston § 4:7; Mesaros; Lovett; Kay-R Elec.; Krumme; Bourque; Crabtree; Horn & Hardart; Grappo.
  • Distinguished: Lefkowitz (definite ad with "first come, first served"); Payne v. Lautz Bros. (unrestricted ad); Carbolic Smoke Ball, Barnes, Newman, James v. Turilli, and the hole-in-one cases (reward offers); Gallagher v. Delaney (jury needed for subtle workplace dynamics); Stewart v. Jackson & Nash (collateral misrepresentation of present fact).
  • Cited as consistent with the jest analysis: Graves (joke column, no contract); Lucy v. Zehmer (jest not apparent, contract enforced).
  • Overruled: None. A footnote notes that Foremost Pro Color was overruled on other grounds by Hasbrouck v. Texaco.
