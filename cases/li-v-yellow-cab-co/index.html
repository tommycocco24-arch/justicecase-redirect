Black-Letter Rule

In California negligence actions for injury to person or property, the plaintiff's contributory negligence does not bar recovery. Damages are reduced in proportion to the negligence attributable to the plaintiff, under "pure" comparative negligence, even when the plaintiff is as much at fault as the defendant or more. Last clear chance is abolished. Assumption of risk is abolished to the extent it is merely a variant of contributory negligence. Both are absorbed into the apportionment of liability by fault.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff Nga Li sued defendant Robert Phillips, a Yellow Cab Company employee driving a company-owned taxi, and Yellow Cab Company.
  • Accident: Li was northbound on Alvarado Street in Los Angeles. About 70 feet before the Third Street intersection, she began a left turn across three southbound lanes toward a service station driveway. Phillips came southbound over the crest of a hill, passed through the intersection, and hit the right rear of her car. Li was injured and her car was heavily damaged.
  • Defendant's fault: The trial court found Phillips entered the intersection at about 30 mph, an unsafe speed for that time and place, on a yellow light.
  • Plaintiff's fault: The trial court found Li turned when an oncoming vehicle was "so close as to constitute an immediate hazard." Her negligence was a proximate cause of the collision.

Procedural Posture

The trial court, sitting without a jury, held that Li's contributory negligence barred her recovery and entered judgment for defendants. The California Supreme Court reviewed that judgment.

Issue

May the California Supreme Court, despite Civil Code § 1714, judicially replace the all-or-nothing contributory negligence bar with a system of comparative negligence?

Holding

Yes. Section 1714 does not freeze contributory negligence into law or preclude judicial development, so the court replaced the all-or-nothing rule with pure comparative negligence and reversed the judgment. Sullivan, J., wrote for the court, joined by Wright, C.J., Tobriner, J., and Burke, J. Mosk, J., concurred. Clark, J., dissented, joined by McComb, J.

Reasoning

  • Merits of the old rule: Contributory negligence fails to distribute responsibility in proportion to fault. In a fault-based system, the extent of fault should govern the extent of liability. None of the justifications offered for the bar (proximate cause, penal theory, deterrence) holds up.
  • Practical experience: Juries already discount damages for plaintiff fault in an informal, "haphazard" way. That gap between the formal rule and actual practice undermines public confidence in the law's ability to assign liability consistently.
  • Trend elsewhere: Twenty-five states had adopted apportionment by statute. Florida did so by judicial decision in Hoffman v. Jones. Federal law (FELA, Jones Act, Death on the High Seas Act) uses pure comparative negligence.
  • Section 1714 and separation of powers: Defendants argued § 1714 codified contributory negligence and only the Legislature could change it. The court rejected that argument in three steps:
  • Code character: The 1872 Civil Code continues the common law and is liberally construed (Civ. Code §§ 4, 5). Under Estate of Elizalde, code sections are read in light of the common law unless they clearly depart from it.
  • Legislative intent: The court rejected plaintiff's argument that "except so far as" enacted comparative negligence in 1872. The Code Commissioners' Note cited the same sources the Field draft used for the basic negligence rule. No jurisdiction the note cited applied comparative negligence in 1872. The real intent was to reject the harsh New York rule (Johnson v. Hudson River R.R.) and adopt contributory negligence softened by last clear chance (Davies v. Mann, Tuff v. Warman).
  • Room for development: The Legislature did not mean to stop judicial evolution. Courts had already developed proximate cause and duty (e.g., Dillon v. Legg, Biakanja v. Irving) and alternative-liability rules (Summers v. Tice, Ybarra v. Spangard) under § 1714's general language. Its defensive language should get the same "dynamic" reading in service of its two precepts: negligent actors pay for harm they cause, and negligent plaintiffs cannot shift their own share of the loss.
  • Practical objections: Multiple-party problems, jury difficulty in setting percentages, last clear chance, assumption of risk, and willful misconduct were real concerns but did not justify inaction. Special verdicts and jury guidelines can help. Trial courts get broad discretion.
  • Pure versus 50 percent: The court chose the pure form. A 50 percent cutoff "shifts the lottery aspect" of contributory negligence: a plaintiff at 49 percent recovers 51 percent of damages, while a plaintiff at 50 percent recovers nothing. In multi-car collisions the bar drops even lower. Wisconsin's experience produced repeated appeals over whether fault was equal. The court adopted the reasoning of Chief Justice Hallows's dissent in Vincent v. Pabst Brewing Co.
  • Retroactivity: The court applied a rule of limited retroactivity based on fairness, public policy, and reliance. The new rule covers:
  • all cases where trial had not begun before this decision became final;
  • this case itself, to reward litigants who challenge outmoded doctrine (Stovall v. Denno; Mishkin);
  • any retrial after a reversal for other reasons.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Mosk, J. (concurring): Mosk agreed with the judgment and the substance of the opinion but objected to the majority's treatment of retroactivity. Despite the majority's footnote disclaimer, applying the new rule to the litigant before the court in fact overrules Westbrook v. Mihaly and related cases to the extent they are inconsistent: Alhambra City Sch. Dist. v. Mize, Larez v. Shannon, Foytik v. Aronson, In re Yurko, and People v. Hitch. Precedent comes from what a court does as well as what it says. He faulted the majority's "standardless" appeals to fairness for failing to explain why Li gets the new rule when earlier litigants did not.
  • Clark, J., joined by McComb, J. (dissenting):
  • The majority concedes the Legislature intended § 1714 to codify contributory negligence modified by last clear chance, yet it refuses to honor that intent.
  • Changing a statute uniformly construed for 103 years is "amendment by judicial fiat" and intrudes on legislative power.
  • The Legislature can best choose among competing systems because courts cannot investigate beyond the record. The majority's quick reduction of the options to two forms shows that limit.
  • Most other state courts have deferred to their legislatures on this question.

Notes

  • Scope: The rule covers "all actions for negligence resulting in injury to person or property." "Fault" in the opinion means only negligence in the accepted legal sense.
  • Expressly left open: The court declined to decide problems involving multiple parties (absent tortfeasors, res judicata, contribution, and indemnity) and willful misconduct because neither was presented. It noted the argument that apportionment should reach misconduct short of intentional, but did not adopt it as a holding. Punitive damages remain a separate matter.
  • *Assumption of risk split (Grey v. Fibreboard Paper Products Co.):*
  • Where the plaintiff unreasonably encounters a known risk created by the defendant's negligence, the conduct is a form of contributory negligence. It is merged into comparative fault.
  • Where the plaintiff agrees to relieve the defendant of the duty of care, the doctrine reduces the defendant's duty. It is not abolished.
  • Jury supervision: Special verdicts are not mandatory. Jury supervision stays within the trial court's discretion.
  • Not reached: Plaintiff's equal protection challenge to contributory negligence.
  • Precedent superseded: The opinion supersedes the all-or-nothing rule applied since Innis v. The Steamer Senator, Gay v. Winter, and Needham v. S.F. & S.J. R. Co., along with the line of cases holding that only the Legislature could change it. It does not name specific cases as overruled.
  • Precedent relied on:
  • Hoffman v. Jones: judicial adoption of pure comparative negligence, and its caution against deciding unripe issues.
  • Needham: its statement that the bar existed only because apportionment was impossible.
  • Estate of Elizalde and In re Jessup: construction of the Civil Code.
  • Grey v. Fibreboard: assumption of risk.
  • Williams v. Carr: willful misconduct.
  • Stovall v. Denno: applying a new rule to the parties before the court.
  • Vincent v. Pabst (dissent): the case for the pure form.
  • Precedent distinguished or limited:
  • Westbrook v. Mihaly: the court gave its own decision non-prospective effect and stated it was not overruling Westbrook. Mosk disputes this.
  • Rowlands v. Christian: its statement that § 1714 is a civil law principle is correct as to the basic duty of care and incorrect as to defenses, which are of common law origin.
