Black-Letter Rule

The general right to make a contract about one's business, including the right to buy and sell labor, is part of the liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. A state may restrict that liberty under its police power only if the law is a fair, reasonable, and appropriate exercise of that power: the end must be appropriate and legitimate, and the means must have a direct relation to it. A health-based limit on working hours is valid only where there is some fair ground, reasonable in itself, to find material danger to the public health or to the employees' health. Whether a law falls within the police power is a question for the court, judged by the law's natural effect and not by its proclaimed purpose.

Facts

  • Statute: Section 110 of article 8, chapter 415 of the New York Laws of 1897 (the state "labor law") provided that no employee "shall be required or permitted to work" in a biscuit, bread, or cake bakery or confectionery establishment more than sixty hours in a week or ten hours in a day. The statute had no emergency exception.
  • Charge: The indictment charged that Lochner, an employer, "required and permitted" an employee working for him to work more than sixty hours in one week.
  • Voluntary labor: No state court read "required" to mean physical compulsion. All the opinions below treated "required" and "permitted" as equivalent, covering labor done under a voluntary contract.
  • Other sections of the act: Separate provisions regulated bakery conditions: inspection, washrooms and water closets apart from the bakeroom, drainage, plumbing, painting, ceiling height, and floors.
  • State's justifications: New York defended the hours limit as a health law. It argued that bakers' health was at risk, that the state has an interest in a robust population, and that less-overworked bakers would be cleaner and produce cleaner bread.

Procedural Posture

Lochner was convicted in the County Court of Oneida County. The Appellate Division of the New York Supreme Court affirmed, with two of five judges dissenting. The Court of Appeals affirmed, with three of seven dissenting, and upheld the act as a health law. The case reached the U.S. Supreme Court on a writ of error to the County Court.

Issue

Does a New York statute that bars bakery employees from working more than ten hours a day or sixty hours a week violate the liberty of contract protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment?

Holding

Yes. The hours limit is not a fair, reasonable, and appropriate exercise of the police power because it has no direct relation to, and no substantial effect on, public health or the health of bakers, so it unconstitutionally interferes with the liberty of contract of employers and employees; the judgments below were reversed and the case remanded to the County Court for further proceedings not inconsistent with the opinion (Peckham, J.).

Reasoning

  • Liberty of contract is protected. Relying on Allgeyer v. Louisiana, the Court held that the right to make contracts about one's business, and to buy or sell labor, is part of Fourteenth Amendment liberty. The right belongs to both parties: "The one has as much right to purchase as the other to sell labor."
  • Police power limits that liberty. States have police powers relating to safety, health, morals, and general welfare. Liberty and property are held subject to reasonable conditions imposed under those powers (Mugler v. Kansas; In re Kemmler; Crowley v. Christensen; In re Converse). A state may prohibit contracts it legitimately has power to prohibit.
  • Prior police-power cases distinguished. The Court acknowledged that it had upheld state statutes in many border cases but found none controlling. Holden v. Hardy upheld Utah's eight-hour law for underground mining and smelting, which contained an emergency exception and rested on the character of that work and those workers; "There is nothing in Holden v. Hardy which covers the case now before us." Atkin v. Kansas turned on the state's control of its municipal corporations and of public work. Knoxville Iron Co. v. Harbison involved miners at a wage disadvantage and a law that only required cashing coal orders. Jacobson v. Massachusetts upheld compulsory vaccination where a dangerous disease endangered public health and safety. The Court also noted Petit v. Minnesota as a proper exercise of the police power relating to Sunday observance, upholding a law treating barber shops open on Sunday as not a work of necessity or charity.
  • The police power has limits, and courts enforce them. Otherwise the Fourteenth Amendment would have no efficacy and "police power" would be a pretext for unlimited state sovereignty. The governing question is whether the law is "a fair, reasonable and appropriate exercise of the police power" or "an unreasonable, unnecessary and arbitrary interference" with personal liberty and freedom of contract.
  • The court disclaims second-guessing policy. If an act is within the police power, it is valid even if the court thinks it unwise. Whether it is within that power is a question the court must answer.
  • Means-end test. A remote relation to public health is not enough. The act must have "a more direct relation, as a means to an end, and the end itself must be appropriate and legitimate."
  • Rationale 1, labor law: rejected. Bakers are equal in intelligence and capacity to workers in other trades and can protect themselves. They are "in no sense wards of the State." As a pure labor law, the act does not touch public safety, morals, or welfare.
  • Rationale 2, health of bakers: rejected. Baking is not unhealthy to a degree that justifies interference. Almost every occupation affects health somewhat. If this law were valid, legislatures could limit hours for printers, carpenters, clerks, and professionals, and "no trade, no occupation" would escape regulation.
  • Rationale 3, robust population: rejected. This argument would make constitutional protection of liberty "visionary." It would let the state regulate the hours of employers, professionals, and athletes as well.
  • Rationale 4, cleanliness of bread: rejected. Clean bread does not depend on whether a baker works ten hours a day. The link between hours and bread quality is "too shadowy and thin." Drawing the line at ten hours is "unreasonable and entirely arbitrary."
  • Other sections already address health. The act's sanitation and inspection provisions "go to the full extent" of providing for healthy bakery conditions. Adding an hours cap is "so wholly beside the matter" of a reasonable provision that it violates liberty of contract.
  • Real purpose versus stated purpose. Courts judge a statute by its natural effect in operation, not its proclaimed purpose (Minnesota v. Barber; Brimmer v. Rebman; Yick Wo v. Hopkins). Strained health arguments raise "at least a suspicion" of some other legislative motive. The Court concluded the real object was simply to regulate hours between master and employees, "all being men, sui juris," in a private business not dangerous to morals or, in any real degree, to health.
  • State court support. The Court noted rising state interference with ordinary trades and cited state decisions striking down horseshoer licensing laws defended as health laws (People v. Beattie; In re Aubry; Bessette v. People), plus Godcharles v. Wigeman and Low v. Rees Printing Co., as upholding free contract in labor.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Harlan, J. (joined by White and Day, JJ.), dissenting. Liberty of contract is real but subject to reasonable regulation for the common good. A statute may be struck down only if it is "beyond question, plainly and palpably in excess of legislative power," or has "no real or substantial relation" to health, morals, or safety (Jacobson v. Massachusetts; Mugler). Doubt is resolved for validity, and the burden is on the challenger (McCulloch v. Maryland). Citing medical writers, a New York labor report, and international hours data, Harlan found reasons "of a weighty, substantial character" to think long bakery hours endanger health. Because the question is open to honest debate, the courts should defer. He suggested the law may also reflect unequal bargaining power between bakers and employers. He warned that the decision enlarges the Fourteenth Amendment beyond its purpose and cripples the states' power over health (Gibbons v. Ogden), and he quoted Atkin v. Kansas on judicial restraint. He would affirm.
  • Holmes, J., dissenting. The case "is decided upon an economic theory which a large part of the country does not entertain." A judge's agreement with that theory is irrelevant to a majority's right to enact its views into law. Many accepted laws restrict liberty of contract: Sunday laws, usury laws, lottery bans, school laws, Jacobson, Northern Securities, Otis v. Parker, and Holden v. Hardy. "The Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics," and a constitution "is not intended to embody a particular economic theory," whether paternalism or laissez faire. "General propositions do not decide concrete cases." "Liberty" is perverted when used to block the outcome of dominant opinion unless a rational and fair man would necessarily admit the statute infringes fundamental principles as understood by the traditions of our people and our law. A reasonable man might view this law as a health measure or as a first installment of general hours regulation. Holmes found it unnecessary to discuss whether the law would face an inequality challenge in that second aspect.

Notes

  • Scope of the holding. The Court struck down only the hours limitation in section 110. It said the act's sanitation and inspection provisions "may be wise and valid regulations" without deciding their validity.
  • Factual framing the Court relied on. The workers were adult men, "sui juris," in a private business. The statute had no emergency exception and was mandatory in all cases.
  • Relied on: Allgeyer v. Louisiana (liberty of contract); Mugler v. Kansas, In re Kemmler, Crowley v. Christensen, In re Converse (police power exists and conditions liberty); Minnesota v. Barber, Brimmer v. Rebman, Yick Wo v. Hopkins (judge a law by its natural effect, not its stated purpose).
  • Distinguished:
  • Holden v. Hardy: Utah's eight-hour law covered underground mining and smelting, contained an emergency exception, and turned on the character of that work and those workers. The Utah court's language quoted there expressly declined to decide whether hours could be fixed in other employments.
  • Atkin v. Kansas: decided on the state's control of its municipal corporations and conditions for public work.
  • Knoxville Iron Co. v. Harbison: the miners were at a wage disadvantage, and the law only required cashing coal orders.
  • Jacobson v. Massachusetts: compulsory vaccination against a dangerous disease that endangered public health and safety.
  • Petit v. Minnesota: noted as a proper police-power regulation relating to Sunday observance, upholding the legislature's declaration that keeping barber shops open on Sunday was not a work of necessity or charity.
  • Overruled: None.
  • Open questions. The Court did not attempt to define the exact limits of the police power. Holmes left open whether the statute, viewed as general hours regulation, would be vulnerable to an inequality challenge.
