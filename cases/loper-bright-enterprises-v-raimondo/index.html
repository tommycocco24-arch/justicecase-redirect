Black-Letter Rule

The APA requires courts to exercise independent judgment in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority. Courts may not defer to an agency's interpretation of the law simply because a statute is ambiguous, and Chevron is overruled. Courts may still look to agency interpretations for guidance under Skidmore. When the best reading of a statute is that it delegates discretionary authority, the court must respect the delegation, fix its boundaries, and ensure the agency engaged in reasoned decisionmaking within them.

Facts

  • Statute: The Magnuson-Stevens Act (MSA) lets fishery management plans require that observers be carried on domestic vessels to collect conservation data. NMFS administers the MSA for the Secretary of Commerce.
  • Express funding provisions: The MSA requires three groups to cover observer costs: foreign vessels in the exclusive economic zone, vessels in certain limited access privilege programs, and vessels under the North Pacific Council. For the latter two, it caps fees at two or three percent of the value of the harvest.
  • Statutory silence: The MSA says nothing about whether Atlantic herring fishermen may be required to pay for observers. NMFS once fully funded observer coverage in that fishery.
  • The Rule: In 2020, NMFS approved an amendment creating an industry-funded program targeting observer coverage on 50 percent of trips. If NMFS requires an observer but does not assign a government-paid one, the vessel must contract with and pay for a certified third-party observer. NMFS estimated the cost at up to $710 per day, reducing annual returns by up to 20 percent.
  • Parties: Petitioners are family businesses in the Atlantic herring fishery. The Relentless petitioners' vessels freeze fish at sea, take longer trips, and declare into multiple fisheries. They must carry an observer if selected even when they catch little or no herring.
  • Claim: Petitioners argued the MSA does not authorize NMFS to make them pay for observers.

Procedural Posture

In No. 22–451, the D.D.C. granted summary judgment to the Government, noting Chevron deference would apply if the statute were ambiguous. A divided D.C. Circuit affirmed at Chevron step two, with Judge Walker dissenting that the statute unambiguously barred the Rule. In No. 22–1219, the D.R.I. deferred under Chevron and granted summary judgment to the Government. The First Circuit affirmed without saying whether it ruled at step one or step two. The Court granted certiorari limited to whether Chevron should be overruled or clarified.

Issue

Does the APA permit a reviewing court to defer to an agency's permissible interpretation of an ambiguous statute the agency administers, as Chevron required?

Holding

No. The APA requires courts to exercise independent judgment on whether an agency acted within its statutory authority, and they may not defer to an agency's legal interpretation because a statute is ambiguous. Chevron is overruled, and the judgments of the D.C. and First Circuits are vacated and remanded. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for the Court, joined by Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Justice Jackson did not participate in No. 22–451.

Reasoning

  • Constitutional and historical baseline: Article III and The Federalist No. 78 make interpreting the laws the province of the courts. Marbury declared that "[i]t is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." Early cases gave executive interpretations "respect," especially when contemporaneous and consistent (Edwards' Lessee, Moore). That respect informed judicial judgment and never superseded it (Decatur, Dickson).
  • New Deal era: The Court treated agency factfinding as binding if supported by evidence (St. Joseph Stock Yards). It kept statutory interpretation "exclusively a judicial function" (American Trucking).
  • Skidmore let agency views carry weight according to the thoroughness of their consideration, the validity of their reasoning, their consistency with earlier and later pronouncements, and all factors that give them power to persuade.
  • Gray v. Powell and NLRB v. Hearst deferred only on factbound applications of broad terms. Hearst itself said questions of statutory interpretation are for the courts. Even that deference was inconsistent (Davies Warehouse).
  • APA § 706 text:
  • The reviewing court "shall decide all relevant questions of law" and "interpret constitutional and statutory provisions." That covers ambiguous laws too.
  • Section 706 prescribes deferential standards for policymaking (arbitrary and capricious, § 706(2)(A)) and factfinding (substantial evidence, § 706(2)(E)), and none for questions of law. The Court found that omission telling.
  • Section 706 treats statutory and constitutional provisions alike. Agency interpretations of the Constitution get no deference, so agency interpretations of statutes get none either.
  • APA history: The House and Senate Reports, prominent supporters, the Attorney General's Manual, and commentators (Dickinson, Schwartz, Jaffe) confirm that § 706 restated the traditional rule that courts decide questions of law.
  • Delegations survive as a matter of statutory meaning. The best reading of a statute may be that it grants discretion. The opinion identifies three forms:
  • Express delegation to give meaning to a particular term (Batterton; "as defined by regulations of the Secretary").
  • Authority to "fill up the details" of a statutory scheme (Wayman v. Southard).
  • Flexible terms such as "appropriate" or "reasonable" (Michigan v. EPA).

In each case, the court's job is to recognize constitutional delegations, fix their boundaries, and ensure reasoned decisionmaking within them (Michigan, Allentown Mack, State Farm).

  • **Chevron conflicts with the APA:**
  • Chevron never mentioned the APA.
  • It required courts to ignore the reading they would otherwise reach and to give binding deference even to inconsistent agency positions.
  • Under Brand X, an agency could displace a prior judicial construction.
  • The implied-delegation presumption fails:
  • Ambiguity is not delegation, and many ambiguities are unintentional.
  • In non-agency cases, courts resolve ambiguity by finding the best reading, and statutes have a single best meaning. "[I]f it is not the best, it is not permissible."
  • Agencies have no special competence in resolving statutory ambiguity. Courts do. Deference is least appropriate when the ambiguity concerns the scope of the agency's own power.
  • The Government's justifications are rejected:
  • Expertise: Courts handle technical questions and can draw on the agency's views under Skidmore. Those views may be especially informative to the extent they rest on factual premises within the agency's expertise (BATF v. FLRA).
  • Uniformity: It is unclear that Chevron produces uniformity, and a uniform wrong answer has little value.
  • Policy: Resolving ambiguity is legal interpretation. It does not become policymaking because an agency is available to fall back on (Kisor).
  • **Chevron's erosion:** The Court spent decades limiting the doctrine:
  • step zero (Mead, Encino Motorcars);
  • questions of deep economic and political significance (King v. Burwell, West Virginia v. EPA);
  • judicial review provisions (Adams Fruit);
  • statutes the agency does not administer (Epic Systems);
  • mixed signals on criminal statutes (Abramski, Babbitt).

Lower courts often bypassed the doctrine, and the Court has not deferred under Chevron since 2016.

  • Stare decisis. The factors are the quality of the reasoning, workability, and reliance (Knick, Janus). All three favor overruling.
  • Reasoning: Chevron ignored the APA, and Members of the Court long questioned it.
  • Workability: "Ambiguity" has no stable meaning ("How clear is clear?"), and repeated refinements added complexity.
  • Reliance: Constant tinkering defeated reliance, and Chevron destroyed it by letting agencies change positions at will.

Waiting for Congress to act was unnecessary.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Thomas, J. (concurring): Joins the opinion in full. He adds that Chevron also violates the separation of powers. It strips courts of the Article III judicial power to exercise independent judgment. It lets agencies exercise judicial power, or legislative power if recast as policy formulation, beyond Article II. He reasons that this deference is unconstitutional regardless of what a statute says.
  • Gorsuch, J. (concurring): Writes on stare decisis. Drawing on the common law and the Founders, he sets out three lessons:
  • Precedent cannot override the written law, and Chevron contravenes the APA.
  • Respect is owed to mainstream, time-tested decisions. Chevron broke from precedent and conflicts with judicial independence, due process (nemo iudex, fair notice), and the rule of lenity.
  • Opinions should not be read like statutes. Chevron's deference language was an unconsidered "stray" passage elevated by later cases.

He illustrates unworkability and harm to reliance with the Brand X rule changes, Buffington, and De Niz Robles.

  • Kagan, J. (dissenting), joined by Sotomayor, J., and by Jackson, J., as to No. 22–1219 only:
  • Implied delegation: Chevron rests on a sound presumption about congressional intent. When a statute has gaps or ambiguities, Congress would usually prefer the agency to resolve them because of agency expertise, regulatory experience, and political accountability for policy choices. Congress has almost never legislated around the presumption.
  • APA text: Section 706 is indeterminate. It prescribes no standard of review for legal questions, de novo or deferential, and a deferring court still "decide[s]" the legal question.
  • Pre-APA law: Section 706 restated a body of law that included deference under Gray and Hearst.
  • Stare decisis: Chevron deserves heightened protection because Congress could change it and because it is deeply embedded, applied by this Court at least 70 times and cited in thousands of lower-court decisions. Kisor refused to overrule Auer on similar grounds.
  • Workability and disruption: Ambiguity triggers are common across the law, and Skidmore "respect" will invite its own disputes. The overruling will cause a massive shock, and the majority's assurance about prior holdings is weak.

Notes

  • Overruled: Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837.
  • Prior holdings preserved: The Court does not call into question prior cases that relied on Chevron. Their holdings that specific agency actions are lawful, including Chevron's own Clean Air Act holding, remain subject to statutory stare decisis (CBOCS West). Mere reliance on Chevron is not a "special justification" for overruling such a holding (Halliburton, Dickerson).
  • Merits not reached: Certiorari was limited to the Chevron question. The Court did not decide whether the MSA authorizes the Rule and remanded because the lower courts relied on Chevron.
  • Agency views still count: Under Skidmore, agency interpretations may inform judgment. Contemporaneous and consistent interpretations may be especially useful.
  • Delegations still count: Courts must respect statutory delegations of discretion, police their outer boundaries, and review for reasoned decisionmaking. The Court cites State Farm and Michigan v. EPA for that last task.
  • Distinguished: The Court describes Gray v. Powell and NLRB v. Hearst as factbound applications of broad terms, inconsistently applied, that did not change the rule that courts decide questions of statutory interpretation. The dissent argues the majority's pure-law versus mixed-question distinction is unworkable. It says it would welcome a holding confined to pure legal questions but suspects the majority intends no such limit. The majority does not adopt that limit expressly.
  • Criticized: Brand X, for letting agencies override prior judicial constructions, and Smiley's implied-delegation presumption, which the Court calls a fiction.
  • Relied on:
  • Marbury, Skidmore, American Trucking, Decatur, Dickson;
  • Kisor, for the points that interpretive issues may fall more naturally into a judge's bailiwick and that legal interpretation does not become policymaking because an agency is available;
  • BATF v. FLRA, Michigan v. EPA, Allentown Mack, State Farm;
  • Knick and Janus, for the stare decisis factors.
  • *Former Chevron limits: Mead, Encino Motorcars, King v. Burwell, West Virginia v. EPA, Adams Fruit, and Epic Systems are described as limitations on Chevron*. The Court cites them as evidence of its unworkability.
  • Auer deference not addressed: The majority does not address Auer deference to agency interpretations of their own regulations. The dissent invokes Kisor's refusal to overrule Auer as a comparison point.
