Black-Letter Rule

A permanent physical occupation of property authorized by government is a taking, without regard to the public interests it may serve or how small its economic impact on the owner. The rule applies whether the occupant is the State or a third party the State authorizes. Physical invasions short of permanent occupation, and regulations restricting use, stay under the Penn Central multifactor balancing inquiry.

Facts

  • Parties: Jean Loretto owned a five-story apartment building in Manhattan. Teleprompter provided cable television (CATV) service. The City of New York, which gave Teleprompter an exclusive franchise, intervened.
  • Installation: With the prior owner's permission, Teleprompter installed a cable slightly under half an inch in diameter and about 30 feet long, 4" x 4" x 4" directional taps, and two large silver boxes on the roof. Screws or nails penetrated the masonry every two feet or so, and other equipment was bolted on. Loretto bought the building in 1971 and found out about the cable only after the purchase.
  • Crossover and noncrossover: The roof cables first served as a "crossover" carrying service to other buildings. Two years after Loretto bought the building, Teleprompter ran a "noncrossover" line down the front of the building to serve her tenants.
  • Statute: Before 1973, Teleprompter paid owners 5% of gross revenues from the property. N.Y. Exec. Law § 828, effective January 1, 1973, barred landlords from interfering with CATV installation and from demanding payment above what the State Commission set as reasonable. Landlords could require the CATV company or tenant to bear installation costs and to indemnify for damage.
  • Fee: The Commission set a one-time $1 payment as the normal fee, with more available on a "special showing of greater damages."
  • Suit: In 1976 Loretto brought a class action for trespass and, insofar as Teleprompter relied on § 828, a taking without just compensation. She sought damages and an injunction.

Procedural Posture

The New York trial court (Supreme Court, Special Term) granted summary judgment to Teleprompter and the city, upholding § 828 for both crossover and noncrossover installations. The Appellate Division affirmed without opinion, and the Court of Appeals, over a dissent, upheld the statute under a balancing analysis. The U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Is a minor but permanent physical occupation of an owner's property, authorized by state law, a taking that requires just compensation under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments?

Holding

Yes. A permanent physical occupation authorized by government is a taking regardless of the public interest served or the economic impact, so § 828's authorization of Teleprompter's installation took a portion of Loretto's property. The judgment was reversed and remanded for the state courts to determine compensation (Justice Marshall for the Court; Justice Blackmun, joined by Justices Brennan and White, dissenting).

Reasoning

  • Police power is a separate question. The Court accepted that § 828 serves a legitimate public purpose within the police power. Whether a valid regulation so frustrates property rights that compensation is due is a different inquiry.
  • Character of the action is determinative here. Penn Central calls for ad hoc inquiry into economic impact, interference with investment-backed expectations, and the character of the government action. Physical invasion has always weighed heavily under the third factor. When the invasion reaches the extreme form of permanent physical occupation, that factor decides the case.
  • Historical line of cases:
  • Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co. held that permanent flooding from a state-authorized dam was a taking.
  • Northern Transportation Co. v. Chicago found no taking from a temporary dam that only impaired access, because there was no entry on the land.
  • The flooding cases (Lynah, Bedford, Cress, Sanguinetti, Kansas City Life) found a taking only for permanent occupation. Temporary invasions and consequential damage did not qualify.
  • St. Louis v. Western Union Telegraph Co. treated telegraph poles as an exclusive and permanent use requiring compensation. Western Union v. Pennsylvania R. Co. assumed telegraph lines over a right-of-way required compensation. State cases treated small installations such as lines, rails, and pipes as takings.
  • Modern cases preserve the line:
  • Causby found a taking from direct overflights.
  • Pewee Coal found a taking from government possession and operation of a mine. Central Eureka found none from a wartime order closing gold mines, because the government took no physical possession.
  • Penn Central, Kaiser Aetna, and PruneYard subject physical invasions to balancing. None suggests a permanent occupation could escape the Takings Clause.
  • Bundle-of-rights rationale. Permanent occupation "chops through the bundle, taking a slice of every strand." It defeats each right General Motors lists:
  • Possession: The owner can neither possess the occupied space nor exclude the occupier. The Court called the power to exclude one of the most treasured strands (citing Kaiser Aetna).
  • Use: The owner can make no use of the space.
  • Disposition: The bare right to sell is emptied of value, since a buyer also cannot use the space.
  • Special injury. Property law protects an owner's expectation of being undisturbed in possession. Forcing the owner to suffer a stranger's complete dominion "adds insult to injury." The owner also may have no control over the timing, extent, or nature of the invasion.
  • Administrability.
  • The rule avoids line-drawing by size: a mandated rooftop swimming pool would plainly be a taking, and protection cannot turn on the area occupied.
  • The fact of occupation is easy to prove.
  • Extent of occupation matters when setting compensation, so it need not be weighed on liability.
  • Application.
  • The attached plates, boxes, wires, bolts, and screws completely occupied space on and above the roof and along the exterior wall.
  • Crossover and noncrossover installations do not differ constitutionally, since both permanently appropriate the owner's property.
  • Rejected objections:
  • Rental-only limit: Applying the law only to rental buildings does not make the occupation any less physical. It is permanent because the landlord must permit it as long as the building stays residential and the company wants it there.
  • Tenant appurtenance: § 828 gives tenants no enforceable property right, and Teleprompter owns the equipment. A State cannot by "ipse dixit" turn private property into public property (Webb's Fabulous Pharmacies).
  • Landlord-tenant power: The regulations upheld in Block v. Hirsh, Bowles v. Willingham, Queenside Hills, Blaisdell, Levy Leasing, and Heart of Atlanta did not authorize permanent occupation by a third party.
  • Use-dependency (fn.): A landlord's ability to rent cannot be conditioned on forfeiting compensation for a physical occupation. Teleprompter's argument would let the government requisition apartments for offices or fill a building with others' vending and washing machines.
  • Private occupant (fn.): Penn Central's reference to invasion "by government" means only that, for invasions short of permanent occupation, government's direct benefit is one relevant factor.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Blackmun (joined by Justices Brennan and White), dissenting:
  • Formalism. Takings law has no set formula (Goldblatt, Penn Central, Pennsylvania Coal). The per se rule reduces the issue to a "formalistic quibble" over permanent occupation versus temporary invasion.
  • Outdated precedent. The 19th-century cases belong to an agrarian context and suit the modern urban age poorly.
  • Each element is unstable:
  • The installation is not truly permanent, because it lasts only while the building is rental property and Teleprompter keeps it there.
  • The third-party distinction cannot be decisive, since temporary third-party invasions get balancing.
  • Ownership of the cable is incidental, and even a landlord-owned cable might invite claims that a signal passing through it is a "physical" touching.
  • Temporary invasions such as Block v. Hirsh, PruneYard, Kaiser Aetna, and Causby intruded far more than this long-unnoticed cable, yet none got a per se rule.
  • Bundle-of-rights reply.
  • Disposition: A non-rental buyer can remove the cable, and the statute likely raises resale and rental value.
  • Use: New York already requires landlords to provide and pay for larger fixtures such as mailboxes. Loretto admitted she had no other use for the space.
  • Exclusion: The majority assumes tenants have no countervailing interest, and legislatures may revise common-law rights like trespass (Munn; Marshall's own PruneYard concurrence).
  • Balancing. The Court of Appeals properly applied the balancing test. The economic impact was de minimis and there was no interference with investment-backed expectations, since Loretto bought without knowing of the cable.
  • Crossover. The dissent agrees there is no constitutional difference between crossover and noncrossover lines. Even if the crossover extension were a taking, the dissent would treat it as de minimis and noncompensable.
  • Compensation. The dissent welcomes leaving compensation open and suggests the result may be a taking deserving little or nothing.

Notes

  • Narrow holding. The Court called its holding "very narrow." It reaffirms the traditional rule and does not question the State's broad power to restrict an owner's use of property.
  • Compensation left open. The Court expressed no opinion on the amount owed and remanded that issue to the state courts. It did not presuppose that the pre-statute 5% fee is the proper measure. The extent of the occupation, and arguments about increased resale value or lack of alternative use, may bear on compensation.
  • Due process not reached. The Court did not address Loretto's due process claim.
  • State-law rights left open. The Court declined to decide the landlord's and tenant's rights under state law, before § 828, to the occupied space.
  • Landlord-owned installation. A statute requiring landlords to provide cable themselves "might present a different question," because ownership would give the landlord control over placement, manner, and use.
  • Building codes. Building codes, utility connections, mailboxes, smoke detectors, fire extinguishers, and similar requirements in common areas remain under Penn Central balancing, as long as they do not require physical occupation by a third party.
  • Temporary invasions. PruneYard, Kaiser Aetna, the intermittent flooding cases, and the labor-access cases (Babcock & Wilcox, Central Hardware, Hudgens) involve temporary, limited intrusions on the right to exclude. Those intrusions get balancing. The Court analogized to antitrust, where a per se rule survives even though its boundary calls for rule-of-reason analysis.
  • Size irrelevant. Whether the installation is a taking "does not depend on whether the volume of space it occupies is bigger than a breadbox." The Court disputed the dissent's one-eighth cubic foot figure because it left out the silver boxes.
  • Airspace. Prior occupation of the space is irrelevant, because a landowner owns at least as much airspace as can be used in connection with the land (Causby, citing Butler v. Frontier Telephone Co.).
  • Precedent treatment:
  • Relied on: Pumpelly, St. Louis v. Western Union, Western Union v. Pennsylvania R. Co., the permanent-flooding cases, Causby, Pewee Coal, Kaiser Aetna (right to exclude), General Motors (possess, use, dispose), Andrus v. Allard (bundle metaphor), and Webb's Fabulous Pharmacies.
  • Distinguished: Northern Transportation, Central Eureka, PruneYard, the labor-access cases, Andrus v. Allard (no physical invasion), and the landlord-tenant regulation cases.
  • Reconciled: Penn Central. The Court read it as consistent with the per se rule.
  • Overruled: None.
