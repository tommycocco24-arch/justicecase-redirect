Black-Letter Rule

Under the federal-question jurisdiction statute, a suit "arises under the Constitution and laws of the United States" only when the plaintiff's statement of his own cause of action shows that it is based on federal law. Alleging an anticipated defense, and then alleging that federal law defeats that defense, does not create federal-question jurisdiction. Federal courts must police the statutory limits of their jurisdiction on their own motion, even when neither party raises the issue.

Elements of the test:

  1. Look only to the plaintiff's statement of his own cause of action.
  2. That statement must show the claim is based on the Constitution or laws of the United States.
  3. Allegations about the defendant's expected defense, and the plaintiff's federal answer to it, are disregarded. This holds even if a federal question will "very likely" arise during the litigation.

Facts

  • Underlying contract: Before Congress passed the act, the plaintiffs had accepted in good faith a contract for transportation from the railroad. They took it in satisfaction of a valid cause of action they held against the railroad.
  • Federal statute: The Act of June 29, 1906 (34 Stat. 584) forbids railroads to give free passes or to collect any compensation for passenger transportation other than the rate in the filed tariff.
  • Federal questions raised by the demurrer: The demurrer to the bill raised two questions of law. The first was whether the 1906 Act makes it unlawful to perform the pre-Act contract. The second was whether the Act, if read that way, violates the Fifth Amendment.
  • Jurisdictional basis: The parties were not diverse. The only possible ground for federal jurisdiction was that the suit arose under the Constitution and laws of the United States (Act of August 13, 1888, c. 866, 25 Stat. 433, 434).
  • No jurisdictional challenge: Neither party questioned the Circuit Court's jurisdiction.

Procedural Posture

The plaintiffs filed a bill in the federal Circuit Court, and the railroad demurred. The two questions of law raised by the demurrer came to the Supreme Court on appeal.

Issue

Did the Circuit Court have federal-question jurisdiction over a suit where the only federal issues were the plaintiff's allegations that the defendant would rely on a federal statute as a defense and that the statute, so applied, was invalid?

Holding

No. A suit arises under federal law only when the plaintiff's own statement of his cause of action shows it is based on federal law, and an anticipated federal defense does not meet that standard. The Court (Moody, J.) reversed and remitted the case to the Circuit Court with instructions to dismiss for want of jurisdiction. The opinion text does not state the vote.

Reasoning

  • Duty to raise jurisdiction sua sponte: A statute defines and limits the Circuit Court's jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has a duty to make sure that jurisdiction is not exceeded, and it has "frequently performed" that duty of its own motion. The Court cited Mansfield, &c. Railway Co. v. Swan, King Bridge Co. v. Otoe County, Blacklock v. Small, Cameron v. Hodges, Metcalf v. Watertown, and Continental National Bank v. Buford.
  • Only ground available: Without diversity, jurisdiction depended entirely on the "arising under" language of the 1888 Act.
  • Settled reading of the statute: The Court treated the rule as the "settled interpretation" of the statutory words. A suit arises under federal law only when the plaintiff's statement of his own cause of action shows it is based on federal law.
  • Anticipated defenses don't count: Such allegations may show that a federal question will likely come up in the litigation. They do not show that the plaintiff's original cause of action arises under federal law.
  • **Tennessee v. Union & Planters' Bank:** Tennessee sued in federal Circuit Court to recover taxes alleged to be due under state law. Tennessee alleged that the bank claimed an immunity from the taxation by virtue of its charter, and that therefore the tax was void as violating the Contract Clause. That case was held outside federal jurisdiction: "a suggestion of one party, that the other will or may set up a claim under the Constitution or laws of the United States, does not make the suit one arising under that Constitution or those laws."
  • **Boston & Montana Consolidated Copper & Silver Mining Co. v. Montana Ore Purchasing Co.:** The plaintiff sued for conversion of ore and alleged that the defendant would rely on federal law. That case was also held outside federal jurisdiction. The Court quoted Justice Peckham's pleading rationale:
  • Pleading the defendant's defense and then answering it is "inconsistent with any known rule of pleading" and "improper."
  • The plaintiff should be "confined to a statement of its cause of action." The defendant raises its own defense in its answer and bears the burden of proving it.
  • Line of authority: The Court said Metcalf v. Watertown first announced the interpretation. It listed a long series of later cases repeating and applying it, including Colorado Central Consolidated Mining Co. v. Turck, Chappell v. Waterworth, Joy v. City of St. Louis, and Devine v. Los Angeles.
  • Application: The rule was "decisive against the jurisdiction of the Circuit Court."

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Merits not reached: The Court expressly declined to decide either merits question. It left open whether the 1906 Act bars performing a pre-Act settlement contract for transportation, and whether the Act would violate the Fifth Amendment if read that way.
  • Statutory interpretation: The Court framed the rule as the interpretation of "these words, as used in this statute, conferring jurisdiction," meaning the Act of August 13, 1888.
  • Disposition: The case was dismissed for want of jurisdiction. The Court did not decide it for either side on the merits.
  • Sua sponte review: Party silence or consent did not save jurisdiction. The Court relied on its prior practice of raising jurisdiction on its own, citing Mansfield, &c. Railway Co. v. Swan and the other cases listed above.
  • Precedents relied on: The Court relied principally on Tennessee v. Union & Planters' Bank, 152 U.S. 454, and Boston & Montana Consolidated Copper & Silver Mining Co. v. Montana Ore Purchasing Co., 188 U.S. 632. It identified Metcalf v. Watertown, 128 U.S. 586, as the origin of the rule.
  • Overruled or distinguished: The opinion overrules and distinguishes nothing.
