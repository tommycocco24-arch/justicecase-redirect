Black-Letter Rule

A statute that classifies by race does not escape the Equal Protection Clause because it punishes members of each race equally. Racial classifications, especially in criminal statutes, receive the "most rigid scrutiny" and must be shown necessary to accomplish a permissible state objective independent of racial discrimination. The freedom to marry is a vital personal right and one of the "basic civil rights of man," and restricting it on the basis of racial classifications also violates the Due Process Clause.

Facts

  • Parties: Mildred Jeter, a Black woman, and Richard Loving, a white man, both Virginia residents.
  • Marriage: They married in the District of Columbia in June 1958 under its laws, then returned to Virginia and set up their marital home in Caroline County.
  • Prosecution: A grand jury indicted them for violating Virginia's ban on interracial marriage. They pleaded guilty and received one-year jail sentences, suspended for 25 years on the condition that they leave Virginia and not return together for 25 years.
  • Trial judge's rationale: The judge wrote that God "placed [the races] on separate continents" and "did not intend for the races to mix."
  • Statutes: They were convicted under § 20-58 (leaving the state to evade the ban, then returning and cohabiting) and § 20-59 (felony punishment for intermarriage). The scheme also included § 20-57, which voided interracial marriages without any judicial proceeding, and §§ 20-54 and 1-14, which defined "white persons" and "colored persons."
  • Scope of the ban: The scheme, rooted in the Racial Integrity Act of 1924, barred only white persons from marrying anyone other than a white person. Other races could intermarry without statutory interference.
  • Undisputed classification: The Lovings never disputed that each fell within the statutory racial definitions.

Procedural Posture

After the trial court denied the Lovings' motion to vacate their convictions on Fourteenth Amendment grounds, the Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia upheld the statutes, modified the sentence, and affirmed the convictions. The Lovings appealed, and the U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Does a state statutory scheme that prohibits and punishes marriage solely on the basis of racial classifications violate the Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment?

Holding

Yes. Virginia's antimiscegenation statutes rest solely on racial classifications that serve no legitimate overriding purpose independent of invidious racial discrimination, and they deprive the Lovings of the fundamental freedom to marry without due process, so the convictions are reversed (opinion by Chief Justice Warren; the opinion text does not state the vote).

Reasoning

  • State police power has limits. Marriage is a social relation subject to state police power (Maynard v. Hill). That power is still bounded by the Fourteenth Amendment (Meyer v. Nebraska, Skinner v. Oklahoma). Virginia did not argue otherwise before the Court.
  • "Equal application" theory rejected. Virginia argued the statutes were valid because they punished white and Black participants equally. The Court held that equal application does not remove a racial classification from the Fourteenth Amendment's prohibition on invidious racial discrimination.
  • Rational basis review does not apply. Deferential review applies to non-racial distinctions like truck advertising (Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York) or tax exemptions (Allied Stores of Ohio, Inc. v. Bowers). Racial classifications carry "the very heavy burden of justification" the Fourteenth Amendment requires. So the Court refused to defer to the legislature on supposedly doubtful scientific evidence.
  • Legislative history inconclusive. Statements in the Thirty-ninth Congress about the Freedmen's Bureau Bill and the Civil Rights Act of 1866 concerned specific statutes and did not settle the Amendment's broader purpose. Quoting Brown v. Board of Education, the Court called the historical sources "inconclusive." McLaughlin v. Florida had already rejected the State's reading of those debates.
  • **Pace v. Alabama rejected.* Pace upheld a harsher penalty for interracial adultery or fornication because each participant was punished the same. The Court repeated McLaughlin's statement that Pace* "represents a limited view of the Equal Protection Clause which has not withstood analysis."
  • Central purpose of the Amendment. The Fourteenth Amendment's "clear and central purpose" was to eliminate all official state sources of invidious racial discrimination (Slaughter-House Cases, Strauder, Ex parte Virginia, Shelley v. Kraemer, Burton v. Wilmington Parking Authority).
  • Strict scrutiny applied. Distinctions based solely on ancestry are "odious to a free people" (Hirabayashi). Racial classifications, "especially suspect in criminal statutes," get "the most rigid scrutiny" (Korematsu). To survive, they must be necessary to a permissible state objective independent of racial discrimination.
  • No legitimate purpose. The statutes criminalize generally accepted conduct only when the participants are of different races. Because Virginia barred only interracial marriages involving white persons, the classifications stood as "measures designed to maintain White Supremacy." The Court also described Naim v. Naim, the state precedent relied on below, as "obviously an endorsement of the doctrine of White Supremacy."
  • Due process. The freedom to marry is "one of the vital personal rights essential to the orderly pursuit of happiness by free men" and one of the "basic civil rights of man," fundamental to existence and survival (Skinner; see Maynard). Denying it on a basis as unsupportable as racial classification deprives citizens of liberty without due process. The choice whether to marry a person of another race "resides with the individual and cannot be infringed by the State."

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Stewart (concurring in the judgment). Stewart restated his McLaughlin view that "it is simply not possible for a state law to be valid under our Constitution which makes the criminality of an act depend upon the race of the actor." He concurred in the judgment on that basis. His position is categorical and goes beyond the majority's scrutiny framework, which still asks whether a racial classification is necessary to a permissible objective.

Notes

  • Two independent grounds. The Court rested its judgment on both equal protection (Part I) and due process (Part II). Either ground supports reversal.
  • Due process ground tied to race. The due process analysis describes marriage as fundamental. Its specific holding is that the freedom to marry cannot be restricted by "invidious racial discriminations." The opinion does not set out a general test for other marriage restrictions.
  • Expressly left open. The appellants argued that protecting only the "integrity" of the white race made the scheme arbitrary even if a racial-integrity purpose were valid. The Court did not reach this argument. It held the classifications unconstitutional "even assuming an even-handed state purpose to protect the 'integrity' of all races."
  • State power over marriage acknowledged. The Court agreed that marriage is subject to state police power (Maynard). The Tenth Amendment argument from Naim did not displace Fourteenth Amendment limits.
  • Rejected: Pace v. Alabama (reasoning rejected, following McLaughlin v. Florida); Naim v. Naim (state decision relied on below).
  • Distinguished: Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York and Allied Stores of Ohio, Inc. v. Bowers (rational basis cases involving non-racial classifications).
  • Relied on: Maynard v. Hill; Meyer v. Nebraska; Skinner v. Oklahoma; Brown v. Board of Education; Strauder v. West Virginia; McLaughlin v. Florida; Slaughter-House Cases; Ex parte Virginia; Shelley v. Kraemer; Burton v. Wilmington Parking Authority; Hirabayashi v. United States; Korematsu v. United States.
  • Context in the footnotes. Virginia was one of 16 states with such bans. Fourteen states had repealed theirs in the prior 15 years. Perez v. Sharp was the first state court decision to hold miscegenation statutes violate equal protection.
