Black-Letter Rule

A regulation that denies all economically beneficial or productive use of land is a categorical taking. The government must pay compensation without any case-specific inquiry into the public interest behind the regulation. The State escapes compensation only if it shows that background principles of its own property and nuisance law already barred the prohibited use, so that the use was never part of the owner's title. A legislative declaration that the use is harmful is not enough.

Facts

  • Purchase: In 1986 Lucas paid $976,000 for two residential lots on the Isle of Palms, South Carolina. He planned to build single-family homes, as owners of the adjacent parcels had already done, and commissioned architectural drawings.
  • Prior law: The lots were about 300 feet from the beach and outside the "critical area" of the 1977 Coastal Zone Management Act. No permit was required when Lucas bought. Both lots were zoned for single-family residential use with no restrictions.
  • The 1988 Act: The Beachfront Management Act directed the Council to draw a "baseline." The Council drew it landward of Lucas's lots. The Act flatly banned occupable improvements seaward of a line 20 feet landward of the baseline, with no exceptions. Only minor nonhabitable structures, such as small wooden walkways and decks, were allowed.
  • Lucas's theory: Lucas did not challenge the Act as a valid exercise of the police power. He argued that the Act wiped out his lots' value, so compensation was owed whatever the State's purposes.
  • Trial findings: The trial court found that the ban was permanent as to Lucas's lots and rendered them "valueless."
  • Legislative findings: The Act recited that the beach/dune system protects life and property from storms, supports tourism, provides habitat, and offers a healthy leisure environment. It also found that development sited too close to the system accelerates erosion and endangers adjacent property.
  • 1990 amendment: While the case was pending in the state supreme court, the Act was amended to let the Council issue discretionary "special permits" for habitable construction seaward of the baseline.

Procedural Posture

After a bench trial, the South Carolina Court of Common Pleas found a taking and awarded $1,232,387.50. The Supreme Court of South Carolina reversed, over a two-justice dissent, holding that no compensation is owed for a regulation designed to prevent serious public harm. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does a land-use regulation that deprives an owner of all economically beneficial use of land effect a compensable taking, even when the legislature declares that the regulation prevents serious public harm?

Holding

Yes. A regulation that deprives land of all economically beneficial use is a taking unless the State shows that background principles of its nuisance and property law already prohibited the intended use. The judgment was reversed and remanded for the state court to decide that state-law question. Justice Scalia wrote for the Court. The opinion text does not state the vote.

Reasoning

  • Ripeness: The 1990 special-permit amendment did not make the case unripe.
  • The South Carolina Supreme Court decided the merits rather than ripeness.
  • Its judgment would bar any claim for the 1988–1990 period, and temporary takings are compensable under First English.
  • The Council had stipulated that no permit would have issued under the 1988 Act, so applying for one would have been pointless.
  • The special-permit procedure goes only to prudential ripeness, and the Court declined to apply that requirement here.
  • Origins of regulatory takings: Before Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon, the Takings Clause was thought to reach only direct appropriations or the practical ouster of possession. Mahon held that regulation that "goes too far" is a taking. Since then the Court has generally used ad hoc, factual inquiries (Penn Central).
  • Two categorical rules: The Court identified two categories that are compensable without case-specific inquiry into the public interest:
  • Permanent physical invasions, however small (Loretto; see Causby, Kaiser Aetna).
  • Regulations that deny all economically beneficial or productive use of land (Agins, Nollan, Keystone, Hodel).
  • Why total deprivations are categorical:
  • From the owner's point of view, a total deprivation may be the equivalent of a physical appropriation.
  • It is less realistic to assume the legislature is merely "adjusting the benefits and burdens of economic life" with an "average reciprocity of advantage."
  • The Mahon rationale that government "could not go on" if it had to pay for every diminution in value does not apply to these rare cases.
  • Requiring land to stay in its natural state carries a heightened risk that private property is being "pressed into some form of public service" under the guise of preventing harm. Statutes that use eminent domain to buy scenic easements show that negative regulation and appropriation are practically equivalent here.
  • Rejecting the harm/benefit test: The state court relied on the Mugler line (Mugler, Hadacheck, Miller v. Schoene, Goldblatt). The Court gave three reasons why that line does not decide the case:
  • The "noxious use" principle was only an early way of describing the police power. Later cases (Penn Central, Nollan) recast it as whether regulation "substantially advances legitimate state interests."
  • Whether a regulation "prevents harm" or "confers benefits" is in the eye of the beholder. It cannot be decided on an "objective, value-free basis."
  • A legislature's recited harm-prevention rationale therefore cannot excuse a total taking. If it could, the exception would swallow the rule and nullify Mahon's limits.
  • None of the noxious-use cases involved an allegation that the regulation wholly eliminated the land's value.
  • The background-principles exception: The State may resist compensation only if the "logically antecedent inquiry into the nature of the owner's estate" shows the prohibited use was never part of the title.
  • The regulation must "do no more than duplicate the result that could have been achieved in the courts." That means a private nuisance suit by neighbors, the State's power to abate public nuisances, or "otherwise," which a footnote explains chiefly means the doctrine of actual necessity, such as destroying property to stop a fire (Bowditch).
  • The Court drew an analogy to physical occupation. Government can assert a pre-existing navigational servitude (Scranton v. Wheeler) but must pay for a new one (Kaiser Aetna).
  • Two illustrations of no compensation: a lakebed owner denied a permit to fill land in a way that would flood others' land, and a nuclear plant sitting astride an earthquake fault.
  • Property interests are defined by "existing rules or understandings that stem from an independent source such as state law" (Board of Regents v. Roth).
  • Land versus personal property: Owners expect new police-power regulation to restrict use from time to time. For personal property, given the State's traditionally high control over commerce, an owner should be aware that regulation might make the property worthless (Andrus v. Allard). For land, an implied limitation that the State may later eliminate all economically valuable use is "inconsistent with the historical compact recorded in the Takings Clause."
  • Factors for the nuisance inquiry: The Court drew on the Restatement (Second) of Torts:
  • The degree of harm the claimant's proposed activity poses to public lands and resources or to adjacent private property.
  • The social value of the claimant's activity and its suitability to the locality.
  • The relative ease with which the harm can be avoided by the claimant, the government, or adjacent owners.
  • Whether similarly situated owners have long engaged in the use. Long use ordinarily shows there is no common-law prohibition, though changed circumstances or new knowledge may change that.
  • Whether other similarly situated owners are still allowed the use denied to the claimant.
  • Burden on remand: South Carolina must identify background principles of nuisance and property law that prohibit Lucas's intended uses "in the circumstances in which the property is presently found."
  • Conclusory invocation of sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas or a legislative declaration will not do. A "State, by ipse dixit, may not transform private property into public property without compensation" (Webb's Fabulous Pharmacies).
  • The defense requires an "objectively reasonable application of relevant precedents."
  • The Court said it "seems unlikely" common-law principles would bar all habitable improvements on the lots, since they rarely prohibit the "essential use" of land (Curtin v. Benson). It left the question to the state court.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Kennedy (concurring in the judgment):
  • The Court does not decide the permanent-taking claim. It also does not stop the state court from requiring Lucas to pursue the new special-permit procedure.
  • The temporary-taking claim is ready for review, and any uncertainty is the State's fault for amending the Act. On remand the court must consider whether Lucas had the intent and capacity to develop during 1988–1990 and was prevented by the State.
  • He called the "valueless" finding "curious" but accepted it as entered.
  • The test should be whether the total deprivation is contrary to the owner's reasonable, investment-backed expectations, read in light of "the whole of our legal tradition." Common-law nuisance is "too narrow a confine." Coastal land's fragility may justify regulation beyond what nuisance law permits.
  • The state court erred by reciting general purposes without asking whether they matched Lucas's reasonable expectations. Promoting tourism alone should not justify a total deprivation without compensation. The State also acted only after most lots were developed, throwing the whole burden on the remaining lots.
  • Justice Blackmun (dissenting):
  • "The Court launches a missile to kill a mouse."
  • The claim was unripe and nonjusticiable because Lucas never used administrative procedures to challenge the setback line. He also showed no concrete plans to build, citing Lujan.
  • The "valueless" finding is almost certainly wrong. Lucas can still exclude others, picnic, camp, or sell the land.
  • The Court reversed the usual presumption of constitutionality and shifted the burden to the State.
  • Mugler, Hadacheck, Miller, and Goldblatt upheld harm-preventing regulations regardless of economic effect and without regard to common-law nuisance.
  • Nuisance law is as value-laden as the harm/benefit line the Court rejects, and the denominator question has no objective answer.
  • History does not support the Court's "historical compact."
  • Justice Stevens (dissenting):
  • Judicial restraint (Ashwander) counseled against deciding the merits, and Lucas may have suffered no injury in fact.
  • The categorical rule is arbitrary: a 95% loss recovers nothing while a 100% loss recovers fully.
  • The rule invites manipulation of the denominator, either through courts defining property broadly or investors creating narrow estates.
  • The nuisance exception rejects Mugler's holding and "freezes" the common law, denying legislatures power to respond to new understandings about the environment.
  • The Court ignores the "character of the governmental action" factor. The Act is general and statewide, burdens developed and undeveloped owners alike, and serves the compelling purpose of protecting life and property. On that basis, he would find no taking even assuming the lots were valueless.
  • Justice Souter (statement):
  • He would dismiss the writ as improvidently granted.
  • The unreviewable assumption of total deprivation is "highly questionable," so the Court cannot clarify what total deprivation means. That leaves the scope of the nuisance exception uncertain too.
  • Nuisance law targets conduct, and its remedies usually leave other reasonable uses. A nuisance-based regulation will rarely cause a complete deprivation in fact.

Notes

  • Limits of the holding:
  • The categorical rule applies only to total deprivations of economically beneficial use of land.
  • Owners short of a total loss are not shut out. They proceed under Penn Central, where economic impact and interference with distinct investment-backed expectations remain "keenly relevant."
  • The Court did not decide whether Lucas actually suffered a taking. It remanded the background-principles question to the state court.
  • Expressly left open:
  • The denominator problem: For example, does a rule leaving 90% of a tract in its natural state take all use of the burdened portion, or merely diminish the whole tract? The Court suggested the answer may turn on how state property law has recognized the particular interest. It avoided the issue because Lucas pleaded a fee simple and the trial court found each lot valueless.
  • The permanent-taking claim: Lucas may still apply for a special permit under the 1990 amendment and challenge any denial.
  • Other issues on remand: The questions the state court did not reach.
  • Accepted findings: The Court accepted the "valueless" finding because the Council did not challenge it in its brief in opposition to certiorari (Oklahoma City v. Tuttle).
  • Scope of protected interests: The focus on developmental uses does not mean noneconomic interests in land, such as the right to exclude (Loretto), go unprotected.
  • Remedy: The State may rescind the regulation to avoid paying for a permanent taking. It must still pay for the period the taking was in effect (First English).
  • Generally applicable laws: The Court suggested that a law that destroys land value without being aimed at land, such as the criminal liquor ban in Mugler, perhaps cannot be a compensable taking. A regulation aimed at land use gets no immunity just because it burdens landowners generally.
  • Precedents relied on: Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon; Penn Central (ad hoc test, and the reading of the Mugler line in n. 30); Agins, Nollan, Keystone, and Hodel (the "economically viable use" statements); Loretto, Causby, and Kaiser Aetna (physical invasions); First English (temporary takings); Board of Regents v. Roth (state law defines property); Andrus v. Allard (personal property); Scranton v. Wheeler (pre-existing servitude); Webb's Fabulous Pharmacies; Curtin v. Benson; Bowditch v. Boston (necessity); Esposito (ripeness under the same Act).
  • Precedents distinguished or recharacterized:
  • Mugler, Hadacheck, Miller v. Schoene, Goldblatt, Plymouth Coal, and Reinman are read as early police-power formulations. None involved an alleged total loss of value, and in several, other uses remained.
  • Williamson County and MacDonald (ripeness) are distinguished on this case's posture.
  • Blackmun's reliance on Lujan is distinguished as a summary-judgment standard, while this case was treated as on the pleadings.
  • Overruled: None. The Court said it "do[es] not invent but merely appl[ies]" the total-deprivation rule.
