Black-Letter Rule

A manufacturer of a finished product owes a duty of care, irrespective of contract, to persons other than the immediate purchaser when (1) the nature of the thing makes it reasonably certain to place life and limb in peril if negligently made, and (2) the manufacturer knows it will be used by persons other than the purchaser without new tests. The source of that duty is the law. Contract is not its source, and privity is not required.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff MacPherson was injured. Defendant Buick Motor Co. manufactured automobiles.
  • Chain of sale: Buick sold the car to a retail dealer. The dealer resold it to the plaintiff.
  • Accident: While the plaintiff was in the car, it suddenly collapsed and threw him out. One wheel was made of defective wood, and its spokes crumbled into fragments.
  • Component source: Buick did not make the wheel. It bought the wheel from another manufacturer. The dissent identifies that manufacturer as the reputable Imperial Wheel Co., which had supplied 80,000 wheels with no prior defects.
  • Inspection: There was evidence that reasonable inspection would have revealed the defect, and that Buick omitted inspection. Buick relied on the wheel maker to test the material.
  • No fraud: The plaintiff did not claim that Buick knew of the defect or concealed it. The claim sounded in negligence.
  • The car: It was designed to go fifty miles an hour and had seats for three. According to the dissent, it was being driven prudently at eight miles an hour when the wheel failed.

Procedural Posture

The case was tried to a jury on a negligence theory, and judgment was entered for the plaintiff. The Appellate Division approved the trial court's rulings, and the defendant appealed to the Court of Appeals.

Issue

Does a manufacturer of an automobile owe a duty of care in its construction and inspection to a remote purchaser who bought the car from a dealer and has no contract with the manufacturer?

Holding

Yes. A manufacturer of a thing reasonably certain to endanger life and limb if negligently made, which it knows will be used by persons other than the buyer without new tests, owes those users a duty to make it carefully, including a duty to inspect component parts bought from others. Judgment affirmed. Cardozo, J., wrote for the court, joined by Hiscock, Chase, and Cuddeback, JJ.; Hogan, J., concurred in result; Willard Bartlett, Ch. J., dissented; Pound, J., did not vote.

Reasoning

  • Framing the question: The case involves negligence, not fraud. The only question is whether Buick owed a duty of care and vigilance to anyone besides its immediate purchaser.
  • *Starting point, Thomas v. Winchester: A falsely labeled poison put human life in imminent danger. Because the danger was foreseeable, there was a duty to avoid the injury. The illustrations Thomas* gave of non-liability (such as the wagon builder) "might be rejected to-day." What matters is the principle of the distinction, which this state has never doubted.
  • Early narrow cases: Loop v. Litchfield and Losee v. Clute suggested a narrow reading of the rule. Losee is confined to its special facts and rested on remoteness of risk, since the buyer had accepted and tested the boiler.
  • Later liberal cases: In Devlin v. Smith (scaffold), Statler v. Ray Mfg. Co. (coffee urn), and Torgeson v. Schultz (aerated water bottles), the court imposed liability for things that are not destructive by nature and become dangerous only when imperfectly made. If those cases extended Thomas, "this court is committed to the extension."
  • Rejecting the defendant's narrow category: Buick argued that "imminently dangerous" things are limited to poisons, explosives, and deadly weapons. The court held that Thomas no longer carries that restricted meaning.
  • *Adopting Heaven v. Pender:* Lord Esher's formulation of a duty imposed by law on suppliers of goods is accepted. Its "tests and standards, at least in their underlying principles," are the tests and standards of New York law, subject to whatever qualification later application requires.
  • The test, element by element:
  • Thing of danger: Its nature must be such that it is reasonably certain to place life and limb in peril when negligently made. The danger must be probable. Mere possibility is not enough, because almost anything can be dangerous if defective. Whether a thing qualifies is sometimes a question for the court and sometimes one for the jury.
  • Knowledge of use by others: The manufacturer must know that in the usual course of events the danger will be shared by others than the buyer. Such knowledge may often be inferred from the nature of the transaction.
  • Use without new tests: The thing is put on the market to be used without inspection by customers.
  • Proximity factor: Knowledge of danger and of use may not always be enough. "The proximity or remoteness of the relation is a factor to be considered."
  • Application to the car: The nature of an automobile gives warning of probable danger if it is defectively built. The car was designed to go fifty miles an hour, and with unsound wheels injury was almost certain. Buick knew others would use the car: it had seats for three, and the buyer was a dealer who bought to resell. The dealer was the one person almost certain not to use the car. Protecting only the dealer would be an "inconsequent" result.
  • Changing conditions: Precedents from the stage-coach era do not fit modern travel. The principle that danger must be imminent stays fixed. What changes is the set of things subject to it.
  • Invitation analogy: Under both English and New York law, one who invites another to use an appliance must use reasonable care. The scaffold builder invites the owner's workmen to use the scaffold. The car maker invites the dealer's customers to use the car. One invitation runs to determinate persons and the other to an indeterminate class. The consequence is the same in both.
  • Landlord analogy: A lessor of a tumbledown house is not liable to the lessee's guests, because the lessee has the duty to repair. A lessor of a building meant for immediate use as a place of public entertainment does owe a duty to others, because injury to them is foreseeable.
  • Source of the duty: The duty to safeguard life and limb against foreseeable consequences does not grow "out of contract and nothing else." Its source is the law.
  • Jury charge: The trial judge's statement that an automobile is "not an inherently dangerous vehicle" meant that a well-built car poses no expected danger. The jury decided whether a negligently built car would be "imminently dangerous." The distinction between "inherent" and "imminent" danger is a verbal nicety. If danger was reasonably certain, a duty of vigilance existed. Any error in the charge was not one the defendant could complain of.
  • Duty to inspect components: Buying wheels from a reputable maker did not excuse Buick. As manufacturer of the finished product, Buick had to subject component parts to "ordinary and simple tests." The obligation to inspect varies with the nature of the thing. The more probable the danger, the greater the need for caution.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Hogan, J. (concurring in result): He wrote no separate opinion in the text.
  • Willard Bartlett, Ch. J. (dissenting, no joiners stated): A vendor's liability for negligent defects is confined to the immediate vendee, except for articles that are inherently dangerous in ordinary use. The trial judge told the jury an automobile is not inherently dangerous, so the exception did not apply. Winterbottom v. Wright, recognized in Thomas v. Winchester, establishes the general rule, and Thomas's wagon illustration assumes non-liability for an ordinary vehicle. Devlin treated the scaffold as inherently dangerous. A defective wheel at eight miles an hour is no more dangerous than a defective wheel on a horse-drawn carriage at the same speed, and the carriage maker would not be liable to strangers to the sale. Bartlett relied on Judge Sanborn's opinion in Huset, on Earl v. Lubbock, and on Cadillac Motor Car Co. v. Johnson (same facts, no liability). If old rules are archaic, the legislature should change them, not the courts. He would reverse.

Notes

  • Scope of the holding: The holding addresses the manufacturer of the finished product who puts it on the market for use without inspection. Cardozo said: "That is as far as we are required to go for the decision of this case."
  • Left open: component-part makers. The court expressly declined to decide whether liability reaches back to manufacturers of component parts. In such cases the finished-product maker's failure to inspect may be an intervening cause, making the earlier negligence too remote. "We leave that question open."
  • Left open: intermediate appellate cases. The court neither approved nor disapproved the applications in Burke v. Ireland (building), Kahner v. Otis Elevator Co. (elevator), and Davies v. Pelham Hod Elevating Co. (rope). It cited them only to show the trend of judicial thought.
  • Not resolved: whether the car was dangerous as a matter of law. The court did not say the trial judge would have been unjustified in ruling that the car was a dangerous thing as a matter of law.
  • No express overruling. The court called Devlin settled law and said some of Thomas's illustrations "might be rejected to-day."
  • Relied on: Thomas v. Winchester (foundational principle); Devlin v. Smith, Statler v. Ray Mfg. Co., Torgeson v. Schultz (extension beyond destructive things); Heaven v. Pender (Lord Esher's test); Elliott v. Hall, White v. Steadman, Dominion Natural Gas Co. v. Collins (English support); Caledonian Ry. Co. v. Mulholland and Indermaur v. Dames (invitation principle); Olds Motor Works v. Shaffer (Kentucky authority opposed to Cadillac); Junhermann v. Tilyou R. Co. (landlord analogy); Richmond & Danville R. R. Co. v. Elliott (duty to test components).
  • Distinguished or limited:
  • Kuelling v. Lean Mfg. Co.: a fraud and concealment case, not involved here.
  • Loop v. Litchfield: the defect was disclosed, the buyer assumed the risk, and the risk was not imminent.
  • Losee v. Clute: confined to its facts, because the buyer had tested the boiler and the risk was too remote.
  • Winterbottom v. Wright: the defendant was not the manufacturer, the case was decided on demurrer, and the declaration was criticized for failing to plead a duty outside the contract.
  • Earl v. Lubbock: the defendant was not the manufacturer and had only contracted to repair.
  • Carlson v. Phoenix Bridge Co.: an employer buying a tool outside its business could properly rely on the tool maker. Buick, as a manufacturer, had a stricter duty.
  • Contrary authority acknowledged: In Cadillac M. C. Co. v. Johnson the court held an automobile is outside Thomas, over a vigorous dissent. As for Huset, some of the cases Sanborn summarized can be reconciled on remoteness or intervening cause. Where they cannot, the difference lies in applying the principle, not in the principle itself. The court rejected Sanborn's view that a car maker cannot ordinarily foresee injury to non-owners.
