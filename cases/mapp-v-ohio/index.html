Black-Letter Rule

All evidence obtained by searches and seizures in violation of the Constitution is inadmissible in a state court. The exclusionary rule is an essential part of the Fourth Amendment right of privacy, so it binds the States through the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment by the same sanction of exclusion used against the Federal Government.

Facts

  • Tip: Cleveland police went to Mapp's home on information that a person wanted for questioning in a recent bombing was hiding there and that a large amount of policy paraphernalia was hidden there.
  • Refusal: After calling her attorney, Mapp refused to admit the officers without a search warrant. The officers put the house under surveillance.
  • Forced entry: About three hours later, with four or more additional officers present, police forcibly opened at least one door and entered. They kept Mapp's attorney from seeing her or entering.
  • The "warrant": An officer held up a paper claimed to be a warrant. Mapp grabbed it and put it in her bosom. Officers recovered it in a struggle and handcuffed her.
  • Search: Officers searched her bedroom (dresser, chest of drawers, closet, suitcases, photo album, personal papers), the rest of the second floor, the basement of the building, and a trunk found in the basement. The obscene materials were discovered in the course of that widespread search.
  • No warrant shown: No warrant was produced at trial and its absence was never explained. The Ohio Supreme Court said there was "considerable doubt" any warrant ever existed.
  • Charge: Mapp was convicted under Ohio Rev. Code § 2905.34 for knowingly possessing or controlling lewd and lascivious books, pictures, and photographs.

Procedural Posture

Mapp was convicted at trial, and the Ohio Supreme Court upheld the conviction even though it rested primarily on evidence unlawfully seized, because the evidence had not been taken from her person by brutal or offensive physical force. The U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction on appeal.

Issue

Does the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment bar a state court from admitting evidence obtained through a search and seizure that violates the Fourth Amendment?

Holding

Yes; because the Fourth Amendment's right of privacy is enforceable against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, all evidence obtained by searches and seizures in violation of the Constitution is, by that same authority, inadmissible in a state court, so the judgment of the Ohio Supreme Court is reversed and the cause remanded (opinion by Justice Clark).

Reasoning

  • Exclusion is constitutional in origin. The Court traced the rule from Boyd v. United States through Weeks v. United States, Silverthorne Lumber Co. v. United States, Byars v. United States, and Olmstead v. United States. Without exclusion, the Fourth Amendment would be reduced to "a form of words." Passing references to Weeks as a rule of evidence do not disturb its constitutional basis.
  • Wolf's own premise. Wolf v. Colorado held that the security of privacy against arbitrary police intrusion is "implicit in 'the concept of ordered liberty'" and enforceable against the States, and it "stoutly adhered" to Weeks. Yet it refused to require exclusion in state courts.
  • Logical necessity. Once the right is enforceable against the States, it cannot be severed from the sanction on which it has always depended. Granting the right while withholding exclusion "is to grant the right but in reality to withhold its privilege and enjoyment."
  • Deterrence. Quoting Elkins v. United States, the purpose of the rule "is to deter... by removing the incentive to disregard it."
  • Wolf's factual grounds no longer hold. The Court said these grounds were not basically relevant to the constitutional question but addressed them anyway:
  • State practice: Before Wolf, almost two-thirds of the States rejected exclusion. Since then, more than half of those passing on it have wholly or partly adopted it, including California in People v. Cahan.
  • Other remedies: California found other remedies "completely failed." The Court had recognized the futility of other remedies in Irvine v. California.
  • Cardozo's critique: People v. Defore called the federal rule "either too strict or too lax." Later cases largely vitiated that reasoning: Elkins (discarding the "silver platter" doctrine), Jones v. United States (relaxing standing), and Rea v. United States (preventing state use of evidence unconstitutionally seized by federal agents).
  • Coerced-confession analogy. States must exclude coerced confessions regardless of reliability (Rogers v. Richmond). The Court saw no reason to treat unconstitutionally seized goods and papers differently, since they are "tantamount to coerced testimony." It relied on the "intimate relation" between Fourth and Fifth Amendment principles (Bram v. United States; Feldman v. United States; cf. Rochin v. California).
  • Federalism and the double standard. A federal prosecutor could not use illegally seized evidence, but a state prosecutor "across the street" could. This invited "working arrangements" and needless state-federal conflict (Wilson v. Schnettler; Rea).
  • Judicial integrity. Responding to Cardozo's line that "[t]he criminal is to go free because the constable has blundered," the Court said: "The criminal goes free, if he must, but it is the law that sets him free." It quoted Justice Brandeis's Olmstead dissent that a lawbreaking government "breeds contempt for law."
  • Law enforcement not fettered. Citing Elkins, the Court noted that federal courts had operated under Weeks for almost half a century without rendering the FBI ineffective.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Black (concurring, joins the opinion): The Fourth Amendment standing alone does not require exclusion, because it contains no exclusion provision. Read together with the Fifth Amendment's privilege against self-incrimination, as in Boyd, it does require exclusion. Black had concurred in Wolf and now departs from that view. Black reads the majority as rejecting the "shock-the-conscience" standard of Rochin and Wolf in favor of the more predictable Boyd doctrine.
  • Justice Douglas (concurring, joins the opinion): Adds factual detail about the siege, entry, and search. Argues Wolf reduced the Fourth Amendment to a "dead letter" because the alternatives are illusory: police discipline or prosecution requires officials to prosecute themselves, and trespass suits are onerous with meager relief. Finds the case procedurally appropriate because the search issue was raised in the notice of appeal, the jurisdictional statement, and the brief. Notes that under the Ohio Supreme Court's reading of the statute, Mapp was guilty whichever account of where the materials were found was true.
  • Justice Stewart (memorandum): Agrees with Part I of the Harlan dissent and expresses no view on the exclusionary-rule question. Would reverse because § 2905.34 is inconsistent with the rights of free thought and expression protected against state action by the Fourteenth Amendment.
  • Justice Harlan (dissenting, joined by Justices Frankfurter and Whittaker):
  • Procedure: The Court "reached out" to overrule Wolf. The principal issue briefed and argued was whether the Ohio statute, which punishes knowing possession of obscene material irrespective of purpose or opportunity to get rid of it, violates the Fourteenth Amendment. Appellant's counsel expressly disavowed asking for Wolf to be overruled. At minimum the case should have been set for reargument.
  • Merits: Wolf carried the core principle of privacy into the Fourteenth Amendment. It did not carry over every federal configuration of the Fourth Amendment. Exclusion is a remedy aimed at deterring police misconduct, and States should be free to choose their own remedies. Half the States still followed the non-exclusionary rule. The Court has no supervisory power over state courts, and admitting relevant evidence does not make a trial unfair.
  • Confession analogy fails: Coerced confessions are excluded to protect the fairness of the trial itself (Lisenba v. California; Watts v. Indiana). Exclusion of seized evidence disciplines the police.
  • Split rationale: Only four Justices support the majority's reasoning, since Black rests on the Fifth Amendment, and the Fifth Amendment privilege had recently been held inapplicable to the States (Cohen v. Hurley).

Notes

  • Overruled: Wolf v. Colorado's holding that the Fourteenth Amendment does not forbid admission of unreasonably seized evidence in a state prosecution for a state crime. Wolf's holding that the privacy right is enforceable against the States is retained and serves as the foundation for the decision.
  • Relied on: Boyd, Weeks, Silverthorne, Byars, Olmstead (majority and Brandeis dissent), Elkins, Jones, Rea, Rogers v. Richmond, Bram, Feldman, Miller v. United States; cf. Rochin.
  • Distinguished: McNabb v. United States, which formulated a non-constitutional rule of evidence for federal trials and did not reach the constitutional issue.
  • Rejected reasoning: People v. Defore (Cardozo), relied on in Wolf, whose force the Court found "largely vitiated" by later decisions.
  • Left undecided: The other issues raised on appeal, including the constitutionality of § 2905.34 under the Fourteenth Amendment. Appellant did not urge overruling Wolf; the amicus did.
  • Limits stated in the opinion: State procedural requirements governing direct and collateral constitutional challenges must still be respected. The Court described the class of state convictions possibly affected as of "relatively narrow compass."
  • Reasonableness: The Court acknowledged "recurring questions of the reasonableness of searches" and said reasonableness is in the first instance for the trial court to determine (United States v. Rabinowitz).
  • Criminal Law angle: The underlying offense is a strict possession crime. As construed by the Ohio Supreme Court, § 2905.34 reached knowing possession regardless of purpose, and the dissent noted it applied regardless of any reasonable opportunity to rid oneself of the material.
