Black-Letter Rule

An act of Congress repugnant to the Constitution is void, and because it is "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is," a court faced with a conflict between a statute and the Constitution must apply the Constitution. Article III's grant of original jurisdiction to the Supreme Court is exclusive, so Congress cannot add to it, and a writ of mandamus to an executive officer to deliver a paper is an exercise of original jurisdiction. Courts may compel a department head to perform a specific ministerial duty on which individual rights depend, but they cannot review acts committed to executive political discretion.

Facts

  • Statute: A February 1801 act of Congress concerning the District of Columbia authorized the President to appoint justices of the peace for each county, "to continue in office for five years."
  • Appointment: President John Adams signed a commission for William Marbury as justice of the peace for the county of Washington. The seal of the United States was affixed.
  • Non-delivery: The commission never reached Marbury.
  • Relief sought: Marbury sought a writ of mandamus directing the secretary of state to deliver his commission.
  • Jurisdictional statute: The act establishing the judicial courts of the United States authorized the Supreme Court "to issue writs of mandamus, in cases warranted by the principles and usages of law, to any courts appointed, or persons holding office, under the authority of the United States."

Procedural Posture

Marbury applied directly to the Supreme Court. At the prior term, on affidavits, the Court granted a rule requiring the secretary of state to show cause why a mandamus should not issue; no cause was shown, and Marbury moved for the writ.

Issue

Can the Supreme Court issue a writ of mandamus to the secretary of state in an original proceeding, where an act of Congress purports to authorize the writ but the case falls outside the original jurisdiction Article III assigns to the Court?

Holding

No: Marbury has a vested legal right to his commission and mandamus is the proper remedy, but the statute authorizing the Court to issue the writ in an original proceeding exceeds Article III's exclusive grant of original jurisdiction, is void, and cannot be applied, so the rule is discharged.

Reasoning

Question 1: Does Marbury have a right to the commission?

  • Three distinct acts: Article II and the statutes contemplate nomination, appointment, and commission. Nomination and appointment are voluntary presidential acts. Commissioning is a separate act, since the Constitution provides for it in a separate section.
  • When the appointment is complete: The appointment is complete when the President performs the last act required of him, which is signing the commission. The commission is complete when the secretary of state affixes the seal. Sealing and recording are ministerial duties prescribed by law, beyond the President's control.
  • Delivery not required: The deed analogy fails. Even if delivery were required, delivery to the secretary for sealing would suffice. Transmission is a matter of convenience. Acceptance is the officer's act and comes after the appointment. A recorded copy is legally equal to the original.
  • Vested right: Where an officer is removable at will, timing does not matter because the appointment is revocable. Marbury held a five-year term independent of the executive, so his appointment was irrevocable and vested legal rights. Withholding the commission violated those rights.

Question 2: Do the laws afford a remedy?

  • Right-remedy principle: The Court cites Blackstone: "where there is a legal right, there is also a legal remedy." A "government of laws, and not of men" must furnish a remedy for the violation of a vested legal right.
  • No exemption for this case: The case is not damnum absque injuria. Offices of trust, honor, or profit are worth legal protection.
  • Political versus ministerial acts: Whether an act of a department head is reviewable "must always depend on the nature of that act."
  • Political or discretionary acts: Where the officer acts as the President's agent in matters of constitutional or legal discretion, his acts "are only politically examinable." These subjects "respect the nation, not individual rights."
  • Ministerial acts: Where "a specific duty is assigned by law, and individual rights depend upon the performance of that duty," the injured individual may seek a legal remedy.
  • Application: Nomination and appointment are political powers. Once the appointment is made, the President's discretion is spent. Whether a right has vested "is, in its nature, judicial."

Question 3: Is mandamus from this Court the proper remedy?

  • Nature of the writ: Relying on Blackstone and Lord Mansfield in King v. Baker, mandamus lies where there is a right to execute an office of public concern, the holder is kept out of possession, and there is no other specific legal remedy.
  • Elements:
  • The applicant has a right to an office of public concern and is kept from it.
  • The writ is directed to an officer to whom it may properly issue.
  • The applicant has no other specific legal remedy.
  • Officer's rank is no bar: The propriety of the writ turns on "the nature of the thing to be done." The office of the person to whom it is directed does not control. The Court disclaims any power to "intrude into the cabinet" or review discretionary executive acts.
  • Statutory silence on delivery: The acts of Congress do not expressly direct delivery. That does not matter: the commission was placed in the secretary's hands for Marbury's use and cannot lawfully be withheld.
  • Detinue inadequate: Detinue yields the thing or its value, and a public office has no ascertainable value. Mandamus is therefore proper to deliver the commission or a copy from the record.

The jurisdictional problem

  • The statute covers the case: The secretary of state is "precisely within the letter" of the statute. So the Court can deny the writ only if the statute is unconstitutional.
  • Original jurisdiction is exclusive: Article III gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, and cases in which a state is a party. "In all other cases" its jurisdiction is appellate.
  • If Congress could reassign jurisdiction, the distribution clause would be "mere surplussage" and "form without substance."
  • "Affirmative words are often, in their operation, negative of other objects than those affirmed."
  • No clause of the Constitution may be presumed to be without effect.
  • Appellate jurisdiction defined: Its "essential criterion" is that it "revises and corrects the proceedings in a cause already instituted, and does not create that cause." A mandamus to an officer to deliver a paper amounts to an original action for that paper. It is also not necessary to the exercise of appellate jurisdiction.

Judicial review

  • Written, limited constitution: The people established fundamental, permanent principles and limited the legislature's powers in writing. If those limits bind no one, the distinction between limited and unlimited government disappears. There is "no middle ground": either the Constitution is paramount law or it is alterable by ordinary act. Therefore a repugnant legislative act is void.
  • Judicial duty: Courts must expound the law when applying it. When a statute and the Constitution conflict in a case, the court must decide which governs. Because the Constitution is superior, it governs. Requiring courts to "close their eyes on the constitution" would give the legislature "a practical and real omnipotence."
  • Textual support:
  • The judicial power extends to cases "arising under the constitution."
  • The Court gives hypotheticals on the export-tax ban, the bill of attainder and ex post facto bans, and the two-witness treason rule. The treason rule is addressed directly to courts.
  • Judges take an oath to support the Constitution, and the statutory oath requires them to discharge duties "agreeably to the constitution."
  • The Supremacy Clause names the Constitution first and gives supreme rank only to laws made "in pursuance" of it.

Notes

  • Sequence matters: The Court decides the merits and remedy questions before jurisdiction. Its conclusions on Marbury's right and remedy are stated as the opinion of the court, but the disposition rests on the jurisdictional holding.
  • Narrow ground for invalidation: The statute falls only insofar as it confers original jurisdiction to issue mandamus to public officers. The Court acknowledges that mandamus "may be directed to courts" and that appellate jurisdiction "may be exercised in a variety of forms" Congress chooses.
  • Appellate exceptions: The Court notes appellate jurisdiction exists "with such exceptions as congress might make."
  • Political questions reserved: "Questions, in their nature political, or which are, by the constitution and laws, submitted to the executive, can never be made in this court." The Court concedes "there may be difficulty in applying the rule to particular cases."
  • Removable officers: Where an officer is removable at the President's will, the moment of completed appointment "is of no concern," and the commission "may be arrested, if still in the office."
  • Judicial review framed as a duty of the courts: The opinion's conclusion is that "courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument."
  • Authorities relied on:
  • Blackstone's Commentaries on the right-remedy principle, Crown officers' liability, and the definition of mandamus.
  • Lord Mansfield in King v. Baker (3 Burrows 1266) on when mandamus lies.
  • The 1792 pension act episode. The act was deemed unconstitutional at the circuits insofar as it imposed duties on courts. A 1793 act then sought a Supreme Court adjudication. In the resulting mandamus motion against the secretary at war, the Court denied the writ because the applicant lacked a legal right. It did not hold that mandamus could never lie to a department head. The Court cites this episode to show its doctrine "is by no means a novel one."
  • Overruled: The opinion overrules no decision.
