Black-Letter Rule

An act of Congress repugnant to the Constitution is void, and because it is "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is," a court must apply the Constitution over a conflicting statute in a case before it. Article III's grant of original jurisdiction to the Supreme Court is exclusive, so Congress cannot add to it, and a statute authorizing the Court to issue mandamus to an executive officer as an original matter is unconstitutional. Where the law assigns an executive officer a specific ministerial duty and individual rights depend on it, the injured party has a legal remedy; acts within the executive's constitutional or legal discretion are only politically examinable.

Facts

  • Statute creating the office: A February 1801 act of Congress concerning the District of Columbia provided for justices of the peace, appointed as the President thought expedient, to serve five-year terms.
  • Appointment: President John Adams signed a commission naming William Marbury a justice of the peace for Washington County, and the seal of the United States was affixed.
  • Non-delivery: The commission never reached Marbury. The secretary of state withheld it.
  • Relief sought: Marbury asked the Supreme Court for a writ of mandamus directing the secretary of state to deliver the commission.
  • Jurisdictional statute: The act to establish the judicial courts of the United States authorized the Supreme Court to issue mandamus "to any courts appointed, or persons holding office, under the authority of the United States."

Procedural Posture

Marbury applied directly to the Supreme Court. At the prior term, on affidavits, the Court granted a rule requiring the secretary of state to show cause why mandamus should not issue; no cause was shown, and Marbury moved for the writ.

Issue

Does the Supreme Court have power to issue a writ of mandamus, in an original proceeding, compelling the secretary of state to deliver Marbury's commission, where a federal statute purports to authorize it?

Holding

No. Marbury has a vested legal right to the commission and mandamus is the proper remedy, but the statute authorizing the Supreme Court to issue mandamus to public officers in this posture unconstitutionally extends the Court's original jurisdiction beyond Article III, a statute repugnant to the Constitution is void and cannot be applied by the courts, and the rule is discharged.

Reasoning

Question 1: Does Marbury have a right to the commission? Yes.

  • The Constitution and statutes contemplate three distinct operations: nomination (sole, voluntary act of the President), appointment (voluntary presidential act requiring Senate advice and consent), and commission.
  • Appointment and commission are separate because the Constitution grants the powers in separate sections. Congress may vest appointment of inferior officers elsewhere, in which case the President's duty to commission would be distinct from the appointment.
  • The appointment is complete when the President performs the last act required of him: signing the commission. The commission is complete when the secretary of state affixes the seal.
  • After signature, the secretary's duties to seal and record are ministerial. They are prescribed by law and not subject to presidential direction.
  • Delivery, transmission, and acceptance are not required. Even treating the commission as a deed, delivery occurred when it went to the secretary for sealing. Transmission is a practice of convenience, not a legal requirement. Acceptance is the officer's act and comes after the appointment.
  • Where the officer is not removable at the executive's will, the appointment is irrevocable and confers vested legal rights. Marbury's five-year term was independent of the executive, so withholding the commission violated a vested right.

Question 2: Do the laws afford a remedy? Yes.

  • Relying on Blackstone: where there is a legal right, there is a legal remedy whenever the right is invaded. The United States is "a government of laws, and not of men."
  • A public office is not damnum absque injuria. An office of trust, honor, or profit created by Congress is worthy of legal protection.
  • Whether an executive act is judicially examinable depends on the nature of the act, not the office of the actor.
  • Political powers committed to presidential discretion, and acts of officers who merely carry out the President's will, are only politically examinable. "The subjects are political. They respect the nation, not individual rights."
  • Where the law assigns a specific duty and individual rights depend on its performance, the officer is "so far the officer of the law" and the injured individual may resort to the courts.
  • Whether a right has vested is "in its nature, judicial."

Question 3: Is Marbury entitled to mandamus from this Court? Mandamus is proper, but not from this Court.

  • Nature of the writ. Relying on Blackstone and Lord Mansfield in King v. Baker, mandamus lies where a person has a right to an office of public concern, is kept out of possession, and has no other specific legal remedy.
  • Elements applied: (1) a right to execute an office; (2) the applicant kept out of possession; (3) the officer is one to whom the writ may properly be directed, judged by the nature of the act, not the officer's station; (4) no other specific legal remedy.
  • Detinue is inadequate because it yields the thing or its value, and a public office not for sale has no ascertainable value.
  • That no statute expressly orders delivery does not matter. The commission was placed in the secretary's hands for the person entitled to it.
  • Power of the Court. The statute reaches the secretary of state by its terms, so the question is whether Congress could confer the power.
  • Article III gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction in cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, and those in which a state is a party; "in all other cases" jurisdiction is appellate.
  • The argument that Congress may add original jurisdiction because the clause lacks negative words fails. That reading would make the distribution of jurisdiction "mere surplussage" and "form without substance." Affirmative words may operate negatively, and no constitutional clause is presumed to be without effect.
  • The "essential criterion of appellate jurisdiction" is that it "revises and corrects the proceedings in a cause already instituted, and does not create that cause." Mandamus to an officer to deliver a paper is in effect an original action for that paper, and it is not necessary to the exercise of appellate jurisdiction.
  • Judicial review.
  • The people established the Constitution as fundamental, permanent law, and wrote it down to define and limit legislative power. Limits that can be passed at will are no limits.
  • The Constitution is either paramount law unchangeable by ordinary means or on a level with ordinary acts. There is no middle ground. Written constitutions assume the former, so a repugnant legislative act is void.
  • Courts must say what the law is. When a statute and the Constitution both apply to a case and conflict, the court must decide which governs, and the superior law governs.
  • The contrary view would give the legislature "a practical and real omnipotence" and reduce the written constitution to nothing.
  • Textual support: (1) the judicial power extends to cases arising under the Constitution, which presupposes judges look into it; (2) prohibitions such as the export-tax clause, the bar on bills of attainder and ex post facto laws, and the two-witness treason rule (addressed specifically to courts) would be meaningless if courts had to enforce contrary statutes; (3) judges swear an oath to support the Constitution, and the statutory judicial oath requires duties be discharged "agreeably to the constitution"; (4) the Supremacy Clause lists the Constitution first and gives supreme-law status only to laws made "in pursuance" of it.

Notes

  • Narrow disposition. The Court found the right, the violation, and the propriety of mandamus as a remedy, then discharged the rule solely for want of jurisdiction in this Court.
  • Limits on review of executive action. Questions "in their nature political," or submitted by the Constitution and laws to the executive, "can never be made in this court." The Court disclaimed any power to inquire how the executive performs discretionary duties.
  • Removability matters. The irrevocability analysis turns on the officer not being removable at the executive's will. For an officer removable at will, the Court said the circumstance completing the appointment "is of no concern."
  • Acknowledged difficulty. The Court said there may be difficulty applying the political/ministerial rule to particular cases, though not in stating it.
  • Timing of appointment. The Court held the appointment is made on signature and the commission complete on sealing, and described sealing as "the latest point of time" at which the appointment could be considered complete.
  • Appellate mandamus. The Court recognized that appellate jurisdiction may take various forms and that mandamus "may be directed to courts." It held only that this writ, to an officer for a paper, was original.
  • Authorities relied on:
  • Blackstone, Commentaries vol. 3: right-remedy principle; crown officers answerable for injuries to property rights; definition of mandamus.
  • King v. Baker (Lord Mansfield, 3 Burrows 1266): when mandamus lies.
  • Statutory analogies: the 1794 invalids act (pension list) and the 1796 Kentucky lands act (land patents), offered to show that withholding a legally required act injuring an individual must have a remedy.
  • The 1792 pension act and the 1793 act directing adjudication in the Supreme Court: cited to show that a mandamus to the secretary at war was previously sought, and that the Court's refusal there rested on the absence of a legal right. It did not rest on any rule that mandamus never lies to a department head.
  • Overruled: None.
