Black-Letter Rule

Due process does not require a State to let a biological father rebut the marital presumption and obtain parental rights to a child conceived and born into an existing marriage, where the mother and her husband were cohabiting and both wish to raise the child as their own. Under the plurality's approach, a claimed liberty interest must be fundamental and rooted in history and tradition, and no tradition protects the adulterous natural father's claim against the marital family. The plurality reviewed a child's equal protection challenge to her exclusion from rebutting the presumption under rational basis.

Facts

  • Statute: Cal. Evid. Code § 621 conclusively presumes that the child of a wife cohabiting with her husband, who is not impotent or sterile, is a child of the marriage. Only the husband may rebut it by blood tests within two years of birth. The wife may do so too, within the same two years, if the biological father has filed an affidavit acknowledging paternity.
  • Parties: Carole and Gerald married in 1976 and lived in California. Carole had an affair with Michael and conceived Victoria, born in 1981. Gerald was listed on the birth certificate and always held Victoria out as his daughter.
  • Blood tests: Tests showed a 98.07% probability that Michael was the father.
  • Shifting households: Victoria lived with Carole throughout. Carole moved among Gerald, Michael (including time in St. Thomas and about eight months in Los Angeles, where Michael held Victoria out as his child), and another man, Scott K.
  • Stipulation: In April 1984, Carole and Michael signed a stipulation that Michael was the natural father. Carole then left Michael and told her lawyers not to file it.
  • Reconciliation: In June 1984, Carole reconciled with Gerald. They live in New York with Victoria and two other children born into the marriage.
  • Litigation: Michael filed a filiation action seeking paternity and visitation. Victoria, through a guardian ad litem, claimed a right to maintain filial relationships with both men. Gerald intervened and moved for summary judgment under § 621.

Procedural Posture

The Superior Court granted Gerald summary judgment under § 621, rejected the constitutional challenges, and denied visitation under Cal. Civ. Code § 4601. The California Court of Appeal affirmed and read the judgment as also denying permanent visitation. The California Supreme Court denied review, and the U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Does California's conclusive marital presumption violate the due process rights of a biological father, or the due process or equal protection rights of the child, by barring him from establishing paternity of a child born to a married woman cohabiting with her husband?

Holding

No. Affirmed. Justice Scalia announced the judgment in a plurality opinion. A four-Justice plurality (Scalia, Rehnquist, O'Connor, and Kennedy) concluded that the presumption denies no constitutionally protected liberty interest of the father, that the child's due process claim fails even assuming she has a liberty interest, and that her equal protection claim fails rational basis review. Justice Stevens concurred only in the judgment: he assumed Michael had a protected interest but found that Michael received a fair opportunity to seek visitation under § 4601. The judgment drew five votes as reflected in the joins (Scalia, Rehnquist, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Stevens concurring in the judgment), over dissents by Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, and White.

Reasoning

  • What Michael sought: California law recognizes no dual fatherhood. Being declared the father would carry visitation and the right to be considered for custody. Denying him paternity denied him all parental rights, and the state courts held that he also could not get discretionary nonparent visitation against the mother's wishes (Vincent B. v. Joan R.).
  • Procedural due process rejected: Section 621 is phrased as a presumption, but it implements a substantive rule. California treats biological paternity as irrelevant in this setting. The "irrebuttable presumption" cases (Stanley v. Illinois, Vlandis v. Kline, Cleveland Board of Education v. LaFleur) did not rest on procedural due process. They test the fit between a classification and its policy, as with any general classification (Craig v. Boren, Carrington v. Rash, Weinberger v. Salfi).
  • Substantive due process test: "Liberty" extends beyond physical restraint (Meyer, Pierce). To limit judicial discretion, the interest must be fundamental and also traditionally protected, "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental" (Snyder v. Massachusetts; Harlan concurrence in Griswold; Moore v. East Cleveland).
  • Unwed-father cases recharacterized: Michael read Stanley, Quilloin, Caban, and Lehr as making biology plus an established relationship sufficient. The plurality read them as resting on the historic respect given to relationships within the "unitary family."
  • Tradition favors the marital family: The common-law presumption of legitimacy could be rebutted only by proof of incapacity or nonaccess. Its rationales were avoiding illegitimacy and protecting the peace of families. A 1957 annotation showed no decision granting standing to the natural father.
  • Burden and the right framed: Michael bears the burden, and the lack of supporting evidence alone might defeat him. The relevant question is whether States have traditionally awarded substantive parental rights to the natural father of a child born into an extant marriage that wishes to embrace the child. The plurality found no case, old or new, doing so. Modern statutes giving natural fathers theoretical standing to rebut the presumption are irrelevant.
  • No right to a bare declaration: A State need not declare facts unless some legal consequence hinges on the declaration.
  • Lehr's "opportunity" interest: The biological father's unique opportunity conflicts with the husband's equally unique opportunity, and the State may categorically prefer the husband. Lehr (quoting Justice Stewart's Caban dissent) recognized that the father's lack of a legal tie to the mother may limit his claim. A mother married to and cohabiting with another man, both wishing to raise the child, imposes a similar limit.
  • Legislative choice: Whether to allow rebuttal is legislative policy. Protecting Michael means denying protection to Gerald, and the Constitution leaves that choice to California.
  • Victoria's due process claim: The plurality assumed without deciding that a child has a liberty interest in her filial relationship. A right to two fathers has no support in history or tradition. A narrower claim to a relationship with Michael alone is the obverse of his claim and fails for the same reasons.
  • Victoria's equal protection claim: Strict scrutiny does not apply because illegitimacy is a legal construct and Victoria is legitimate under California law (distinguishing Gomez v. Perez). Under rational basis, the limit on who may rebut is rational. A spouse's challenge means the marriage is already shaken, and a guardian ad litem's challenge could disrupt a peaceful union.
  • Footnote 3 (unitary family): The traditionally respected family includes the marital family and the household of unmarried parents and their children. It does not stretch to a married woman, her lover, and their child during brief, intermittent periods together.
  • Footnote 6 (level of generality), joined only by Scalia and Rehnquist: Courts should look to the most specific level at which a relevant tradition protecting or denying the asserted right can be identified. Only if no specific tradition exists should a court reason from more general traditions, such as those on natural fathers. The footnote invokes Bowers v. Hardwick and Roe v. Wade as examples and says Griswold and Eisenstadt did not reject an extant tradition withholding the right.
  • Footnote 7 (limit): The plurality rested on the absence of a protected right shown by tradition, with no independent balancing of interests. It confined its holding to these facts because traditions might lead elsewhere when the marital parents do not wish to raise the child.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • O'Connor (joined by Kennedy), concurring in part: Joins all but footnote 6. The "most specific level" approach may be inconsistent with past cases that described traditions more generally (Griswold, Eisenstadt, Loving v. Virginia, Turner v. Safley). She would not lock in a single mode of historical analysis (citing Harlan's Poe v. Ullman dissent).
  • Stevens, concurring in the judgment: Agrees a State need not declare biological paternity with no legal consequence attached. Rejects the plurality's suggestion that a natural father can never have a protected interest in this situation, citing Stanley and Caban. Assumes Michael's relationship gave him a right to try to show that visitation would serve Victoria's best interests. Reads § 4601's second sentence ("any other person having an interest in the welfare of the child") as giving the trial judge that authority. Since the trial court separately found visitation not in Victoria's best interests, Michael received a fair opportunity and due process was satisfied.
  • Brennan (joined by Marshall and Blackmun), dissenting: Notes that five Justices refuse to foreclose a protected interest for fathers like Michael, and five see the flaw in a conclusive presumption that ends a protected interest as procedural. Attacks the plurality's reliance on tradition as malleable, novel, and inconsistent with Eisenstadt, Griswold, Ingraham, Vitek, and Stanley. Argues that "liberty" must include "the freedom not to conform." Reads Stanley, Quilloin, Caban, and Lehr as protecting biology plus a substantial parent-child relationship, which Michael has. Criticizes the "unitary family" framing for turning on marriage and for letting the State's interest define liberty. Treats § 621 as a conclusive presumption that terminates a protected interest without any hearing. Rejects Stevens's reading of § 4601 as contrary to Vincent B. Under procedural due process, California's interests (mainly family privacy) are too slight to deny a hearing on paternity.
  • White (joined by Brennan), dissenting: Biology is highly relevant to Michael's rights. Michael satisfies even Lehr's demanding standard: he lived with Victoria, held her out as his daughter, supported her, and she called him "Daddy." The stigma-of-illegitimacy rationale is outdated given blood tests (Little v. Streater) and has little force when a father seeks to establish paternity. The family-integrity interest is weaker here because Gerald knew of the affair. Barring Michael's blood-test evidence denied him a meaningful opportunity to be heard (Armstrong v. Manzo).

Notes

  • Plurality caution: Only four Justices joined the reasoning, and only two joined footnote 6's level-of-generality rule. Five Justices (Stevens plus the four dissenters) declined to foreclose a natural father's protected interest in this setting.
  • Narrow facts: The holding is limited to a mother married to and cohabiting with her husband at conception and birth, where both wish to raise the child as theirs.
  • Expressly left open: Whether a child has a liberty interest in her filial relationship. Whether tradition supports a different result when the marital parents do not wish to raise the child (footnote 7). Michael's equal protection claim, which was not reached because it was neither raised nor passed on below.
  • Relied on: Meyer v. Nebraska, Pierce v. Society of Sisters, Snyder v. Massachusetts, Moore v. East Cleveland (plurality and White's dissent), Harlan's Griswold concurrence, Weinberger v. Salfi, Bowers v. Hardwick (footnote 6), and Vincent B. v. Joan R. (state-law reading of §§ 621 and 4601).
  • Distinguished or recharacterized: Stanley, Quilloin, Caban, and Lehr, read as unitary-family cases. The irrebuttable-presumption cases (Stanley, Vlandis, LaFleur), read as fit cases outside procedural due process. Gomez v. Perez, inapplicable because Victoria is legitimate. Griswold and Eisenstadt, which did not reject an extant contrary tradition.
  • Overruled: None.
