Black-Letter Rule

A promise made without consideration cannot be enforced by action, even when breaking it is morally disgraceful. Moral obligation supports an express promise only where some preexisting obligation, founded on an antecedent good or valuable consideration, has become unenforceable by positive law. Past benefits conferred without the promisor's request, on a person the promisor had no legal duty to support, are not consideration for a later promise to pay for them.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff cared for the defendant's son. Defendant is the son's father.
  • The son: Twenty-five years old. He had long since left his father's family and was in no respect under the father's care.
  • The care: Returning from a foreign country, the son fell sick among strangers. Plaintiff gave him shelter and comfort until he died.
  • No request: Plaintiff's services were not given at the father's request.
  • The promise: After learning of the plaintiff's care, the father, "influenced by a transient feeling of gratitude," promised in writing to pay the plaintiff's expenses.
  • Breach: The father later refused to pay.

Procedural Posture

Plaintiff sued on the written promise. The Court of Common Pleas directed a nonsuit, and the Supreme Judicial Court considered whether that nonsuit was correct.

Issue

Is a father's written promise to pay for care already given, without his request, to his adult son who was not under his care enforceable on the theory that moral obligation is sufficient consideration?

Holding

No. Moral obligation is sufficient consideration for an express promise only where a good or valuable consideration existed at some point, and here none did. The court, in an opinion by Parker C.J., held that the nonsuit was right and ordered judgment entered on it for the defendant with costs, with all the judges agreeing ("we are all of opinion").

Reasoning

  • No-consideration rule is universal. A bare promise without consideration cannot be enforced. The rule cannot bend to fit particular cases where refusal looks disgraceful. Occasional injustice is a defect built into general rules made to protect people who promise carelessly.
  • Limiting the moral-obligation doctrine. Some authorities say broadly that moral obligation is sufficient consideration. The court found that "the universality of the rule cannot be supported." There must be a preexisting obligation that positive law has made inoperative. The elements of the exception are:
  • An original quid pro quo, meaning an antecedent valuable consideration;
  • A resulting obligation that natural justice says should be paid;
  • A rule of positive law that bars enforcement, mainly for public convenience; and
  • A later express promise that revives the obligation by removing that legal impediment.
  • Illustrations of the exception. The standard examples are debts barred by the statute of limitations, debts contracted during infancy and later promised by the adult, and debts discharged in bankruptcy. These are "not promises to pay something for nothing." The court finds the needed equivalent "by looking back to the origin of the transaction."
  • Why broad moral obligation proves too much. If moral obligation alone supported an express promise, the court could see no reason it would not also support an implied promise. Yet the law leaves many moral duties to "the tribunal of conscience." A wealthy son need not relieve his father's poverty, and a wealthy father need not rescue his adult son from debt, short of the point where public charity becomes necessary. These are "duties of imperfect obligation." They bind the conscience, but social law imposes no sanction on them.
  • Bargain principle. A deliberate written promise freely made may be morally binding. But the law enforces a promise only when the promisor gains something or the promisee loses something. The equivalent need not be exact, since parties set their own value, although equity may relieve against gross inadequacy.
  • Legal obligation as consideration. A legal obligation always supports an express or implied promise. Examples are an infant's debt for necessaries and a father's promise to pay for his minor children's support and education. Once a child reaches adulthood and acts as his own agent, his debts create no obligation on the father. A promise founded on such a debt therefore lacks binding force.
  • Apparent exceptions are consistent. Sealed instruments import consideration on their own. Negotiable mercantile instruments fall under a branch of commercial law that disregards consideration for the needs of commerce.
  • Poor-relief statute rejected. Plaintiff argued that a statute requiring lineal kindred to support poor relations likely to become chargeable to their town created a legal obligation. The court held that any such liability arises only in the cases the statute covers, and only after a court with jurisdiction adjudges the essential facts after hearing the party charged. Those facts are settlement in the commonwealth, likelihood of becoming chargeable, and the relative's sufficient ability. None appeared in the report.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Narrow framing of the precedent gap. The court found no case that "nakedly decided" that a promise could be enforced to pay the debt of a son of full age, not living with his father, incurred by sickness ending in death, where no prior request by the father was proved or presumed. The court used these facts to describe the absence of contrary precedent; it did not say that each of them is essential to, or defines the scope of, its holding.
  • Request matters. The court stressed that the services were not given at the father's request. The opinion's framing leaves room for a different analysis where a prior request is proved or presumed. The court did not decide that situation.
  • Statutory liability left open. The court did not hold that the support statute could never apply. It held only that liability had not arisen because no court had adjudicated the required facts.
  • Authority relied on. Instead of citing many cases, the court rests on "a very able review of all the cases in the note in 3 Bos. & Pul. 249." It also acknowledges that judicial opinions had varied on the issue "for a long course of years."
  • Authority distinguished. These are treated as consistent with the rule: statute-of-limitations debts, infancy debts, bankruptcy discharges, sealed instruments, negotiable mercantile instruments, and legal obligations such as necessaries for minors.
  • Overruled. The opinion overrules no case. It rejects the broad statement of the moral-obligation rule found in "some authorities."
  • Appended citations. The text ends with a list of citations, some under "Contra" and "See also." The opinion does not discuss them, so they should not be treated as authority the court analyzed.
