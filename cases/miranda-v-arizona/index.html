Black-Letter Rule

The prosecution may not use statements, whether inculpatory or exculpatory, stemming from custodial interrogation of the defendant unless it demonstrates the use of procedural safeguards effective to secure the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. Custodial interrogation means questioning initiated by law enforcement officers after a person has been taken into custody or otherwise deprived of his freedom of action in any significant way. Absent other fully effective means, the person must be warned before any questioning that he has the right to remain silent, that anything he says can be used against him in court, that he has the right to an attorney's presence, and that one will be appointed if he cannot afford one; any waiver must be made voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently, and the prosecution must prove it.

Facts

  • Consolidated cases: Four cases (Miranda, Vignera, Westover, Stewart) shared the same features: incommunicado interrogation in a police-dominated room, no full and effective warning at the outset, and resulting incriminating statements admitted at trial.
  • Arrest and custody: Police arrested Ernesto Miranda at his home on March 13, 1963, and took him to a Phoenix police station, where the complaining witness identified him.
  • Interrogation: Two officers questioned Miranda in "Interrogation Room No. 2" of the detective bureau. They admitted at trial that they did not advise him of a right to have an attorney present.
  • Confession: About two hours later the officers had a signed written confession. A typed paragraph at the top stated it was made voluntarily and "with full knowledge of my legal rights." Officers also testified to an earlier oral confession.
  • Defendant: The Court described Miranda as an indigent Mexican defendant who was a seriously disturbed individual.
  • Trial: The written confession came in over objection. Miranda was convicted of kidnapping and rape and sentenced to 20 to 30 years on each count, concurrent.
  • Companion cases: Vignera was questioned by a detective and later by an assistant district attorney with no warnings. Westover had been in local custody for over 14 hours and interrogated at length by local police when FBI agents took over the questioning in the same station; the agents warned him at the start of their session. Stewart was held five days and interrogated nine times, with nothing in the record showing he was ever advised of his rights.

Procedural Posture

The Arizona Supreme Court affirmed Miranda's conviction, emphasizing that he did not specifically request counsel. The New York courts affirmed in Vignera and the Ninth Circuit affirmed in Westover, while the California Supreme Court reversed in Stewart under Escobedo; the Court granted certiorari in all four.

Issue

Does the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination bar the prosecution from using statements obtained through custodial police interrogation when the defendant was not given full warnings of his rights and did not knowingly and intelligently waive them?

Holding

Yes: because custodial interrogation carries inherently compelling pressures, statements obtained from it are inadmissible unless the prosecution shows the required warnings (or a fully effective equivalent) and a knowing, intelligent, voluntary waiver, so the Court, in an opinion by Chief Justice Warren, reversed in Miranda, Vignera, and Westover and affirmed in Stewart.

Reasoning

  • The privilege reaches the station house. The privilege applies outside court proceedings and protects persons in all settings where their freedom of action is curtailed in any significant way. A person swept into custody, surrounded by antagonistic forces, and subjected to persuasion techniques "cannot be otherwise than under compulsion to speak." The compulsion there may be greater than in court, where impartial observers are present.
  • Interrogation is psychologically coercive. The Court relied on police manuals and texts describing isolation, assumed guilt, minimizing the offense, "Mutt and Jeff," false line-ups, and tactics for overriding requests for silence or counsel. Even without brutality or these stratagems, custodial interrogation "trades on the weakness of individuals."
  • History and precedent. The privilege traces to the Lilburn trial and the Star Chamber and has always received a liberal construction (Counselman v. Hitchcock; Hoffman v. United States). Its foundation is the government's respect for the dignity and integrity of citizens, requiring the state to produce evidence by its own labors (Chambers v. Florida; Malloy v. Hogan).
  • Bram and federal cases. Bram v. United States held that the Fifth Amendment controls the voluntariness of confessions in federal court, and Wan v. United States followed it. The Government conceded in Westover that in-custody questioning can violate the privilege.
  • Application to the states. Malloy v. Hogan applied the privilege to the states with full force. The state voluntariness doctrine already covered practices likely to disable free and rational choice.
  • Escobedo reaffirmed and explained. The failure to warn Escobedo of his right to silence was "an essential ingredient" of that decision. The denial of counsel there undermined his ability to exercise the privilege.
  • The required safeguards, element by element:
  • Right to remain silent: Must be stated in clear and unequivocal terms. It is an "absolute prerequisite"; courts will not inquire whether the defendant already knew his rights, because a warning is "a clearcut fact."
  • Anything said can be used against him: This makes the suspect aware of the consequences of forgoing the privilege and that he faces an adversary.
  • Right to counsel present: This covers consultation before questioning and presence during it. No request is needed, since the person who does not ask may most need counsel (Carnley v. Cochran; People v. Dorado).
  • Appointed counsel if indigent: Without it, the counsel warning would mean only a lawyer for those who can pay. This draws on Gideon v. Wainwright and Douglas v. California.
  • Invocation. If the person indicates in any manner, at any time, that he wishes to remain silent, interrogation must cease. If he asks for an attorney, interrogation must cease until one is present. Answering some questions first does not forfeit the right to stop.
  • Waiver. The government bears a "heavy burden" to show a knowing and intelligent waiver (Johnson v. Zerbst). Waiver will not be presumed from silence after warnings or from the fact that a confession was obtained. Lengthy interrogation or incommunicado detention before the statement is strong evidence of no valid waiver. Evidence that the accused was threatened, tricked, or cajoled shows the waiver was involuntary.
  • No inculpatory/exculpatory distinction. Admissions and "exculpatory" statements are covered because the prosecution uses them to impeach or to prove guilt by implication.
  • Policy. Society's need for interrogation cannot abridge a constitutional right. The FBI already gave comparable warnings with an "exemplary record." England, Scotland, India, Ceylon, and military law show no marked harm to law enforcement. Authorities in these cases had considerable other evidence yet interrogated for up to five days.
  • Application to the four cases.
  • Miranda: He was never told of his right to counsel. The typed "full knowledge" clause "does not approach" a valid waiver.
  • Vignera: He received no warnings at all.
  • Westover: The FBI warnings came, from his point of view, at the end of a continuous interrogation in the same station, so the agents benefited from the local pressure.
  • Stewart: The record was silent on warnings. His denial through eight of nine sessions showed he was compelled by persistent interrogation.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • *Justice Clark (dissenting in Miranda, Vignera, Westover; concurring in the result in Stewart; no joiners stated): The majority "goes too far on too little," and its police manuals are scholarly writings, not shown to be any department's official manual. He would apply the due process "totality of circumstances" test from Haynes v. Washington. Under it, failure to give counsel warnings would put the burden on the state to prove waiver or clear voluntariness. He would dismiss Stewart* for want of a final judgment, or affirm on the ground that the state failed its burden, leaving it free to retry.
  • Justice Harlan (dissenting, joined by Justices Stewart and White): Due process voluntariness doctrine is a workable, flexible tool. The Fifth Amendment has never barred all pressure or required knowledge of rights, and the new rules really rest on Sixth Amendment trial-counsel precedents that should not apply in the station house. The rules will not stop officers willing to lie about warnings, will sharply reduce confessions, and preempt ongoing legislative and study-group reform. Miranda's own confession came from brief, daytime questioning by two officers without traditional signs of coercion. He would affirm Miranda, Vignera, and Westover and dismiss Stewart, or else reverse and remand it.
  • Justice White (dissenting, joined by Justices Harlan and Stewart): The holding has "no significant support" in the privilege's text or history, which reached only judicial proceedings. Precedent from Hopt through Cicenia held custodial confessions admissible. The Court's factual premise rests on manuals and no actual interrogation transcripts, and the rule is irrational: it treats the first answer to a single question as compelled while accepting a waiver made in the same chair. The rule effectively installs counsel as arbiter of the privilege, will return criminals to the streets, and leaves open hard questions about custody, spontaneity, waiver, and fruits. He would hold the confessions voluntary, affirm the three convictions, and reverse in Stewart.

Notes

  • Equivalent alternatives allowed. The Constitution does not require these particular warnings. Congress and the states may adopt other procedures that are at least as effective in informing suspects and assuring a continuous opportunity to exercise the right.
  • Limits on scope. General on-the-scene questioning and other general fact-finding questioning of citizens are not affected. Volunteered statements of any kind, such as a person walking into a station to confess, are not barred.
  • No station-house lawyer. Police need not keep a lawyer on hand. If they will not provide counsel within a reasonable period, they may simply refrain from questioning.
  • Silence at trial. The prosecution may not use at trial the fact that the suspect stood mute or claimed the privilege under custodial interrogation (footnote).
  • Indigency warning. This warning need not be given to a person known to have an attorney or ample funds. Any doubt is resolved in favor of giving it (footnote).
  • Counsel present after silence invoked. If the suspect invokes silence but has an attorney present, further questioning may be permissible in some circumstances (footnote).
  • Successive interrogators. The Court did not bar a second authority from questioning someone previously held and questioned without warnings. It said a different case would arise if the person were removed in time and place and then adequately warned.
  • Corroborating evidence irrelevant. Independent evidence of guilt does not affect the analysis (footnote).
  • McNabb-Mallory. Federal officers must still comply with Rule 5(a) and the McNabb/Mallory line (footnote).
  • *Left undecided in Stewart. The California Supreme Court had not reached claims of coercion by threats to hold his wife, lack of a Jackson v. Denno* hearing, and an improper jury instruction.
  • Overruled. Crooker v. California and Cicenia v. Lagay "are not to be followed" (footnote).
  • Reaffirmed. Escobedo v. Illinois.
  • Relied on:
  • Bram v. United States
  • Wan v. United States
  • Malloy v. Hogan
  • Chambers v. Florida
  • Johnson v. Zerbst
  • Carnley v. Cochran

  • Distinguished. Rogers v. United States, on grand jury witnesses answering some questions and refusing others, does not apply to custodial interrogation (footnote).
