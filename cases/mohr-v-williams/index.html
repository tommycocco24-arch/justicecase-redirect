Mohr v. Williams

Black-Letter Rule

A surgeon must have the patient's express or implied consent before operating, and consent to one operation authorizes surgery only to the extent of the consent given. An unconsented, unprivileged operation is a battery even if it is skillful, beneficial, and done without negligence or evil intent, because a civil battery requires only an unlawful or unauthorized touching. Consent may be implied in a genuine emergency, or when a surgeon discovers unanticipated, dangerous conditions during an authorized operation.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff was a patient. Defendant was an ear specialist in St. Paul.
  • Consented procedure: After examining her right ear, defendant advised an operation to remove a polyp and diseased ossicles. Plaintiff consulted her family physician and agreed to an operation on the right ear only.
  • Left ear: Defendant could not fully examine the left ear at the first visit. Plaintiff was never told it was diseased. The evidence tended to show he had called it in good condition, and she testified she had never had trouble with it.
  • Under anesthesia: Once plaintiff was unconscious, defendant thoroughly examined the left ear and found it worse than the right, with diseased and dead bone. Plaintiff's family physician, present at her request, confirmed the diagnosis. Defendant found the right ear less serious than expected.
  • The operation: Defendant performed an ossiculectomy on the left ear instead. The operation was successful and skillfully performed.
  • Family physician's role: He was present to calm plaintiff's fears about anesthesia. He was not there to take part in the operation. Nothing showed he had authority to consent to a change, and he gave no express consent.
  • Claim: Plaintiff alleged the operation impaired her hearing and injured her, and sued for assault and battery.

Procedural Posture

The jury returned a verdict for plaintiff of $14,322.50. The trial court denied defendant's motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict but granted a new trial because the damages were excessive. Defendant appealed the denial of judgment, and plaintiff appealed the grant of a new trial.

Issue

Was the surgeon entitled to judgment as a matter of law on the battery claim because the patient impliedly consented, or because consent was unnecessary for a skillful, beneficial operation performed without negligence or wrongful intent?

Holding

No. A surgeon who operates beyond the patient's consent, without an emergency or other justification, commits at least a technical battery regardless of skill or good intent, and here consent and emergency were jury questions. Both orders were affirmed (Brown, J.; Jaggard, J., took no part; the vote is not otherwise stated).

Reasoning

  • Consent is ordinarily required. The court treated it as beyond doubt that a patient must be consulted and must consent before a physician operates. It relied on Pratt v. Davis on the patient's right to bodily integrity, and on Kinkead's treatise: the patient "must be the final arbiter" of whether to undergo an operation.
  • Consent works like a contract. By weighing the risks and agreeing to a specific operation, the patient "in effect, enters into a contract authorizing his physician to operate to the extent of the consent given, but no further."
  • Physician discretion has limits. Methods of treatment are committed largely to the physician's judgment, but no rule gives "free license respecting surgical operations." The court allowed "reasonable latitude" and would not bar emergency measures.
  • Implied consent exceptions. Consent is implied in two situations:
  • The patient is unconscious from injury and needs prompt surgery to preserve life or limb.
  • During an authorized operation, the surgeon discovers unanticipated conditions that would endanger the patient's life or health if not removed.
  • Neither exception applied as a matter of law. The left ear condition was found during an independent examination after the authorized operation proved unnecessary. It was not discovered in the course of the authorized surgery. The evidence also did not show, as a matter of law, an affliction that would immediately cause serious injury. Whether the condition was urgent was a question for the jury.
  • No consent through the family physician as a matter of law. He was present only to reassure plaintiff, not to participate. Nothing showed authority to approve a change, and he gave no express consent. Whether his silence bound plaintiff was a jury question.
  • Battery needs no wrongful intent or negligence. Citing Jaggard's treatise, the court said every person has a right to complete immunity from physical interference, subject to privilege. Any unlawful or unauthorized touching, other than in pleasantry, is a battery. An unconsented, unjustified operation is wrongful and so unlawful. Criminal battery requires unlawful intent, but a civil action needs only a wrongful and unlawful act or negligence (citing Addison, Lander v. Seaver, and Vosburg v. Putney).
  • Damages. On retrial, the jury should consider the injury's character and extent, the nature of the malady being treated, the operation's beneficial nature, and defendant's good faith.
  • New trial order. Granting a new trial for excessive damages rests in the trial court's sound discretion. There was no abuse of discretion. The court could not reduce the verdict itself because the trial court had already set it aside.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Narrow posture. The court did not hold that defendant committed battery as a matter of law. It held he was not entitled to judgment as a matter of law, because consent and emergency were questions for the jury.
  • Emergency is a fact question here. The court did not decide that the left ear condition fell short of an emergency. It held only that the evidence did not establish one as a matter of law.
  • Good faith goes to damages. Defendant's skill, the operation's benefit, and his good faith do not defeat liability, but they bear on how much plaintiff recovers.
  • Defendant conceded the general rule. Defendant did not argue that consent is ordinarily unnecessary. He argued that consent was implied or excused on these facts.
  • First impression. The court called the consent question new in Minnesota and noted that few cases from other courts were cited.
  • Authorities relied on (tort issues): Pratt v. Davis (bodily integrity); 1 Kinkead Torts § 375 (patient as final arbiter); 1 Jaggard Torts 437 (unauthorized touching is battery); 1 Addison Torts 689, Lander v. Seaver, 32 Vt. 114, and Vosburg v. Putney, 80 Wis. 523 (civil battery requires no unlawful intent).
  • Procedural rule clarified for future guidance: Confusion followed Nelson v. Village of West Duluth, 55 Minn. 497, which said Hicks v. Stone, 13 Minn. 398, did not apply. The court declined to review its former decisions and did not expressly overrule either case. It stated the rule going forward:
  • When damages are unliquidated and within the jury's discretion (personal injury, libel, slander), the motion goes under subdivision four of G.S. 1894 § 5398. The verdict stands unless it is clearly excessive or inadequate and given under passion or prejudice.
  • When damages depend on opinion evidence, the motion goes under subdivision five. In doubtful or mixed cases, it goes under both.
  • Relied on: State v. Shevlin-Carpenter Co., Craig v. Cook, Pratt v. Pioneer Press Co., and Epstein v. Chicago Great Western Ry. Co.
