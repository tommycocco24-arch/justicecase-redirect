Black-Letter Rule

When the government intrudes on choices concerning family living arrangements, the usual judicial deference to land-use legislation does not apply, and the court must examine carefully the importance of the governmental interests advanced and the extent to which the regulation serves them (plurality). The family protected by the Due Process Clause is defined by the Nation's history and tradition, and that tradition extends beyond the nuclear family to relatives such as grandparents, uncles, aunts, and cousins sharing a household. A housing ordinance that criminalizes a grandmother living with her grandson because of which relatives are in the home fails that scrutiny.

Facts

  • Household: Inez Moore lived in her East Cleveland home with her son Dale Moore, Sr., and two grandsons, Dale, Jr., and John Moore, Jr. The grandsons were first cousins. John came to live with his grandmother after his mother's death.
  • Ordinance: The Housing Code limited each dwelling unit to a single "family" (§ 1351.02). Section 1341.08 defined "family" narrowly: spouse, certain unmarried children, parents of the head or spouse, and no more than one dependent child plus that child's spouse and dependent children.
  • Violation: In early 1973 the city notified Mrs. Moore that John was an "illegal occupant." She did not remove him.
  • Prosecution: The city filed a criminal charge. She was convicted and sentenced to five days in jail and a $25 fine.
  • City's justifications: Preventing overcrowding, minimizing traffic and parking congestion, and avoiding an undue financial burden on the school system.
  • Separate density law: A different ordinance (§ 1351.03) tied maximum occupancy to habitable floor area. The Moore household stayed well within those limits.
  • Framing: John's father may also have lived in the home, but the citation named only John, Jr. The Court took the case as the city framed it.
  • No variance sought: Mrs. Moore never applied to the Board of Building Code Appeals for a variance.

Procedural Posture

The municipal court overruled Mrs. Moore's motion to dismiss on facial constitutional grounds and convicted her; the Ohio Court of Appeals affirmed, and the Ohio Supreme Court denied review. The U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction of her appeal.

Issue

Does a housing ordinance that limits occupancy to a "family" defined to exclude certain close relatives, and thereby makes it a crime for a grandmother to live with her grandson, violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment?

Holding

Yes, according to the plurality; the judgment was reversed. Justice Powell announced the judgment of the Court and wrote an opinion for four Justices (himself and Justices Brennan, Marshall, and Blackmun). That plurality concluded that the ordinance's intrusion into family living arrangements requires careful examination of the city's interests, and that the ordinance serves its legitimate goals only marginally and therefore violates due process. This due-process rationale did not command a majority. Justice Stevens supplied the fifth vote for reversal, concurring only in the judgment on the separate ground that the ordinance was an impermissible restriction on Mrs. Moore's right to use her property.

Reasoning

  • Belle Terre distinguished: Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas upheld an ordinance under Euclid's rational-relationship standard. That ordinance reached only unrelated individuals and expressly allowed all persons related by blood, adoption, or marriage to live together. East Cleveland's ordinance "slic[es] deeply into the family itself" by selecting which relatives may live together.
  • Deference displaced: When a city regulates the family this intrusively, neither Belle Terre nor Euclid governs, and the usual deference to the legislature is inappropriate.
  • Family-liberty line of cases: Freedom of personal choice in matters of marriage and family life is a protected liberty (Cleveland Board of Education v. LaFleur). The plurality traced this to Meyer and Pierce and the "private realm of family life which the state cannot enter" (Prince), citing Roe, Yoder, Stanley, Ginsberg, Griswold, Justice Harlan's Poe v. Ullman dissent, Loving, and others.
  • Family is regulable: The family is not beyond regulation (Prince). The required inquiry weighs the importance of the government's interests and how well the regulation serves them (drawing on Harlan's Poe dissent).
  • Means-ends fit fails: The city's goals are legitimate, but the ordinance serves them "marginally, at best." It permits a nuclear family with six licensed drivers and their cars but bars an adult brother and sister who use public transit. It would allow a grandmother to live with one dependent son and a dozen school-age grandchildren, yet forces Mrs. Moore to expel John because his uncle and cousin are present. The relation to the city's goals is "tenuous."
  • No nuclear-family limit: The city argued family rights extend only to a couple and their dependent children. The plurality conceded the prior cases did not involve this relationship but held their "force and rationale" apply here.
  • Limits on substantive due process: The plurality acknowledged the Lochner era as a warning that judicial intervention can track the Justices' predilections. That history counsels caution and restraint, not abandonment, and it does not justify cutting off protection at "the first convenient, if arbitrary boundary," the nuclear family.
  • History and tradition as the limiting principle: Appropriate limits come from "respect for the teachings of history [and] solid recognition of the basic values that underlie our society" (Harlan, Griswold concurrence). The Constitution protects the family "precisely because the institution of the family is deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition."
  • Extended family in that tradition: Uncles, aunts, cousins, and especially grandparents sharing a household have roots "equally venerable." Child-rearing decisions protected in Yoder, Meyer, and Pierce have long been shared with grandparents and other resident relatives, especially in times of adversity such as a parent's death.
  • Pierce analogy: Just as Pierce barred Oregon from standardizing its children through compelled public schooling, East Cleveland may not standardize its children and adults "by forcing all to live in certain narrowly defined family patterns."
  • Exhaustion rejected (footnote): Responding to the Chief Justice, the plurality held exhaustion inappropriate for a criminal defendant raising facial invalidity, an issue the zoning board could not resolve. Cases denying defenses for failure to exhaust (Falbo, Yakus, McGee) rested on statutes giving notice; no Ohio law required exhaustion here.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Brennan (joined by Justice Marshall), concurring: Joins the plurality. Writes to stress the "cultural myopia" of confining family to the nuclear pattern of "white suburbia." The extended family is a pervasive and often necessary living pattern for the poor, immigrants, and minorities, especially black families, though the record would not support a racially discriminatory purpose. Notes that Prince protected an aunt-niece relationship and Pierce protected "parents and guardians." Reads Belle Terre as supporting the result. Argues the variance procedure is irrelevant: Euclid held a challenger need not first seek a variance, and a discretionary "escape hatch" heightens the ordinance's irrationality (analogizing to Roe).
  • Justice Stevens, concurring in the judgment: Frames the question as the owner's right to use her property as she sees fit. Applies the Euclid/Nectow standard. Surveys state cases showing single-family ordinances have never validly gone beyond requiring relation by blood, marriage, or adoption, and state courts have sometimes refused to go that far. No precedent supports excluding an owner's relatives, and the city never explained allowing two grandchildren who are brothers but not cousins. The ordinance is a taking of property without due process and without just compensation.
  • Chief Justice Burger, dissenting: Would not reach the constitutional issue. Mrs. Moore deliberately bypassed an adequate variance procedure, and exhaustion should bar her claim. Cites federal court overload, federalism and comity, and Ashwander constitutional avoidance. She could have sought a variance within 10 days without changing her living arrangements. The criminal posture is not dispositive (McGee, Yakus, Falbo).
  • Justice Stewart (joined by Justice Rehnquist), dissenting: Belle Terre largely answers the privacy and association claims. Freedom of association protects First Amendment purposes, not "gratification, convenience, and economy of sharing the same residence." The interest in sharing a kitchen with some relatives is not "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" and cannot be equated with decisions to marry, bear, and raise children. Under rational-basis review, the city may define "family," and any line produces hard cases. The variance procedure is evidence the ordinance is not arbitrary. Notes East Cleveland is a predominantly black community, answering Brennan's "white suburbia" point. Criticizes Stevens for putting the burden of justification on the city.
  • Justice White, dissenting: Urges extreme reluctance to add substantive content to due process. Agrees Mrs. Moore asserts a protected liberty interest, but the interest in living with more than one set of grandchildren warrants no heightened protection. Rejects Powell's "deeply rooted" tradition test as "a far too expansive charter." The ordinance is duly enacted and not wholly lacking purpose, and she may live with all her grandchildren elsewhere in the metro area. Equal protection fails under the McGowan rational-basis standard. Would affirm.

Notes

  • Plurality only: Powell's due process reasoning commanded four Justices. Stevens supplied the fifth vote for the judgment on a separate property/zoning theory. No single rationale commanded a majority.
  • Equal protection left open: The plurality found it unnecessary to reach the equal protection claim.
  • Scope language: The plurality spoke of "the choice of relatives in this degree of kinship to live together" and expressly said the family "is not beyond regulation." The opinion did not define how far the protected extended family reaches; Stewart's dissent pointedly declined to ask how far it "might extend."
  • Distinguished: Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas (regulated only unrelated persons; promoted "family needs" and "family values"). Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. (deferential land-use standard does not govern intrusive family regulation). Exhaustion cases Falbo, Yakus, McGee (statutes gave notice of forfeiture).
  • Relied on: Meyer, Pierce, Prince, LaFleur, Yoder, Stanley, Ginsberg, Griswold (including Goldberg, White, and Harlan concurrences), Harlan's Poe v. Ullman dissent, Roe, Loving, May v. Anderson, Skinner. Moreno was cited for the significance of the city's separate overcrowding ordinance. McKart supported rejecting exhaustion.
  • Method: The plurality adopted Harlan's history-and-tradition approach and, in a footnote answering White, rejected the "abstract formula" of Palko as less constraining, citing Duncan's historical approach.
  • Lochner: Treated as a cautionary example, not a basis for abandoning substantive due process. The plurality explained Meyer and Pierce survived because they rest on deeply rooted tradition.
  • Overruled: None.
  • Family Law takeaway: The case extends constitutional protection of family living arrangements past the parent-child unit to grandparents and other close relatives, and sets up the contrast with Belle Terre: government may regulate households of unrelated persons with deference but must justify slicing into the family itself.
