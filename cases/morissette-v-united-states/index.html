Black-Letter Rule

When Congress codifies a crime taken over from the common law, such as larceny and its variants, its silence about intent does not eliminate the mental element. The statute carries the intent requirement attached to the borrowed terms. Where intent is an element, it is a question of fact for the jury, and the court may not supply it by a presumption drawn from the act of taking.

Facts

  • Property: The Air Force used a Michigan tract as a practice bombing range. Spent bomb casings were dumped in heaps, some accumulating for four years or more, exposed to the weather and rusting away. Signs warned "Danger — Keep Out — Bombing Range," but the area was extensively hunted.
  • The taking: Morissette, a fruit stand operator and scrap collector, went hunting there, got no deer, and loaded three tons of casings onto his truck. He flattened them with a tractor and sold them in Flint for $84.
  • Openness: He did everything in broad daylight, in full view of passers-by, with no effort at concealment.
  • Candor: When investigated, he promptly told the whole story. He said he thought the casings were abandoned, unwanted, and of no value to the Government, and that he had no intention of stealing.
  • Charge: The indictment alleged he "did unlawfully, wilfully and knowingly steal and convert" government property worth $84, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 641.
  • Trial rulings: The trial court barred evidence and argument that Morissette thought the property was abandoned. It instructed the jury that if he took the property without permission from government land and it was worth one cent or more, he was guilty. When counsel argued that felonious intent was required, the court said intent "is presumed by his own act."

Procedural Posture

Morissette was convicted and sentenced to two months' imprisonment or a $200 fine. The Court of Appeals affirmed, one judge dissenting, holding that knowing conversion under § 641 requires no criminal intent, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does § 641's silence on intent eliminate criminal intent as an element of knowingly converting government property, so that the trial court could refuse to submit intent to the jury and presume it from the act of taking?

Holding

No. Mere omission of intent from § 641 does not eliminate that element from the crimes it covers, including knowing conversion, and intent must be left to the jury without a judicially created presumption. Reversed, in an opinion by Justice Jackson, with Justice Douglas concurring in the result and Justice Minton not participating.

Reasoning

  • Part I: common-law crimes carry intent.
  • The requirement of a culpable mind is "universal and persistent in mature systems of law." It rests on belief in free will and the individual's ability and duty to choose between good and evil.
  • Crime generally requires "concurrence of an evil-meaning mind with an evil-doing hand."
  • When states codified common-law crimes without mentioning intent, their courts read intent in. They treated it as so inherent in the offense that it needed no statutory statement.
  • Public welfare offenses are a separate category.
  • Balint and Behrman involved crimes that consist only of forbidden acts or omissions. These offenses arose from industrialization, traffic, urban crowding, and mass distribution of food, drugs, and securities.
  • The Court identified their features:
  • They punish neglect where the law requires care, or inaction where it imposes a duty, rather than positive aggressions.
  • Violations often cause no direct injury. They create a danger the law seeks to minimize.
  • The injury is the same whatever the violator's intent.
  • The accused can usually prevent the violation with reasonable care.
  • Penalties are relatively small.
  • Conviction does no grave damage to reputation.
  • The Balint and Behrman reasoning is narrow.
  • Read literally, their reasoning would strip intent from every federal crime, because all federal crimes are statutory. The Court refused that reading.
  • The "obstruction" rationale in Balint is circular. Any statute's purpose is "obstructed" by an intent requirement if one assumes Congress meant to convict without it.
  • Larceny is a common-law crime with a long pedigree.
  • Stealing and its variants predate legislation. They stir community insecurity, carry high penalties, and can carry the infamy of a felony.
  • The Court's research found no state court that dropped intent from larceny-type offenses.
  • Congressional silence means something different here.
  • Silence when Congress adopts a well-defined common-law crime warrants a different inference than silence when it creates a new offense.
  • When Congress borrows terms of art, it presumably adopts the cluster of ideas attached to them. Absence of contrary direction signals acceptance of the accepted definitions.
  • The rule that federal courts cannot create crimes counsels against enlarging enacted crimes beyond the components the statutory words contemplate.
  • Eliminating intent would shift the balance against the accused.
  • It would ease the prosecution's path and strip the defendant of the benefit of innocent purpose.
  • It would also restrict juries' freedom. Such an impairment should not be extended to common-law crimes "on judicial initiative."
  • Part II: knowing conversion is not a separate no-intent crime.
  • Section 641 consolidated earlier sections. None had been read to dispense with intent, and the 1948 revision was meant to recodify existing crimes, not create new ones.
  • Each offense in the section is at least a misdemeanor, and a felony at $100 or more. None fits Congress's petty-offense classification.
  • The Government's own indictment charged that Morissette acted "wilfully and knowingly."
  • Congress made only "knowing" conversions criminal. Civil conversion can be committed unwittingly, so the word "knowingly" separates the crime from the tort.
  • Knowing conversion requires knowledge of the facts, though not necessarily the law, that made the taking a conversion. Morissette could not knowingly convert property he believed was abandoned.
  • Reading intent into conversion does not make it redundant.
  • Codifiers grouped overlapping offenses to close gaps between larceny-type crimes.
  • Conversion covers conduct that stealing does not reach: misuse, unauthorized use, use beyond the scope of permission, and commingling funds held under a duty to keep them separate.
  • Conversion can occur without intent to keep and without a wrongful taking, so it adds protection without punishing unwitting conduct.
  • Part III: the trial court made three errors. It treated intent (a) as a question for the court, (b) as a presumption of law, apparently conclusive, and (c) as based on the isolated act of taking.
  • Intent is for the jury. Where intent is an element, it is a question of fact that must go to the jury, however clear the proof seems to the judge (People v. Flack).
  • No presumption of intent.
  • A conclusive presumption would eliminate the element altogether.
  • A permissive presumption from an isolated fact would prejudge what the jury should decide on its own.
  • A presumption unsupported by all the evidence together would give a proven fact an artificial effect.
  • Each version conflicts with the presumption of innocence, which extends to every element. Even Congress's power to substitute presumptions for proof is limited (Tot v. United States).
  • The whole record matters. Taking the casings consciously was admitted. That alone cannot establish intent to steal or knowingly convert, meaning intent wrongfully to deprive another of possession. The jury must weigh the act together with the defendant's testimony and all the surrounding circumstances.
  • Either verdict was possible.
  • A properly instructed jury could have rejected his story as an afterthought. It could rely on his awareness that he was on government land, his failure to ask permission, and his self-interest as a witness.
  • The jury could also have found the rusting heaps looked abandoned. His good character, the openness of the taking, and his candor could support acquittal.
  • Either verdict would have ended the matter.

Notes

  • No closed test.
  • The Court expressly declined to draw a precise line or set comprehensive criteria for separating crimes that require a mental element from those that do not. It said the law there "is neither settled nor static."
  • The features of public welfare offenses are the Court's description of that category. They are not stated as a formal test.
  • Balint and Behrman: approved but confined.
  • United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250, and United States v. Behrman, 258 U.S. 280, keep the Court's "approval and adherence for the circumstances to which it was there applied."
  • The Court refused to treat them as authority for removing intent from offenses incorporated from the common law.
  • The question in Morissette was framed as whether to expand the doctrine of crimes without intent. The Court declined to do so.
  • Dotterweich relied on. United States v. Dotterweich, 320 U.S. 277, ties strict liability to the nature of the offense. It covers a person "standing in responsible relation to a public danger," and the Court quoted its warning about hardship.
  • State and English lineage of strict liability. The Court traced public welfare offenses through these cases:
  • Barnes v. State (Conn. 1849).
  • The Massachusetts adulterated-milk cases.
  • People v. Roby (Cooley, J.).
  • Tenement House Department v. McDevitt and People ex rel. Price v. Sheffield Farms Co. (Cardozo, J.).
  • Nineteenth-century English food and tobacco cases (footnote).
  • Imprisonment for strict-liability offenses. The Court quoted Cardozo's reservation in Sheffield Farms, which left open whether unintended violations could be punished by imprisonment. Morissette itself did not resolve that question.
  • Congress can still eliminate intent. In a footnote, the Court said policy concerns would not justify disregarding a clear command from Congress to eliminate intent. They counsel caution before assuming Congress meant to do so without clear expression.
  • Civil liability distinguished.
  • In a footnote, the Court said Morissette could be held liable for civil conversion. The trial court's instructions might have been appropriate in a civil action.
  • That assumes actual abandonment was not proven, which petitioner "should be allowed to prove if he can."
  • Presumptions.
  • Kemp v. State (Fla.) was cited to show that courts can frame presumptions running the opposite way: open taking and avowal suggest no felonious intent.
  • The Court's point was that presumptive intent "has no place in this case."
  • Disposition. The text says only "Reversed."
