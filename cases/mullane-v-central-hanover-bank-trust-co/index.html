Black-Letter Rule

Due process requires, in any proceeding to be accorded finality, notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections. The means chosen must be one that a person actually desiring to inform the absentee might reasonably adopt. Publication suffices for parties who are missing, unknown, or not reasonably ascertainable, but parties whose names and addresses are known must get at least notice by ordinary mail.

Facts

  • Statute: N.Y. Banking Law § 100-c lets a trust company pool many small trusts into one common fund. Periodic judicial settlements of the trustee's accounts bind everyone with any interest in the fund or a participating trust.
  • The fund: Central Hanover set up a common fund in January 1946. It held 113 trusts and nearly $3 million in gross capital. Beneficiaries were many, and some lived outside New York.
  • Accounting: In March 1947 the bank petitioned the Surrogate's Court to settle its first account.
  • Notice given: Only newspaper publication, once a week for four weeks, as § 100-c(12) required. The notice did not name beneficiaries. It listed the trust company, the fund, and the participating trusts.
  • Earlier mailing: When each trust first invested in the fund, the bank mailed known adult beneficiaries a notice that included the statute's accounting provisions, as § 100-c(9) required.
  • Representation: The court appointed Mullane special guardian for all income beneficiaries not otherwise appearing and appointed Vaughan for principal beneficiaries. No one else appeared.
  • Effect of decree: The decree cuts off every beneficiary claim against the trustee for mismanagement during the accounting period. Beneficiaries' interests may also be diminished by fees paid to the guardian.

Procedural Posture

Mullane appeared specially and objected that the statutory notice violated due process, so the court lacked jurisdiction to enter a binding decree. The Surrogate overruled the objection and entered a final decree, and the Appellate Division and the New York Court of Appeals affirmed. The case came to the Supreme Court on appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1257.

Issue

Does notice by newspaper publication alone, as required by N.Y. Banking Law § 100-c(12), satisfy the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause as to common trust fund beneficiaries in a judicial settlement of accounts that cuts off their rights?

Holding

No as to beneficiaries whose names and addresses are known, because publication is not reasonably calculated to reach people who could easily be notified by mail; yes as to beneficiaries who are unknown, whose whereabouts could not be ascertained with due diligence, or whose interests are conjectural or future. Reversed and remanded (Jackson, J., for the Court; Burton, J., dissenting; Douglas, J., not participating).

Reasoning

  • Power to adjudicate: Appellant argued that under Pennoyer v. Neff the proceeding was in personam, so the court had no jurisdiction over nonresidents not personally served. The Court declined to classify the proceeding as in rem or in personam. It called those categories "elusive and confused" and noted that they vary by state.
  • State interest supplies power: A state's interest in closing trusts that exist under its laws and are supervised by its courts establishes its courts' right to determine the interests of all claimants, resident or nonresident. That holds only if the procedure gives full opportunity to appear and be heard.
  • Deprivation of property: The decree may cut off claims against the trustee and may reduce beneficiaries' interests through fees. Due process notice standards therefore apply.
  • Balancing: The state's interest in final settlement, including over nonresidents, is weighed against the individual's right to be heard (Grannis v. Ordean). The right to be heard is worth little without notice that the matter is pending.
  • The test: The Court drew the rule from Milliken v. Meyer, Grannis, Priest v. Las Vegas, and Roller v. Holly. Its elements:
  • Notice must be reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action.
  • It must reasonably convey the required information.
  • It must allow a reasonable time to appear.
  • If those conditions are reasonably met with due regard for the practicalities of the case, the Constitution is satisfied. Possible injury in some conceivable case does not defeat the method (American Land Co. v. Zeiss).
  • "Mere gesture" principle: The means must be what one desirous of actually informing the absentee might reasonably adopt. A method can be defended in two ways:
  • It is reasonably certain to inform those affected (compare Hess v. Pawloski with Wuchter v. Pizzutti).
  • Where conditions do not permit such notice, it is not substantially less likely to give notice than other feasible and customary substitutes.
  • Publication is weak: Notice in small type in a local paper reaches people by chance. It is worse for those living outside the paper's circulation area and worse still when it does not name them. The Court called it "a feint."
  • No reinforcing steps: Publication works as a supplement where seizure of tangible property is itself likely to alert the owner, or where the owner may be presumed to have abandoned the property or left a caretaker (The Mary; Ballard v. Hunter; Huling). Here the caretaker, the trustee, is the beneficiaries' adversary in the accounting. The special guardian has no duty to contact them.
  • Unknown and missing parties: Publication is acceptable where better notice is not reasonably possible (Cunnius; Blinn v. Nelson; Jacob v. Roberts).
  • Conjectural or future interests: No extended searches are required. Constant investigation into the status of many remote beneficiaries would burden the plan and erase its advantages. The Court deferred to state judgment on those practical matters.
  • Known beneficiaries: The trustee has their names and addresses and already sends them income. Mail is efficient and cheap. The bank had already mailed notice when the fund was set up, which shows mailing would not seriously burden the plan. A copy of the statute sent months or years earlier does not suffice.
  • Personal service not required: The beneficiaries' interests are identical to the class's interest. Notice reaching most of them will protect all, because any successful objection benefits everyone. Some risk that notice misses a few is acceptable (Blinn).
  • Prudent businessman check: The Court asked whether a prudent businessman, counting his pennies but wanting to reach people in his files, would rely on publication. It concluded he would not (McDonald v. Mabee: do not let fiction deny fair play).

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Burton, J. (dissenting, alone): Common trusts are available only when the instruments creating the participating trusts permit participation. Whether to add notice beyond the publication and representation provided is within the state's discretion, and the Constitution does not require more here.

Notes

  • Scope of holding: The holding is limited to "known persons whose whereabouts are also known" being deprived of "substantial property rights." Publication remains valid as to unknown, missing, or not reasonably ascertainable beneficiaries and those with conjectural or future interests.
  • Minimum required: The Court required "at least" ordinary mail to record addresses. It expressly declined to weigh whether personal service on all known beneficiaries would unduly burden the fund, saying only that personal service is not required here.
  • Left open: The Court reserved "whatever searches might be required in another situation under ordinary standards of diligence."
  • Left open: The Court did not disparage the in rem/in personam distinction "in many branches of law, or on other issues." It declined only to rest the state's power in this proceeding on that classification.
  • No precedent overruled: The Court said it "disturb[s] none of the established rules" on personal service and constructive notice. It said no decision was controlling or "even a very illuminating precedent."
  • *Pennoyer v. Neff:* Invoked by appellant. The Court neither applied nor overruled it and decided the power question without resolving the in rem/in personam label.
  • Relied on for the core standard: Milliken v. Meyer, Grannis v. Ordean, Priest v. Las Vegas, Roller v. Holly; also Goodrich v. Ferris (cf.), American Land Co. v. Zeiss, Blinn v. Nelson.
  • **Hess v. Pawloski compared with Wuchter v. Pizzutti:* Cited to show a method may be defended as reasonably certain to inform. Wuchter* is cited again for requiring mail to known addresses.
  • Tangible property and publication: Anderson National Bank v. Luckett and Security Savings Bank v. California (abandonment); Ballard v. Hunter, Huling v. Kaw Valley R. Co., and The Mary (caretaker). These were distinguished because the caretaker here is the adversary.
  • Unknown or missing persons: Cunnius v. Reading School District, Blinn v. Nelson, Jacob v. Roberts. These were followed as to unknown beneficiaries.
  • Fiction versus fact: McDonald v. Mabee, cited for not letting fictional notice defeat fair play.
