Black-Letter Rule

The Commerce Clause, even with the Necessary and Proper Clause, lets Congress regulate existing commercial activity. It does not let Congress compel individuals to enter commerce by buying a product because their inactivity affects interstate commerce. Congress may impose an exaction on those who forgo health insurance under its taxing power if the exaction functions as a tax in practice, whatever its label. Congress may attach conditions to new federal grants, but it may not coerce States into a new program by threatening to withhold their existing grant funds, because Spending Clause legislation is valid only when the State has a genuine choice to accept or decline.

Facts

  • The Act: In 2010 Congress enacted the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act to increase insurance coverage and decrease health care costs. Two provisions are challenged here: the individual mandate and the Medicaid expansion.
  • Individual mandate: 26 U.S.C. § 5000A requires most Americans to maintain "minimum essential" coverage. Starting in 2014, non-exempt individuals who do not comply must make a "shared responsibility payment," which the Act calls a "penalty."
  • Payment mechanics: The payment is calculated from household income, with a floor and a ceiling. The ceiling is the average cost of qualifying insurance. Individuals pay it to the IRS with their taxes. The IRS may not use criminal prosecution, liens, or levies to collect it. People below the income-tax filing threshold owe nothing.
  • Congress's rationale: Uninsured people shift costs to the insured. The Act's guaranteed-issue and community-rating reforms would encourage healthy people to wait until they are sick to buy coverage. The mandate brings healthy people into the risk pool.
  • Medicaid expansion: The Act requires States to cover all individuals under 65 with incomes below 133 percent of the federal poverty line. The federal government pays 100 percent of the costs for newly eligible people through 2016, declining to a 90 percent minimum.
  • The funding threat: Under 42 U.S.C. § 1396c, a State that does not comply may lose all of its federal Medicaid funds, not only the expansion funds. Federal Medicaid funds make up over 10 percent of most States' total revenue. Medicaid accounts for over 20 percent of the average State's budget.
  • Parties: Florida and other States, several individuals, and the National Federation of Independent Business sued federal officials.

Procedural Posture

The District Court for the Northern District of Florida held the mandate unconstitutional, found it non-severable, and struck down the entire Act. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed that the mandate exceeded Congress's power. It held unanimously that the mandate was not a tax, and by majority that it exceeded the commerce power. It reversed on severability, leaving the rest of the Act intact, and unanimously upheld the Medicaid expansion under the Spending Clause. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and appointed amici to argue severability and the Anti-Injunction Act question.

Issue

Are the ACA's individual mandate, and its threat to withhold a State's existing Medicaid funds if the State rejects the expansion, within Congress's Article I powers?

Holding

Yes as to the mandate, and no as to the Medicaid funding threat. Chief Justice Roberts, writing only for himself on this point, concluded that the mandate exceeds the commerce power; the four joint dissenters reached the same conclusion separately, while Justices Ginsburg, Sotomayor, Breyer, and Kagan would have upheld the mandate under the Commerce Clause. Writing for the Court with Justices Ginsburg, Sotomayor, Breyer, and Kagan, the Chief Justice held that the mandate is a valid exercise of the taxing power. Seven Justices agreed that withholding existing Medicaid funds from States that refuse the expansion is unconstitutionally coercive. The remedy bars that withholding and leaves the rest of the Act standing, and the Eleventh Circuit's judgment is affirmed in part and reversed in part.

Reasoning

Anti-Injunction Act (Part II, opinion of the Court)

  • The Anti-Injunction Act bars suits to restrain the collection of "any tax." Congress labeled the payment a "penalty" and called many other exactions in the Act "taxes." Under Russello, that difference in wording is presumed intentional.
  • Whether the Anti-Injunction Act applies is up to Congress, so Congress's label controls. Bailey v. George applied the Act to a statutory "tax" even though Drexel Furniture held the same exaction was not really a tax.
  • Section 5000A(g)(1) directs the Secretary to assess and collect the payment "in the same manner" as taxes. That provision governs IRS procedures. It does not make the Anti-Injunction Act apply. Section 6201(a) does not equate assessable penalties with taxes, because the Code consistently treats the two terms as distinct.
  • The suit is not barred, so the Court reached the merits.

Commerce Clause (Part III-A, Roberts alone)

  • Text: The power to "regulate" commerce presupposes existing commercial activity. The Constitution separately grants the powers to "coin Money" and "raise and support Armies" alongside the powers to regulate them. Those separate grants would be superfluous if "regulate" included the power to create.
  • Precedent: Every Commerce Clause case, including Jones & Laughlin, Wickard, Perez, and Lopez, describes the power as reaching "activity."
  • Novelty: Congress has never used the commerce power to compel people to buy an unwanted product. Under Free Enterprise Fund, the lack of historical precedent can signal a constitutional problem.
  • Wickard distinguished: The farmer in Wickard was actively producing wheat. The government's theory would let Congress order people to buy wheat, or vegetables, whenever enough of them failed to.
  • Limiting principle: Regulating inactivity would bring countless individual decisions within federal power and change the relationship between citizen and federal government. Any police power over individuals as such remains with the States.
  • Government's arguments rejected:
  • Being "active in the market for health care" has no constitutional significance.
  • Congress may regulate classes of activities. It may not regulate classes of individuals apart from any activity (Raich, Perez).
  • Congress may anticipate the effects of economic activity (Heart of Atlanta, McClung). It may not anticipate the activity itself.
  • Health insurance is not unique. Insurance and health care are different transactions, entered at different times, with different providers.

Necessary and Proper Clause (Part III-A, Roberts alone)

  • The Clause allows means that are incidental to an enumerated power. It does not grant a "great substantive and independent power" (McCulloch).
  • A law that undermines constitutional structure is not "proper" (Printz, New York).
  • The cases upholding laws under the Clause (Comstock, Sabri, Jinks) involved authority derived from and serving a granted power. The mandate instead lets Congress create the predicate for exercising its power.
  • Raich distinguished: That case involved individual applications of a concededly valid scheme regulating existing activity.

Saving construction (Part III-B, Roberts alone)

  • The most natural reading of § 5000A is a command to buy insurance. Because the Commerce Clause cannot support a command, the Court asks whether a tax reading is "fairly possible" (Crowell v. Benson; Hooper).

Taxing power (Part III-C, opinion of the Court)

  • Looks like a tax: Taxpayers pay it to the Treasury with their returns. Its amount turns on taxable income, dependents, and filing status. The IRS enforces it, and it raises revenue (Kahriger).
  • Label not controlling: For constitutional purposes, the Court looks to substance over the label (Constantine, License Tax Cases, New York, Sotelo).
  • Drexel Furniture functional factors, applied:
  • Burden: For most people the payment is far less than the price of insurance, and by statute it can never be more. Paying it may be a reasonable choice, unlike the "prohibitory" exaction in Drexel.
  • Scienter: The provision has none.
  • Enforcement: Only the IRS collects it, through normal means, minus the most punitive tools.
  • Regulatory purpose is fine: Taxes that seek to influence conduct are old and common (Sanchez, Sonzinsky).
  • No unlawful act: A penalty punishes an unlawful act (CF&I; La Franca). The statute need not be read to make going uninsured unlawful. The government conceded that paying the exaction is full compliance with the law. Congress expected about four million people a year to pay, which suggests it did not view them as lawbreakers. New York v. United States read "shall" language as incentives.
  • Direct Tax Clause: The payment is not a capitation, because it is triggered by specific circumstances. It is not a tax on land or personal property. It therefore need not be apportioned (Hylton, Springer, Pollock).
  • Taxing inactivity: Three points allay the concern.
  • The Constitution has never guaranteed that people can avoid taxes through inactivity.
  • The taxing power has limits, though the Court does not fix where an exaction becomes a penalty.
  • A tax can only require payment of money. It leaves a lawful choice.

Why reach the Commerce Clause (Part III-D, Roberts alone)

  • The statute reads more naturally as a command. Only because the command is invalid is the saving construction available.

Medicaid expansion (Part IV-A, Roberts with Breyer and Kagan)

  • Spending Clause legislation is like a contract. It is legitimate only if the State "voluntarily and knowingly" accepts the terms (Pennhurst, Barnes).
  • Congress may not use financial inducements that turn "pressure" into "compulsion" (Steward Machine). Coercion threatens political accountability in the same way commandeering does (New York, Printz).
  • Conditions that govern how granted funds are used are permissible. Threats to terminate other significant, independent grants are a means of pressuring policy change.
  • Dole distinguished: South Dakota risked 5 percent of its highway funds, less than half of one percent of its budget. That was "relatively mild encouragement." Here a State risks all of its Medicaid funds, over 10 percent of its overall budget. The Court called this "a gun to the head" and "economic dragooning."
  • New program: The expansion is a "shift in kind." It transforms Medicaid from a program for four categories of the needy into part of a national plan for universal coverage. Separate funding rates and a different benefit package confirm this.
  • Notice: Congress reserved the right to "alter" or "amend" Medicaid (§ 1304). A State could not have anticipated a transformation this dramatic. Pennhurst bars surprising States with post-acceptance conditions.
  • No line drawn: As in Steward Machine, the Court declines to fix the outer line. It holds that this statute is beyond it.

Remedy (Part IV-B, joined in judgment by Ginsburg and Sotomayor)

  • The Secretary may not apply § 1396c to withdraw existing Medicaid funds from States that refuse the expansion. Congress may still offer expansion funds and enforce conditions on them.
  • The severability clause (§ 1303) preserves other applications of the statute. The test is legislative intent (Booker, Ayotte). Congress would have wanted the rest of the Act to stand even if some States decline the expansion (Champlin).

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Ginsburg (with Justice Sotomayor; Justices Breyer and Kagan join Parts I–IV), concurring in part, concurring in the judgment in part, and dissenting in part.
  • She joins the Court on the Anti-Injunction Act and the tax holding.
  • She would also uphold the mandate under the Commerce and Necessary and Proper Clauses. Under the rational-basis test (Raich, Hodel), the uninsured as a class substantially affect interstate commerce. Everyone inevitably consumes health care, and the uninsured shift costs to others.
  • She calls the activity/inactivity line formalistic and compares it to abandoned distinctions such as production versus commerce (E.C. Knight, Carter Coal) and direct versus indirect effects (Schechter).
  • She reads Wickard and Raich as permitting regulation based on prophesied future transactions. She calls the "broccoli horrible" a chain of inferences of the kind rejected in Lopez and Morrison.
  • She calls the Chief Justice's Commerce Clause analysis non-outcome-determinative.
  • In Part V, joined only by Justice Sotomayor, she would uphold the Medicaid expansion as an amendment to a single program. In her view, § 1304 and Bowen gave States notice, and Pennhurst requires clarity only when funds are accepted. She calls the coercion inquiry judicially unmanageable.
  • Given the majority's holding, she agrees with the Chief Justice's remedy.
  • Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito, dissenting (joint opinion).
  • The mandate exceeds the commerce power because it directs the creation of commerce. It fails the Necessary and Proper Clause because it is not "proper," and Raich is distinguishable.
  • The mandate cannot be read as a tax. Congress framed it as a requirement with a penalty and called it a "penalty" eighteen times. It exempts some people from the payment who are still subject to the mandate. It sits in Title I, away from the Act's revenue provisions. Treating it as a tax is judicial tax-writing that offends the Origination Clause and raises an under-briefed Direct Tax Clause question.
  • Because the payment is not a tax, the Anti-Injunction Act does not apply.
  • The Medicaid expansion is coercive. The Act's structure shows Congress assumed no State could refuse, since it provides no backup coverage for the poor.
  • The proper remedy is to invalidate the expansion. The Court's remedy rewrites § 1396c.
  • Because the mandate and the expansion are the Act's central pillars, the entire Act is non-severable and should fall.

Notes

  • Fractured opinion: The Commerce Clause and Necessary and Proper analysis (Part III-A) is the Chief Justice's opinion alone. The four joint dissenters reach the same conclusion in a separate opinion, and the Chief Justice cites them with "Accord." Justice Ginsburg calls that analysis unnecessary to the outcome.
  • Medicaid split: The Medicaid coercion analysis (Part IV-A) is a three-Justice opinion. The joint dissent states that seven Justices agree the expansion as enacted is unconstitutional.
  • Expressly left open: The Court did not decide "the precise point at which an exaction becomes so punitive that the taxing power does not authorize it." It did not fix the line "where persuasion gives way to coercion."
  • Limits of the tax holding: The Court states that it does not hold any exaction lacking scienter and enforced by the IRS is a tax. Congress could not expand its power to impose criminal fines by creating strict-liability offenses enforced by the IRS.
  • Labels: The "penalty" label decides the Anti-Injunction Act question but does not decide the constitutional taxing-power question.
  • Limits of the Medicaid holding: The holding reaches only withholding of existing funds for refusing the expansion. Section 1396c still applies to the existing program and to States that accept the expansion and then fail to comply.
  • First coercion holding: Justice Ginsburg states this is the first time the Court has found a spending condition unconstitutionally coercive.
  • Precedents relied on: McCulloch, Gibbons, Lopez, Morrison, Drexel Furniture (the functional tax/penalty factors), Bailey v. George, New York v. United States, Printz, Steward Machine, Pennhurst, Crowell v. Benson, Hylton, Springer.
  • Precedents distinguished: Wickard and Raich involved existing activity. South Dakota v. Dole involved a small percentage of funds and a condition related to the funds.
  • Overruled: The opinion overrules no precedent.
