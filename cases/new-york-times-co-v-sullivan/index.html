Black-Letter Rule

The First and Fourteenth Amendments bar a public official from recovering damages for a defamatory falsehood relating to his official conduct unless he proves "actual malice," meaning the statement was made with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not. Malice cannot be presumed, and the proof must have "convincing clarity." A State also may not treat an impersonal attack on government operations as libel of the official in charge based solely on that official's position.

Facts

  • Parties: Sullivan, an elected Montgomery, Alabama City Commissioner who supervised the Police Department, sued the New York Times Company and four Alabama clergymen whose names appeared on a full-page advertisement.
  • The advertisement: "Heed Their Rising Voices" described a "wave of terror" against Southern civil rights demonstrators and asked for funds for the student movement, voting rights, and Dr. King's legal defense. It never named Sullivan or his office.
  • Challenged statements: The third paragraph said "truckloads of police" "ringed" the Alabama State College campus and that the dining hall was padlocked "to starve them into submission." The sixth paragraph said "they" bombed Dr. King's home, assaulted him, arrested him seven times, and charged him with perjury.
  • Falsity: Several statements were inaccurate. Students sang the National Anthem, police were deployed near the campus but never "ringed" it, the dining hall was never padlocked, and Dr. King had been arrested four times. The bombings and three of the arrests predated Sullivan's tenure.
  • "Of and concerning": Sullivan and six witnesses testified they read the statements as referring to him because he supervised the police.
  • No pecuniary loss: Sullivan did not try to prove actual pecuniary loss, and no witness testified to actually believing the statements as applied to him.
  • Times's handling: The Times relied on a letter from A. Philip Randolph certifying that the listed names were authorized and on the sponsors' reputations. No one checked the ad against the Times's own news files. The individual petitioners testified they never authorized use of their names.
  • Retraction: The Times declined Sullivan's retraction demand and wrote back asking how the ad referred to him. Sullivan sued without answering. The Times later retracted for Governor Patterson.
  • Jury instructions: The judge charged that the statements were libelous per se, that falsity, malice, and general damages were presumed, and that punitive damages could be awarded without proof of actual damages. He did not require the jury to separate general from punitive damages.

Procedural Posture

A jury in the Circuit Court of Montgomery County awarded Sullivan the full $500,000 claimed against all defendants, and the Supreme Court of Alabama affirmed in all respects. The U.S. Supreme Court granted the separate certiorari petitions of the individual petitioners and the Times.

Issue

Do the First and Fourteenth Amendments limit a State's power to award libel damages to a public official against critics of his official conduct when the State presumes falsity, malice, and damages and makes truth the only defense?

Holding

Yes: Justice Brennan, for the Court, held that a public official may recover for a defamatory falsehood about his official conduct only on proof of actual malice, and that the record evidence was constitutionally insufficient both on actual malice and on whether the statements were "of and concerning" Sullivan. The judgment was reversed and remanded.

Reasoning

  • State action: A civil suit between private parties still involves state action. Alabama courts applied a state rule of law, and the test is whether state power was exercised, whatever its form (Ex parte Virginia; American Federation of Labor v. Swing).
  • Paid advertisement: The ad does not lose protection because it was paid for. Valentine v. Chrestensen involved "purely commercial advertising." This ad communicated information, expressed opinion, and sought support for a movement of the highest public concern. Payment is as immaterial as the fact that newspapers are sold (Smith v. California). Otherwise papers would drop "editorial advertisements," a key outlet for people without their own presses.
  • Libel has no "talismanic immunity": Earlier statements that the Constitution does not protect libel never sustained libel sanctions for criticism of officials' official conduct. Libel, like contempt or breach of the peace, "must be measured by standards that satisfy the First Amendment."
  • National commitment: Debate on public issues should be "uninhibited, robust, and wide-open," and may include "vehement, caustic, and sometimes unpleasantly sharp attacks on government and public officials." The ad, as protest on a major public issue, qualifies for protection.
  • Factual error alone is insufficient: The First Amendment recognizes no test of truth, especially one placing the burden on the speaker. Erroneous statement is inevitable in free debate and must be protected so expression has "breathing space" (N.A.A.C.P. v. Button; Sweeney v. Patterson).
  • Injury to official reputation alone is insufficient: Criticism of judges cannot be punished as contempt even when it contains "half-truths" and "misinformation" (Bridges; Pennekamp; Craig v. Harney; Wood v. Georgia). Elected commissioners must be equally hardy.
  • Falsity plus defamation is still insufficient: The Sedition Act of 1798 punished false, scandalous, and malicious writings against government, allowed truth as a defense, and was still condemned in "the court of history" by Madison, Jefferson, Congress, and Justices of the Court. The Fourteenth Amendment extends that limit to the States (Gitlow).
  • Civil liability can chill more than criminal law: This award was 1,000 times the maximum fine under Alabama's criminal libel statute and 100 times the Sedition Act's maximum, with no indictment, no reasonable-doubt standard, and no double-jeopardy bar on repeated suits.
  • Truth defense does not save the rule: As Smith v. California required guilty knowledge for booksellers, critics need protection for honest error. Making the speaker prove truth deters even true speech because of doubt about proving it in court or the expense of doing so. Speakers "steer far wider of the unlawful zone" (Speiser v. Randall).
  • Source of the rule: The Court adopted a privilege like the one in Coleman v. MacLennan (Kan. 1908), followed by a number of state courts and favored by scholarly opinion.
  • Parity with official immunity: Under Barr v. Matteo, officials are privileged when sued by citizens for statements within the "outer perimeter" of their duties. Citizen-critics deserve "a fair equivalent," because it is as much their duty to criticize as the official's duty to administer.
  • Application to the jury verdict: Alabama presumed malice for general damages, which is inconsistent with the federal rule. The general verdict made it impossible to know whether the award was general or punitive, so the judgment had to be reversed (Stromberg).
  • Independent review of the record: Because Sullivan might retry the case, the Court reviewed the evidence itself, as required where the line between protected and unprotected speech is at stake (Pennekamp; Edwards v. South Carolina). The Seventh Amendment does not bar this review where law and fact are intermingled (Fiske v. Kansas).
  • No actual malice, individual petitioners: There was no evidence they knew of any errors or were reckless.
  • No actual malice, the Times:
  • The Secretary's view that the ad was "substantially correct" was reasonable and showed no malice at the time of publication.
  • The refusal to retract was not a final refusal, and the Times reasonably doubted the ad referred to Sullivan.
  • The Governor's retraction had a reasonable, unimpeached explanation.
  • Stories in the Times's files did not show knowledge, because the state of mind must be "brought home" to the people responsible for publication. Their failure to check showed at most negligence, which falls short of recklessness.
  • Not "of and concerning" Sullivan: The ad did not name him or his office. Several statements did not concern the police at all. The witnesses connected him to the ad only through his official position. Alabama's theory would turn impersonal criticism of government into personal libel of officials, evading the rule that libel on government has no place in American law (City of Chicago v. Tribune Co.).

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Black, joined by Justice Douglas (concurring): The First and Fourteenth Amendments completely prohibit, and do more than "delimit," state power to award libel damages to public officials for criticism of official conduct. "Malice" is elusive and gives only "evanescent protection." The record, including the pending suits by Alabama officials, shows that libel law threatens the press. Black assumed authorization and reference to Sullivan were proved and voted to reverse solely on an absolute right to criticize officials and discuss public affairs.
  • Justice Goldberg, joined by Justice Douglas (concurring in the result): Citizens and the press hold an absolute, unconditional privilege to criticize official conduct, even when the criticism is false or malicious. A jury's probe into the speaker's state of mind cannot adequately protect speech, and proving malice may add little to proving falsity. Criticism of government is necessarily "of and concerning" the governors. Goldberg would leave defamation about the private conduct of officials or private citizens unprotected, since it has little to do with self-government. Like Barr v. Matteo immunity for officials, critics deserve equal immunity, and officials can answer with counterargument.

Notes

  • Torts frame: The Court displaced key features of common-law libel per se as applied to public officials suing over official conduct. Under Alabama's rules, falsity, malice, and general damages were presumed, and the defendant had to prove truth "in all its particulars." The plaintiff now bears the burden on actual malice, and malice may not be presumed.
  • Elements as stated in the opinion:
  • The plaintiff is a public official.
  • The statement is a defamatory falsehood relating to his official conduct.
  • The statement is "of and concerning" the plaintiff, which official position alone cannot establish for an impersonal attack on government.
  • The plaintiff proves actual malice, meaning knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard, with convincing clarity.
  • Actual malice is not negligence: Failure to check facts available in one's own files showed at most negligence. Common-law "malice" in the sense of motive is different. Under Alabama law, good motives and belief in truth did not negate an inference of malice and were relevant only in mitigation of punitive damages; the Court held inconsistent with the federal rule Alabama's presumption of malice for general damages.
  • Conditional privilege: The Court calls this a "conditional" privilege for honest misstatements of fact, distinguished from the "absolute" privilege in judicial, legislative, administrative, and executive proceedings.
  • Fair comment (footnote): The Fourteenth Amendment requires a fair-comment defense for honest opinion based on privileged, as well as true, facts. Both defenses are defeasible on proof of actual malice.
  • Expressly left open:
  • How far down the ranks of government employees the "public official" designation extends.
  • The boundaries of "official conduct."
  • Whether a failure to retract may ever be evidence of malice.
  • Whether ruling that small discrepancies injured Sullivan's reputation raises its own constitutional problems.
  • The sufficiency of the punitive-damages malice instructions under the federal standard.
  • Not decided: The individual petitioners' due process claim that they never authorized the publication, and their due process and equal protection claims of courtroom segregation and racial bias. The Times's personal-jurisdiction claim was foreclosed because Alabama courts found it entered a general appearance.
  • Distinguished:
  • Valentine v. Chrestensen: purely commercial handbill with a protest added to evade an ordinance.
  • Beauharnais v. Illinois: group libel likely to cause violence, where the Court reserved power to nullify action that punishes speech "under the guise of punishing libel."
  • Pennekamp dictum: it implied no view on what remedy officials may constitutionally receive.
  • Schenectady Union Pub. Co. v. Sweeney: equally divided Court, question undecided.
  • Relied on:
  • Smith v. California (scienter and self-censorship).
  • Speiser v. Randall (burden of proof and "wider of the unlawful zone").
  • N.A.A.C.P. v. Button ("breathing space"; libel as a "mere label").
  • Barr v. Matteo (official privilege analogy).
  • Coleman v. MacLennan (model rule).
  • Bridges, Pennekamp, Craig v. Harney, Wood v. Georgia (criticism of judges).
  • Bantam Books v. Sullivan (civil regulation riskier than criminal).
  • Stromberg (general verdict).
  • Fiske v. Kansas (independent review).
  • Brandeis's Whitney concurrence and the Sedition Act history.
  • Overruled: The opinion does not state that it overrules any prior decision.
