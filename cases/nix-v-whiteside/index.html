Black-Letter Rule

A criminal defendant's Sixth Amendment right to counsel is not violated when defense counsel refuses to help the defendant present testimony counsel knows to be perjured, including by warning that counsel will seek to withdraw, disclose the perjury to the court, and possibly impeach the false testimony. Under Strickland v. Washington, such conduct falls within the range of reasonable professional assistance. As a matter of law it causes no cognizable prejudice, because a defendant has no right to testify falsely or to have counsel assist perjury.

Facts

  • The killing: Whiteside stabbed Calvin Love to death during an argument over marihuana in Love's apartment. Love had told his girlfriend to get his "piece." Whiteside claimed self-defense.
  • No gun: No pistol was found. Whiteside's companions saw no gun. The victim's family removed Love's possessions from the apartment shortly after the police search.
  • Consistent early account: Until shortly before trial, Whiteside told appointed counsel Gary Robinson that he had not actually seen a gun but was convinced Love had one. Robinson advised him that self-defense required only a reasonable belief that Love had a gun, not an actual gun.
  • The change in story: About a week before trial, Whiteside said for the first time that he saw something "metallic" in Love's hand, explaining: "[I]n Howard Cook's case there was a gun. If I don't say I saw a gun, I'm dead."
  • Counsel's response: Robinson told Whiteside the testimony would be perjury. He said counsel could not allow it, that he would have a duty to advise the court, and that he "probably would be allowed to attempt to impeach that particular testimony." He also said he would seek to withdraw if Whiteside insisted.
  • Trial: Whiteside testified that he "knew" Love had a gun and believed Love was reaching for it. On cross he admitted he had not seen a gun. Robinson offered evidence that Love had been seen with a sawed-off shotgun, that the police search may have been careless, and that the family had cleared the apartment.
  • Finding below: On the new-trial motion, the state trial court heard Whiteside and Robinson and found the facts to be as Robinson related them.

Procedural Posture

A jury convicted Whiteside of second-degree murder; the trial court denied a new trial and the Iowa Supreme Court affirmed, commending counsel's ethics. The federal district court denied habeas relief, the Eighth Circuit reversed and directed that the writ issue (rehearing en banc denied over four dissents), and the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Is a criminal defendant's Sixth Amendment right to the assistance of counsel violated when counsel refuses to cooperate in presenting the defendant's intended perjured testimony at trial?

Holding

No. Counsel's response to threatened client perjury was within the range of reasonable professional conduct under Strickland, and being kept from committing perjury cannot establish Strickland prejudice as a matter of law, so the Eighth Circuit's grant of habeas relief was reversed (opinion by Chief Justice Burger; the opinion text does not state the vote, and four Justices concurred only in the judgment).

Reasoning

  • Right to testify: The Court traced the right from common-law disqualification to its statutory abolition. It noted the Court had never explicitly held that a due process right to testify exists, though circuits have so held and the Court has suggested it is a corollary of the Fifth Amendment (Harris v. New York). Whatever its scope, the right does not extend to testifying falsely.
  • **Strickland framework (applied):**
  • Performance prong: counsel's errors must be "so serious that counsel was not functioning as 'counsel.'" Courts must "indulge a strong presumption" that conduct falls within the "wide range of reasonable professional assistance." ABA standards and similar norms are "guides," "only guides."
  • Prejudice prong: a "reasonable probability" that, but for counsel's errors, the result would differ; a probability "sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome." A defendant has "no entitlement to the luck of a lawless decisionmaker."
  • Ethics rules as evidence of reasonableness, not as constitutional rules: A breach of an ethical standard does not necessarily violate the Sixth Amendment. Courts should not constitutionalize particular professional codes and intrude on the states' authority to regulate their bars. The Court said it need not decide how to weigh canons, state codes, and the Sixth Amendment here because "virtually all of the sources speak with one voice."
  • Duty of loyalty is bounded by law: Counsel's duty to advocate is limited to "legitimate, lawful conduct compatible with the very nature of a trial as a search for truth." Counsel may not assist a client in presenting false evidence.
  • Sources canvassed (all pointing one way):
  • ABA Canon 32 (1908): no service involving disloyalty to the law.
  • ABA Canon 37 (1928): a client's announced intention to commit a crime is not a protected confidence.
  • Model Code DR 7-102(A)(4), (7) (adopted in Iowa): no knowing use of perjured testimony; no assisting illegal or fraudulent conduct.
  • Model Rule 1.2(d): no counseling or assisting criminal or fraudulent conduct.
  • DR 4-101(C)(3) and Model Rule 3.3: exception to confidentiality for client perjury. The Court read DR 7-102(B)(1) and Rule 3.3(a)(4) to require disclosure.
  • Iowa criminalizes perjury (§ 720.2) and subornation (§ 720.3).
  • Range of acceptable responses to threatened perjury:
  • First duty, "universally agreed": try to dissuade the client (Rule 3.3 comment).
  • Disclosure to the court of perjury actually given is a professionally acceptable response.
  • Withdrawal is expressly permitted when the client threatens perjury (Rule 1.16(a)(1); DR 2-110(B), (C)). The Court flagged that mid-trial withdrawal raises "difficult questions," including mistrial and double jeopardy.
  • Per the ABA amicus, a lawyer may never "advocate or passively tolerate" client perjury.
  • Application to Robinson: Whether viewed as successful dissuasion or as a "threat" to withdraw and disclose, Robinson's conduct "falls well within" accepted standards. Whiteside did testify. He was restrained only from testifying falsely. Robinson helped develop the basis for his fear of a gun, and Robinson revealed no confidences until Whiteside's post-trial attack on his performance.
  • No impermissible choice: The Eighth Circuit said Robinson forced a choice between the right to counsel and the right to testify. There was "no permissible choice to testify falsely." Under Havens, defendants "must testify truthfully or suffer the consequences," and one consequence is the risk that counsel withdraws.
  • Confidentiality: Confidentiality "totally covers the client's admission of guilt" but does not extend to announced plans for future crimes (Clark v. United States). Perjury is indistinguishable in substance from bribing or threatening witnesses or jurors, and counsel's duty is the same for both.
  • Prejudice: Whether Whiteside was persuaded or compelled to desist, confidence in the verdict is not diminished. Even if the jury might have believed the perjury, that is not prejudice.
  • **Cuyler v. Sullivan distinguished:* Cuyler* presumes prejudice when counsel "actively represent[s] conflicting interests." This "conflict" was imposed on the lawyer by the client's plan to commit a crime. Presuming prejudice from such a conflict would make every conviction suspect whenever a defendant sought acquittal by illegal means, generating "volumes of litigation."

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Brennan (concurring in the judgment; also joined Blackmun's concurrence): The Court has no constitutional or statutory authority to set ethics rules for state-court lawyers. The majority's discussion of the correct response to client perjury is "pure discourse without force of law." The problem "has not now been 'decided.'" He concurred only because Whiteside showed no Strickland prejudice.
  • Justice Blackmun (concurring in the judgment; joined by Brennan, Marshall, and Stevens):
  • Vehicle: A federal habeas case is the wrong vehicle to resolve how lawyers should handle client perjury. The only question is whether Whiteside was denied a fair trial.
  • Fact finding: The state finding that the proposed testimony would have been perjury gets a presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), and Whiteside did not rebut it.
  • Decide on prejudice alone: Strickland permits deciding on prejudice without grading performance. The only effect of Robinson's threat was that Whiteside did not lie. Relying on In re Michael, Agurs, Napue, and the Harris/Havens impeachment line, he called the idea that false evidence aids reliability "simply untenable."
  • No actual conflict: No Cuyler presumption applies. Whiteside had no legitimate interest in perjury or in confidentiality of a perjury plan (Crary), unlike Wood v. Georgia and Glasser. Counsel's interest matched Whiteside's: lying risked a perjury prosecution, impeachment, a first-degree verdict, and a harsher sentence (Grayson).
  • Against a blanket rule: Whether a response to suspected perjury violates the Sixth Amendment may turn on factors including:
  • counsel's certainty that the testimony is false;
  • the stage of proceedings when counsel learns of the plan;
  • the means available to dissuade the client.

Except in the rarest cases, lawyers who act as judge or jury endanger zealous advocacy (Wilcox).

  • Federalism: He was troubled by the majority's implicit adoption of ABA standards and would let states keep their "differing approaches."
  • Footnotes:
  • He was puzzled that the majority treated the constitutional right to testify as open (Jones v. Barnes, Brooks v. Tennessee).
  • He distinguished Wilcox, where counsel had no basis for her belief and her threat kept the defendant off the stand entirely.
  • Justice Stevens (concurring in the judgment; joined Blackmun's concurrence): He agreed this lawyer acted properly and that there was no prejudice. He warned that facts look clearer on appeal than to trial lawyers, and that an honest witness may sincerely recall new details. Reviewing a pretrial threat that prevented perjury differs from reviewing how a threat is carried out. What a lawyer must, should, or may do after the client has testified falsely remains undecided.

Notes

  • Narrow ground available: The judgment rests on two independent grounds: no deficient performance and no prejudice. The four concurring Justices joined only the prejudice ground. Brennan expressly called the ethics discussion non-binding.
  • Federalism limit (majority): Ethics codes are guides to reasonableness, not constitutional commands. The Court reserved for "some future case" how to weigh ethics canons, state codes, and the Sixth Amendment when they diverge.
  • Left open:
  • Whether there is an explicit constitutional right to testify (majority; Blackmun disagreed that it is open).
  • How to handle client perjury discovered mid-trial, where withdrawal raises mistrial and double jeopardy problems (majority).
  • What counsel must do after perjury is committed (Stevens).
  • The proper response where counsel's belief in falsity is less certain (Blackmun, Stevens).
  • Circuit split noted (majority footnotes):
  • United States v. Curtis (7th Cir.): refusal to call a defendant believed to intend perjury was not unconstitutional.
  • United States ex rel. Wilcox v. Johnson (3d Cir.): Sixth Amendment violation where counsel had no basis for believing the alibi was perjured.
  • Lowery v. Cardwell (9th Cir.): withdrawal request mid-bench trial right after the defendant testified.
  • "Free narrative" approach: Proposed ABA Standard 4-7.7 let counsel stand mute while the defendant testified in narrative form, without arguing the false testimony in closing. Most courts rejected it (Curtis, McKissick, Dodd v. Florida Bar). The Eighth Circuit below and the Ninth Circuit (Lowery) approved it. The majority read final Model Rule 3.3 to reject "any participation or passive role whatever."
  • Precedents relied on:
  • Strickland v. Washington (governing test).
  • Harris v. New York and United States v. Havens (no right to commit perjury).
  • Clark v. United States (confidentiality excludes future crimes).
  • Committee on Professional Ethics v. Crary (Iowa; disclosure required).
  • Ferguson v. Georgia (history of testimonial disqualification).
  • Precedent distinguished: Cuyler v. Sullivan (actual-conflict presumption of prejudice).
  • Rejected below: The Eighth Circuit rule, which "seemingly would require an attorney to remain silent while his client committed perjury."
  • Record point: The majority found no support for any threat by Robinson to testify against Whiteside while serving as his counsel.
