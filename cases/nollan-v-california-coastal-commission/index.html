Black-Letter Rule

A permit condition requiring a landowner to convey a property interest is valid only if there is an "essential nexus" between the condition and the legitimate state interest that would justify denying the permit outright. If the condition fails to further the end that justifies the prohibition, it is "an out-and-out plan of extortion" and a taking, and the government must pay for the interest. Requiring the public be given a permanent, continuous right to pass across private land is a permanent physical occupation and a taking if imposed outright.

Facts

  • Parties: James and Marilyn Nollan, beachfront lot owners in Ventura County, against the California Coastal Commission.
  • Property: The lot lies between two public beaches: Faria County Park a quarter-mile north and "the Cove" 1,800 feet south. An eight-foot seawall separates the beach portion from the rest of the lot. The mean high tide line marks the oceanside boundary.
  • Proposed development: The Nollans held an option to buy, conditioned on demolishing a dilapidated 504-square-foot bungalow and replacing it. They applied for a coastal development permit to build a three-bedroom house in keeping with the neighborhood.
  • Condition: The Commission granted the permit on the condition that the Nollans record a deed restriction giving the public a lateral easement across the strip between the mean high tide line and the seawall.
  • Commission findings: The new house would block the ocean view, add to a "wall" of residences creating a "psychological barrier" to beach use, and increase private use of the shorefront, cumulatively burdening public access. It had imposed the same condition on 43 of 60 permits in the tract.
  • Mootness-adjacent fact: While the appeal was pending, the Nollans built the new house and bought the lot without notifying the Commission.

Procedural Posture

The Superior Court struck the condition on statutory grounds, finding no adequate factual basis that the new house burdened public access. The California Court of Appeal reversed, holding the Coastal Act required the condition and that it was constitutional under Grupe v. California Coastal Comm'n; the Nollans appealed to the Supreme Court on the constitutional question only.

Issue

Does the Commission effect a taking when it conditions a permit to rebuild a beachfront house on the owners' grant of a public lateral easement that does not further the purposes the Commission offers to justify restricting the development?

Holding

Yes. A permit condition that lacks an essential nexus to the legitimate purpose justifying a development ban is a taking, and the lateral easement here did not advance any of the Commission's stated purposes; reversed (Scalia, J., for the Court).

Reasoning

  • Outright easement is a taking. Had California simply demanded the easement, there would be a taking. The right to exclude is "one of the most essential sticks in the bundle" (Loretto, quoting Kaiser Aetna). A permanent, continuous public right to pass to and fro is a "permanent physical occupation" under Loretto, even if no one stays put on the land.
  • Calling it a use restriction fails. Describing appropriation of a public easement as "a mere restriction on its use" strips the words of their ordinary meaning. Securing such interests, for a price, is a core use of eminent domain.
  • Greater-includes-lesser logic, with a limit. Land-use regulation is not a taking if it "substantially advance[s] legitimate state interests" and does not deny economically viable use (Agins; see Penn Central). If the Commission could deny the permit outright to protect beach views, it may instead impose a condition serving the same end, such as a height limit, a width restriction, a fence ban, or even a public viewing spot on the property.
  • Essential nexus. That logic collapses when the condition "utterly fails to further the end advanced as the justification for the prohibition." The fire-in-a-theater hypothetical: a ban waived for a $100 payment would be unconstitutional because the unrelated condition changes the purpose. Without nexus, the purpose becomes obtaining an easement without paying for it, which is not a legitimate state interest.
  • Application. The Court accepted, for discussion, the Commission's "reasonably related" fit test and held the condition failed even that. The Commission's argument rested on "a play on the word 'access.'" Letting people already on the beach walk across the lot does nothing to restore views from the road, lower a psychological barrier, or relieve congestion.
  • Heightened scrutiny for exactions. The Takings Clause is "more than a pleading requirement." The Court is "particularly careful" about the word "substantially" where conveyance of property is the price of lifting a land-use restriction, because the risk is high that the real purpose is avoiding compensation.
  • Comprehensive program. A continuous public beach strip may be a good idea, but the Nollans cannot alone be compelled to fund it. California may use eminent domain; "if it wants an easement across the Nollans' property, it must pay for it."
  • Boundary disputes. That risk is inherent in any right to exclude, and a buffer zone merely moves the boundary onto private land. The Court called it "a made-up purpose."
  • Takings standard differs from rational basis. The Court rejected Brennan's view that takings review tracks due process and equal protection rational-basis tests (Clover Leaf Creamery, Lee Optical, Day-Brite). Agins requires "substantially advance." Goldblatt's contrary assumption is inconsistent with later cases.
  • Notice and acquisition. The Commission's announced policy did not strip the Nollans of the right to exclude. The right to build on one's own land is not a "governmental benefit," so Monsanto's voluntary-exchange reasoning does not apply. Buying after the policy began changes nothing; prior owners held full rights and conveyed them.
  • Policy. Allowing police-power leveraging would produce strict regulations that the state then waives for other ends, achieving less of the land-use goal than lenient, non-tradeable rules.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Brennan, J. (joined by Marshall, J.), dissenting: The Court demands a precision long discredited in police-power review; the condition need only be one the State "could rationally have decided" would work. Even under the majority's standard, lateral access responds to the burdens the Commission found, including seaward private encroachment along a shifting tide line and resulting boundary conflicts. Under Penn Central factors there is no taking: the intrusion is minimal, the development increased the lot's value, and the Nollans had no reasonable expectation of excluding the public given Cal. Const. Art. X, § 4, the Coastal Act, the Commission's practice, and Monsanto-style notice. He predicted the Commission could easily document a specific connection in future cases.
  • Blackmun, J., dissenting: The Court's opinion does not implicate the public-trust doctrine. The Court's "eye for an eye" nexus requirement is an anomaly in rational-basis review. The easement is adequately related to the public-access interest, the record shows no economic effect, and the Nollans had notice before buying.
  • Stevens, J. (joined by Blackmun, J.), dissenting: Brennan has the better argument. Writes to stress the tension between encouraging flexible land-use regulation and First English's rule imposing liability for temporary takings, which forces officials to pay for guessing wrong under vague standards.

Notes

  • Assumed, not decided: Whether protecting beach views, overcoming the "psychological barrier," and preventing congestion are legitimate state interests.
  • Fit standard left open: The Court accepted the Commission's "reasonably related" test only for purposes of discussion, because the condition failed "even the most untailored standards." How close a fit is required was not decided.
  • State-law question left open: The Court declined to decide whether Cal. Const. Art. X, § 4 created a pre-existing public right of way. The Court of Appeal did not rely on it and the Commission did not argue it there. Brennan notes the Superior Court reserved the question of public prescriptive rights.
  • Not raised: A "singling out" claim under the Takings Clause or Equal Protection Clause (Armstrong). The Court flagged it as a possible theory but said it was not the basis of the Nollans' challenge.
  • Relied on: Loretto (permanent physical occupation); Kaiser Aetna (right to exclude); Agins ("substantially advance" test); Penn Central (use restrictions; permit denial); Euclid (residential zoning as legitimate); Armstrong (fairness purpose of the Takings Clause); a long string of state and federal cases rejecting unrelated exactions, which the Court said every court but California's had followed.
  • Distinguished: PruneYard (owner had opened property to the public; no permanent access required); Kaiser Aetna (shaped by navigational servitude doctrine); neither involved a classic right-of-way easement. Monsanto (registration was a government benefit; building on one's land is not).
  • Questioned: Goldblatt's assumption that takings and due process inquiries are the same is "inconsistent with the formulations of our later cases." The Court did not overrule it.
  • Rejected below: The California Court of Appeal's Grupe approach, which upheld access conditions where a project merely contributed to the need for access, even through an indirect relationship.
