Black-Letter Rule

Under New York's Equitable Distribution Law, a professional license acquired during the marriage is "marital property" under Domestic Relations Law § 236 (B) (1) (c) and is subject to equitable distribution. The license's value is the enhanced earning capacity it gives the holder. The non-licensed spouse is entitled to an equitable portion of that value, usually through a distributive award under § 236 (B) (5) (e), and is not limited to reimbursement of direct financial contributions.

Facts

  • Parties: Husband (plaintiff) and wife (defendant) married on April 3, 1971. Both were teachers at the same private school.
  • Wife's career: She had a bachelor's degree and a temporary teaching certificate. Permanent certification required 18 months of postgraduate classes. The trial court found she gave up that opportunity while he pursued his education.
  • Husband's education: He had three and one-half years of college at marriage. He finished his degree and premedical courses at night, attended medical school full-time in Guadalajara, Mexico from September 1973, and returned to New York in December 1976 to finish medical school and internship.
  • Wife's contributions: She worked throughout the marriage, did household work, managed finances, and put all her earnings toward living and educational expenses. The trial court found she contributed 76% of the parties' income, excluding a $10,000 student loan she obtained. Both families also helped.
  • Timing: He was licensed in October 1980 and filed for divorce two months later. At trial he was a general surgery resident.
  • Only significant asset: The medical license.
  • Valuation evidence: Wife's expert valued the license at $472,000. He compared average earnings of a college graduate and a general surgeon from 1985 to 2012 (age 65), adjusted for taxes, inflation, and interest, and reduced the difference to present value. He valued her contribution to the medical education at $103,390. Husband offered no expert.
  • Trial award: The trial court awarded the wife $188,800 (40% of the license's value), payable in 11 annual installments, secured by life insurance. It awarded $7,000 counsel fees and a $1,000 expert fee, and no maintenance.

Procedural Posture

After the husband withdrew his complaint, the wife obtained an uncontested divorce on her counterclaim. Supreme Court held the license was marital property and made a distributive award. A divided Appellate Division modified, holding the license was not marital property and striking the expert fee, and remitted for a determination of maintenance and a rehabilitative award. The case reached the Court of Appeals on cross appeals by leave of the Appellate Division.

Issue

Is a professional license acquired during marriage "marital property" subject to equitable distribution under Domestic Relations Law § 236 (B)?

Holding

Yes. A medical license acquired during marriage is marital property under § 236 (B) (1) (c) and subject to equitable distribution under § 236 (B) (5), so the Appellate Division's order was modified by reinstating the trial court's judgment, the case was remitted to the Appellate Division for determination of the facts and exercise of its discretion, and, as modified, affirmed (Simons, J., for a unanimous court; Wachtler, C.J., and Jasen, Meyer, Kaye, Alexander, and Titone, JJ., concurring).

Reasoning

  • Statutory structure: The statute recognizes only marital and separate property. Marital property is defined broadly as "all property acquired by either or both spouses during the marriage." The husband did not claim the license was separate property. He argued it was not property at all.
  • New statutory species: "Marital property" is a statutory creation with no common-law counterpart. The Legislature deliberately went beyond traditional property concepts and left it to courts to decide what interests fall within § 236 (B) (1) (c). Whether a license fits common-law property concepts is irrelevant.
  • *Method from Majauskas: As with pension rights in Majauskas v Majauskas*, the Court looked to other provisions of the statute and legislative intent.
  • Text: § 236 (B) (5) (d) (6) directs courts to consider contributions "to the career or career potential of the other party." Factor (9) refers to difficulty valuing an "interest in a business, corporation or profession." § 236 (B) (5) (e) provides for a distributive award where distributing an interest in a "profession" would be contrary to law. "The words mean exactly what they say": an interest in a profession or career potential is marital property.
  • Legislative history and economic partnership: The statute replaced the inequitable title theory with equitable distribution, premised on marriage as an economic partnership to which both spouses contribute as spouse, parent, wage earner, or homemaker. The scheme aims to wind up economic affairs and sever economic ties at divorce. Maintenance replaced lifetime alimony to promote independence.
  • Fit with this case: Few joint efforts better match the partnership theory than supporting a spouse's professional training. The working spouse earns income, sacrifices her own career, carries the household, and forgoes assets the couple could have accumulated. Nearly the whole nine-year marriage went to acquiring this license, and the wife played a major role.
  • License vs. established practice: The Appellate Division limited the statute to going practices (citing Arvantides and Litman). The Court rejected that limit. An established practice just exercises the privileges the license confers, and practice income is the receipt of the enhanced earning capacity licensure allows. Whether a practice exists matters only for valuation.
  • "Not property" argument rejected on two grounds: (1) The statute, not common law, controls. (2) Even outside § 236 (B), a license is a valuable property right that cannot be revoked without due process (Bender; Greenberg). Lack of market value is irrelevant. Inalienability limits the working spouse's interest, and the Legislature addressed that by authorizing distributive awards.
  • Alternative remedies inadequate: Limiting the working spouse to rehabilitative maintenance or reimbursement frustrates the statute. It contradicts the partnership concept and keeps the economic dependence the Legislature sought to end. Maintenance ends on remarriage, so the working spouse may go uncompensated or be penalized for remarrying. The Court quoted Wood v Wood: marital assets are "the capital product of what was essentially a partnership entity."
  • Valuation: Reimbursement-only would be like limiting a spouse's down payment on real estate to the cash contributed, with no share of appreciation. That conflicts with § 236 (B) (5) (d) (6)'s focus on direct and indirect contributions. Value is the present value of enhanced earning capacity. That is no harder than computing wrongful death or lost earning capacity damages, and only differs in degree from valuing a professional practice, which courts already do.
  • Structuring the award: The trial court has discretion to structure the award considering the working spouse's need for immediate payment, the licensed spouse's ability to pay, and tax consequences of extended payment. It must receive evidence of the license's present value and the working spouse's contributions and consider all § 236 (B) (5) (d) factors. If other marital assets suffice, the court may distribute those instead of making an award against the license.
  • Statement of reasons: Under § 236 (B) (5) (g) and Cappiello, the court need not analyze each factor but must state all factors considered and its reasons. The trial court's findings were supported by the evidence, but it did not state the factors or weight. The Appellate Division may make the award itself with stated reasons or remit to Special Term (Majauskas; Kobylack).
  • Marital fault: Fault may arguably fit factor (10) ("any other factor ... just and proper"), but except in egregious cases that shock the conscience it is not a proper consideration (Blickstein). Reasons: fault is inconsistent with the partnership premise, is hard to assign, and invites time-consuming collateral litigation. No conscience-shocking fault was suggested here.
  • Fees: Counsel and expert fees are discretionary with the trial court and Appellate Division (DRL § 237). The affirmed counsel fee award was not an abuse of discretion as a matter of law and was beyond review. The expert fee was struck only because the Appellate Division thought the license was not property. On remand that court may decide whether to award expert fees.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Meyer, J. (concurring): Joins the majority but flags for the Legislature the unfairness of awards based on the license of a professional still in training. § 236 (B) (9) (b) implies a distributive award, once made, cannot be modified. A trainee may be locked into a career the trial judge "prophesied." Here the husband had done an internal medicine residency the prior year and testified he wanted to return to it, yet the award assumed general surgery income, and the expert admitted his figures were speculative. Meyer would allow either spouse to seek revision if the assumed career turns out wrong (for example, a surgical trainee who loses use of a hand), with reinstatement and counsel fees available if the claimed change proves feigned or illusory.
  • Titone, J. (concurring): Joins the majority and expressly abandons his contrary view in his concurrence in Conner v Conner.

Notes

  • Scope: The license is marital property "to the extent that the license is acquired during marriage."
  • Discretion preserved: Courts may satisfy the working spouse's share from other marital assets instead of an award against the license (Majauskas).
  • Expressly left open: Whether authority for retrospective maintenance or rehabilitative awards may ever be implied from the statute (citing Cappiello with "but see"). The Court said only that a working spouse should not normally be limited to that relief.
  • Fault left open in practice: The Court did not apply the egregious-fault exception because no such fault was suggested.
  • Relied on:
  • Majauskas v Majauskas: analytic method for classifying property; Appellate Division's authority to make awards; distribution of other assets; fee discretion.
  • Cappiello v Cappiello: required statement of factors and reasons.
  • Kobylack v Kobylack: remittal to Special Term.
  • Blickstein, Stevens, Pacifico, McMahan: fault only in egregious cases.
  • Wood v Wood: partnership rationale.
  • Bender v Board of Regents; People ex rel. Greenberg v Reid: license as a due-process-protected property right.
  • Arvantides, Litman, Billington, Cohen, Nehorayoff: courts already value professional practices.
  • Patron v Patron: limits of review of discretionary fee awards.
  • Not followed:
  • Conner v Conner and Lesman v Lesman: the Appellate Division relied on them for the rule that a license is not marital property. The Court reached the opposite conclusion without expressly overruling or otherwise addressing them by name on that point.
  • Kutanovski, Conner, and Lesman: cited (with "see, e.g.") for plaintiff's argument that alternative remedies such as rehabilitative maintenance or reimbursement should be used. The Court held that a working spouse normally should not be restricted to that relief, but did not decide whether authority for such awards may ever be implied from the statute.
  • The Appellate Division's reading of Arvantides and Litman as limited to going practices.
  • Distinguished: Other states' decisions, because they rest on their own statutes and legislative histories.
  • Certified question: Answered in the negative.
