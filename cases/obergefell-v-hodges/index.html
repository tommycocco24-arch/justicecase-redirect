Black-Letter Rule

The right to marry is a fundamental right inherent in the liberty of the person, and under the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment, a State may not exclude same-sex couples from civil marriage on the same terms and conditions as opposite-sex couples. A State also may not refuse to recognize a lawful same-sex marriage performed in another State on the ground of its same-sex character.

Facts

  • Parties: Petitioners were 14 same-sex couples and two men whose same-sex partners had died. Respondents were state officials in Michigan, Kentucky, Ohio, and Tennessee responsible for enforcing those States' laws defining marriage as a union between one man and one woman.
  • Claims: Petitioners argued that the Fourteenth Amendment was violated when the States denied them the right to marry, or refused to give full recognition to marriages lawfully performed in another State.
  • Obergefell (Ohio): James Obergefell and John Arthur married in Maryland inside a medical transport plane because Arthur was dying of ALS. Arthur died three months later. Ohio law barred listing Obergefell as the surviving spouse on Arthur's death certificate.
  • DeBoer and Rowse (Michigan): The couple were raising three adopted children. Michigan allowed adoption only by opposite-sex married couples or single individuals, so each child had only one legal parent.
  • DeKoe and Kostura (Tennessee): The couple married in New York before DeKoe deployed to Afghanistan with the Army Reserve. Tennessee did not recognize the marriage once they settled there.

Procedural Posture

Each District Court ruled for petitioners. The Sixth Circuit consolidated the cases and reversed in DeBoer v. Snyder, holding that a State has no constitutional obligation to license or recognize same-sex marriages. The Supreme Court granted certiorari limited to two questions: licensing and out-of-state recognition.

Issue

Does the Fourteenth Amendment require a State to license a marriage between two people of the same sex and to recognize a same-sex marriage lawfully licensed and performed in another State?

Holding

Yes. In an opinion by Justice Kennedy (5-4, per the dissents' references to a five-Justice majority), the Court held that same-sex couples may exercise the fundamental right to marry under the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses, and that there is no lawful basis for a State to refuse recognition of a lawful out-of-state same-sex marriage on the ground of its same-sex character. The Sixth Circuit was reversed.

Reasoning

  • Due process method. Fundamental liberties include most Bill of Rights guarantees and certain personal choices central to individual dignity and autonomy (Griswold, Eisenstadt). Identifying them "has not been reduced to any formula" (Poe v. Ullman, Harlan, J., dissenting). Courts must exercise "reasoned judgment." History and tradition "guide and discipline this inquiry but do not set its outer boundaries" (Lawrence).
  • Right to marry is settled. Loving, Zablocki, and Turner hold marriage is a fundamental right. The Court conceded those cases presumed opposite-sex partners. It looked instead to the "basic reasons" the right is protected and asked whether they apply to same-sex couples.
  • Four principles showing the reasons apply with equal force:
  • Individual autonomy. Personal choice regarding marriage is inherent in individual autonomy and among the most intimate decisions a person makes (Loving, Zablocki, Lawrence).
  • Two-person union. Marriage supports a committed two-person union unlike any other in importance to the individuals (Griswold, Turner, Windsor). Lawrence protected intimate association from criminal liability. Freedom does not stop there: "Outlaw to outcast may be a step forward, but it does not achieve the full promise of liberty."
  • Children and families. Marriage safeguards children and draws meaning from rights of childrearing, procreation, and education (Meyer, Pierce, Zablocki). Exclusion harms and humiliates children of same-sex couples. Procreation has never been a prerequisite for a valid marriage.
  • Keystone of social order. States make marriage the basis for a long list of rights, benefits, and responsibilities (Maynard v. Hill). Exclusion denies same-sex couples that constellation of benefits and teaches that they are unequal.
  • Framing the right. The Court rejected respondents' reliance on Glucksberg's "careful description" requirement. That approach may have suited physician-assisted suicide. It is inconsistent with how the Court has treated marriage and intimacy. Loving, Turner, and Zablocki asked about the right to marry in its comprehensive sense and whether there was sufficient justification to exclude the relevant class. If rights were defined by who exercised them in the past, received practices could justify themselves.
  • Equal protection. The Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses are "connected in a profound way" though independent. Each may inform the other's meaning and reach (Loving, Zablocki, M.L.B., Eisenstadt, Skinner, Lawrence, and the sex-based marriage cases such as Frontiero and Kirchberg). The challenged laws are "in essence unequal." They deny same-sex couples all marriage benefits and bar them from a fundamental right. Against a long history of disapproval, that works a grave harm that disrespects and subordinates gays and lesbians. The Court did not name a tier of scrutiny.
  • Democratic process objection. There had already been extensive deliberation in referenda, legislatures, courts, and scholarship. Democracy is the appropriate path for change "so long as that process does not abridge fundamental rights" (Schuette). "Fundamental rights may not be submitted to a vote" (Barnette). Bowers shows the cost of a cautious approach: people were harmed before Lawrence corrected it.
  • Harm to marriage argument. Respondents' claim that same-sex marriage would reduce opposite-sex marriage rests on a counterintuitive view of how couples decide to marry. Respondents showed no foundation for it. These cases involve only two consenting adults whose marriages pose no risk of harm to themselves or third parties.
  • Religion. The First Amendment protects religious organizations and persons who teach and advocate that same-sex marriage should not be condoned. The State still may not bar same-sex couples from marriage.
  • Recognition. Respondents' counsel conceded that if States must license same-sex marriages, the justifications for refusing recognition are undermined. Because same-sex couples may marry in all States, recognition bans fall too. Marriages valid in one State and void in another produce instability (Williams v. North Carolina).

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Roberts, C.J. (joined by Scalia and Thomas, JJ.), dissenting. The right-to-marry cases address barriers to marriage as traditionally defined. None supports a right to make a State change the definition. The privacy cases (Griswold, Lawrence) protect against intrusion. They do not create positive entitlements to recognition and benefits. The majority effectively overrules Glucksberg and revives the Lochner approach of enacting judicial policy preferences. Its reasoning offers no stopping point for plural marriage. On equal protection, the majority skips the usual means-ends framework. The laws are rationally related to the legitimate interest in preserving traditional marriage. He suggests the analysis might differ for a focused challenge to specific tangible benefits. The decision ends democratic debate and raises religious liberty questions the majority does not address.
  • Scalia, J. (joined by Thomas, J.), dissenting. Joins the Chief Justice in full. When the Fourteenth Amendment was ratified in 1868, every State limited marriage to one man and one woman, and no one doubted its constitutionality. That resolves the case. The decision is a "threat to American democracy" because an unrepresentative committee of nine lawyers is resolving a policy question. He also criticizes the opinion's reasoning and style as incoherent.
  • Thomas, J. (joined by Scalia, J.), dissenting. Substantive due process is a "dangerous fiction" that distorts the text. Historically, "liberty" in the Due Process Clauses most likely means freedom from physical restraint. At most it means freedom from government action. It has never meant entitlement to government recognition or benefits. Petitioners remained free to live together, hold ceremonies, and raise children. Human dignity is innate, so the government can neither bestow nor take it away. The decision threatens religious liberty and bypasses the political process.
  • Alito, J. (joined by Scalia and Thomas, JJ.), dissenting. Under Glucksberg, due process protects only rights deeply rooted in the Nation's history and tradition, and same-sex marriage is not one. The majority's equal treatment argument depends on viewing marriage as serving the happiness of the spouses. The States reasonably rely on the traditional procreation-centered understanding. The decision will be used to vilify dissenters, forecloses federalist variation, and shows the Court's claimed power has no real limit.

Notes

  • Scope of holding. State laws are invalid "to the extent they exclude same-sex couples from civil marriage on the same terms and conditions as opposite-sex couples." Recognition holding is limited to refusals "on the ground of its same-sex character."
  • Dual constitutional basis. The Court rests on both Due Process and Equal Protection. It does not identify a standard of scrutiny or a suspect classification. The Chief Justice's dissent faults this omission.
  • Methodology point for the outline. The majority treats Glucksberg's narrow, history-specific framing as appropriate for assisted suicide and inconsistent with the marriage and intimacy cases. It does not say it overrules Glucksberg. The Chief Justice argues it effectively does.
  • Overruled: Baker v. Nelson (1972 summary dismissal holding that excluding same-sex couples from marriage presented no substantial federal question) "must be and now is overruled."
  • Relied on: Loving, Zablocki, Turner (right to marry); Griswold, Eisenstadt, Lawrence (intimate choices and association); Meyer, Pierce (family and childrearing); Windsor (dignity of same-sex couples, children's interests, federal benefits); Maynard v. Hill (marriage as foundation of society); Poe v. Ullman (Harlan, J., dissenting) (reasoned judgment); M.L.B., Skinner, Bearden, and sex-based marriage cases (Frontiero, Wiesenfeld, Goldfarb, Orr, Westcott, Wengler, Kirchberg) (interplay of liberty and equality); Schuette and Barnette (limits of democratic process); Williams v. North Carolina (recognition).
  • Discussed as cautionary: Bowers v. Hardwick, which Lawrence overruled as "not correct when it was decided."
  • Religious liberty. The majority affirms that religious persons and organizations may advocate and teach their views under the First Amendment. It does not address the conflicts the dissents raise, such as religious housing, adoption agencies, or tax exemptions.
  • Plural marriage. Raised only by the Chief Justice's dissent. The majority repeatedly frames the right in terms of two persons and does not address plural unions.
