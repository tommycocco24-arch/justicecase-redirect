Black-Letter Rule

A State, or a Bar acting with state authorization, may constitutionally discipline a lawyer for soliciting clients in person, for pecuniary gain, under circumstances likely to pose dangers the State has a right to prevent. Because the anti-solicitation rules are prophylactic, the State need not prove actual harm to the person solicited. In-person commercial solicitation gets some First Amendment protection, but a lower level of scrutiny than truthful advertising under Bates.

Facts

  • Parties: Appellant Ohralik, a member of the Ohio Bar, was disciplined. Appellee is the Ohio State Bar Association.
  • First solicitation: Ohralik heard that Carol McClintock, 18 and a casual acquaintance, had been hurt in a car accident. He called her parents, visited them, then went to her hospital room, where she lay in traction. He told her he would represent her and asked her to sign an agreement. Two days later he returned and she signed a one-third contingent-fee contract.
  • Concealed recorder: Ohralik took photos of the accident scene, then hid a tape recorder under his raincoat before meeting the McClintocks again. He learned their policy had an uninsured-motorist clause worth up to $12,500 each for Carol and her passenger, Wanda Lou Holbert.
  • Second solicitation: Ohralik got Wanda Lou's address from the McClintocks, saying he wanted to ask about the accident. He went to her home uninvited on the day she came home from the hospital and secretly recorded most of the talk. He gave her a "little tip" about the uninsured-motorist clause. Wanda Lou was 18, not a high school graduate, and said she did not understand what was going on. He offered a one-third contingent fee and told her to say "O.K." if she agreed, which she did.
  • "Cost-free" pitch: He told Wanda Lou the representation would not "cost [her] anything" because his fee came out of any recovery.
  • Refusal to withdraw: The next day Wanda Lou's mother repudiated the agreement. Ohralik insisted it was binding. Wanda Lou later confirmed in writing that she did not want him, and the insurer would not release her check while he claimed to represent her. He demanded $2,466.66 before disavowing his claim and later sued her for that sum.
  • Carol's discharge: Carol also fired him. She settled through another lawyer but paid Ohralik one-third of her recovery to settle his breach-of-contract suit against her.
  • Rules at issue: DR 2-103(A) bars a lawyer from recommending his own employment to a non-lawyer who has not sought his advice about hiring a lawyer. DR 2-104(A) bars a lawyer who gave unsolicited advice to obtain counsel from accepting employment resulting from that advice, with an exception for a close friend, relative, former client, or one the lawyer reasonably believes to be a client.

Procedural Posture

Both women filed grievances. The Board of Commissioners on Grievances and Discipline found violations of DR 2-103(A) and 2-104(A), rejected Ohralik's First Amendment defense, and recommended a public reprimand. The Supreme Court of Ohio adopted the findings, held the conduct unprotected, and increased the sanction to indefinite suspension. Bates came down after the Ohio proceedings ended, and the U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Do the First and Fourteenth Amendments bar a State from disciplining a lawyer for in-person solicitation of clients for pecuniary gain where there is no proof or finding that the solicitation actually harmed the persons solicited?

Holding

No. A State may discipline a lawyer for in-person solicitation for pecuniary gain under circumstances likely to produce the harms it seeks to prevent, and proof of actual injury is immaterial, so applying DR 2-103(A) and 2-104(A) to Ohralik did not offend the Constitution. Affirmed (opinion by Justice Powell; Justice Brennan took no part).

Reasoning

  • **Bates does not control.* Bates* protected truthful, restrained advertising of routine legal services and expressly reserved in-person solicitation. Both the First Amendment value of in-person solicitation and the State's interest in banning it differ from advertising.
  • Commercial speech gets limited protection. Virginia Pharmacy brought commercial speech within the First Amendment without making it equal to other speech. Full parity would risk diluting protection for noncommercial speech through "leveling." Commercial speech gets protection "commensurate with its subordinate position."
  • Speech as part of conduct. Under Giboney, a State may regulate a course of conduct even if it is carried out through language. Securities, proxy, antitrust, and labor examples show the State keeps power over harmful commercial activity that includes speech. In-person solicitation is "a business transaction in which speech is an essential but subordinate component," which lowers the level of scrutiny.
  • How in-person solicitation differs from advertising:
  • It may exert pressure and demand an immediate response, with no chance for comparison or reflection.
  • It may give a one-sided pitch and encourage fast, uninformed decisions.
  • There is no chance for intervention or counter-education by the Bar, regulators, or people close to the target.
  • It may actually undercut the Bates interest in informed and reliable decisionmaking.
  • The rules leave the information flow intact. DR 2-104(A) does not stop a lawyer from telling someone about their legal rights or recommending that they get counsel. It bars using that information "as bait" to obtain paid employment, so it prohibits accepting employment that results from unsolicited advice.
  • No political or associational expression. Ohralik could not invoke NAACP v. Button or In re Primus, or the union mutual-assistance cases. Procuring paid employment is "only marginally affected with First Amendment concerns" and falls within the State's sphere of economic and professional regulation.
  • Strong state interests. The State has a special responsibility for licensed professions (Williamson v. Lee Optical; Semler). Lawyers are officers of the court (Goldfarb) and trusted agents and assistants to the court (Cohen v. Hurley). The ban's origins in etiquette do not defeat the other interests it now serves (cf. McGowan v. Maryland). Ohralik conceded a "compelling" interest in preventing fraud, undue influence, intimidation, overreaching, and vexatious conduct.
  • Interests listed (ABA amicus): reducing overreaching and undue influence on lay persons, protecting privacy, and avoiding judgment clouded by the lawyer's pecuniary self-interest. A footnote explains that pecuniary motivation can lead to settling a valid claim too fast or pressing a weak one too far.
  • Prophylactic rule; no need to prove harm. Ohralik argued that only actual proven harm could justify discipline. The Court rejected this. The rules aim to prevent harm before it occurs. A lawyer, "trained in the art of persuasion," soliciting an "unsophisticated, injured, or distressed lay person" creates a real risk of overreaching, and an uninvited approach can itself invade privacy. The State may reasonably presume in-person solicitation "more often than not will be injurious."
  • Enforcement problem. In-person solicitation is not public. Often the only witnesses are the lawyer and the distressed lay person. Requiring proof of actual injury would leave solicitation "virtually immune to effective oversight."
  • *Independent review of the record (Edwards v. South Carolina).* On the undisputed facts, Ohralik approached two young accident victims when they were least able to judge; solicited one in traction and the other on her release day; used Carol's agreement to induce Wanda's "O.K."; used a concealed recorder; pitched the fee as cost-free; and refused to withdraw. The facts are "a striking example" of the inherent potential for overreaching.
  • Overbreadth (footnote). Ohralik did not raise it. It would fail anyway: under Bates, overbreadth applies "weakly, if at all" to commercial speech, and under Broadrick any overbreadth must be substantial. The rules operate mainly against pecuniary solicitation, so any effect on protected noncommercial speech is speculative.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • *Justice Marshall (concurring in part and concurring in the judgment; opinion also applies to In re Primus): Agrees Ohralik's conduct posed dangers the State may prevent. He reads the holding narrowly: solicitation may be banned under circumstances presenting substantial dangers of harm independent of the solicitation itself. He stresses the covert recording, the gross disregard for privacy, and the conflict created by pursuing Wanda Lou while representing Carol. He disagrees that in-person solicitation deserves less First Amendment protection than Bates* advertising, because its informational value is substantial; Ohralik's own advice about the uninsured-motorist coverage was apparently correct. He argues anti-solicitation rules discriminate against less-privileged clients and against solo and small-firm lawyers, so he gives less weight to the ban's longevity. He would limit the State's legitimate interests to substantive evils like fraud, overreaching, and invasion of privacy. He views "benign" commercial solicitation (truthful, noncoercive, dignified, made to a capable person about a non-frivolous claim) as an open question and doubts a sweeping ban could be justified there.
  • Justice Rehnquist (concurring in the judgment): Concurs in affirmance for the reasons in his dissent in In re Primus. No further reasoning in this text.

Notes

  • Narrow scope. The Court says it answers only "part of" the question reserved in Bates. The holding covers in-person solicitation, for pecuniary gain, in circumstances likely to cause the harms the State may prevent.
  • As-applied only. Ohralik challenged the rules as applied, and the Court decided only that.
  • Left open: Solicitation by agents or "runners" was not before the Court, though it "would present similar problems." Marshall flags truthful, noncoercive, "benign" commercial solicitation as unresolved.
  • Exception in the rule. DR 2-104(A)(1) allows employment after unsolicited advice to a close friend, relative, or former client. The Court treats this as covering situations unlikely to present the same dangers.
  • Claims adjusters (footnote). The Court notes that adjusters soliciting releases pose similar dangers but mostly fall outside Bar regulation, and courts view such releases critically.
  • Relied on: Bates (reserved the question; overbreadth weak for commercial speech); Virginia Pharmacy (commercial speech protected, but distinguishable); Giboney (conduct carried out through language); Goldfarb, Cohen v. Hurley, Williamson v. Lee Optical, Semler (state interest in regulating professions); McGowan v. Maryland (original motive does not defeat current interests); Edwards v. South Carolina (independent record review); Broadrick (substantial overbreadth).
  • Distinguished: Bates (advertising is public, leaves the reader free to act or not); NAACP v. Button and In re Primus (political expression and association; under Primus, such solicitation generally needs proof of actual wrongdoing); Railroad Trainmen, Mine Workers, United Transportation Union (mutual assistance in asserting legal rights). The Court reads Railroad Trainmen as implicitly approving regulation of "ambulance chasing."
  • No precedent overruled.
  • Companion case. Read alongside In re Primus. Marshall describes the two cases as sitting at opposite poles: commercial ambulance chasing versus nonprofit, associational solicitation.
