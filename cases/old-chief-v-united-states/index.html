Black-Letter Rule

Under FRE 403, a court may weigh an item's probative value against available evidentiary alternatives, discounting its value when a less prejudicial substitute proves the same point. In a prosecution where the only purpose of the evidence is to prove felon status, and the name or nature of the prior offense risks a verdict tainted by improper considerations, a court abuses its discretion by admitting the full record of conviction when the defendant offers to admit the prior-conviction element. Outside the status context, the prosecution's choice of evidence will generally survive a Rule 403 objection from a defendant offering to concede a point.

Facts

  • Arrest and charges: In 1993, Old Chief was arrested after a fracas involving at least one gunshot. He was charged federally with assault with a dangerous weapon, using a firearm in relation to a crime of violence, and being a felon in possession under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).
  • The statute: § 922(g)(1) bars firearm possession by anyone convicted of "a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year." § 921(a)(20) excludes certain business-regulation offenses and state misdemeanors punishable by two years or less.
  • Prior offense: The predicate conviction was for assault causing serious bodily injury.
  • Offer to stipulate: Before trial, Old Chief moved to bar any mention of the prior conviction beyond the fact that he had been convicted of a crime punishable by more than one year. He offered to stipulate to that fact and proposed a jury instruction. He argued Rule 403 required excluding the name and nature of the offense.
  • Government refusal: The prosecutor refused to stipulate, insisting on the right to prove the case his own way. The District Court agreed: "If he doesn't want to stipulate, he doesn't have to."
  • Evidence at trial: Over renewed objection, the Government introduced the order of judgment and commitment. It stated that in 1988 Old Chief knowingly assaulted a named victim, causing serious bodily injury, and was sentenced to five years.
  • Verdict: The jury convicted on all counts.

Procedural Posture

The Ninth Circuit affirmed in an unpublished decision, holding that the Government may prove a prior felony regardless of a stipulation offer and that, under circuit law, a stipulation is not proof and has no place in Rule 403 balancing. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a sharp circuit split.

Issue

Does a district court abuse its discretion under FRE 403 when it rejects a defendant's offer to concede a prior conviction and admits the full record of judgment, where the name or nature of the prior offense risks a verdict tainted by improper considerations and the evidence is offered solely to prove the prior-conviction element?

Holding

Yes. When proof of felon status is the evidence's only purpose and the prior offense is likely to support conviction on an improper ground, the risk of unfair prejudice substantially outweighs the discounted probative value of the record, so admitting it over an available admission is an abuse of discretion. Reversed and remanded to the Ninth Circuit (Souter, J., for the Court; O'Connor, J., dissenting, joined by Rehnquist, C.J., Scalia, and Thomas, JJ.).

Reasoning

  • Relevance (Rules 401/402): The name of the prior offense was relevant. It placed Old Chief within the subclass of offenders barred from possessing firearms, making his § 922(g)(1) status more probable. Per the Advisory Committee Note to Rule 401, a fact need not be disputed to be relevant. Any exclusion of conceded-point evidence must rest on Rule 403 grounds such as unfair prejudice or waste of time, never on irrelevance.
  • Unfair prejudice defined: "Unfair prejudice" means an undue tendency to suggest decision on an improper basis, commonly an emotional one (Advisory Committee Note to Rule 403). Propensity reasoning, generalizing a past bad act into bad character, is a recognized improper basis. The Court relied on Michelson v. United States, on then-Judge Breyer in United States v. Moccia, and on Rule 404(b).
  • Rule 403 balancing considers alternatives: The Court rejected treating each item "as an island." That approach would let the proponent choose the most prejudicial option, falling back on substitutes only if an objection were sustained. The Court instead read Rule 403 "probative value" as marginal value, informed by available substitutes. The Advisory Committee Notes to Rules 401, 403, and 404(b) all point to the availability of other proof as a factor.
  • The test as framed:
  • On objection, decide whether the item raises a danger of unfair prejudice.
  • If so, evaluate probative value and unfair prejudice for the item and for any actually available substitutes.
  • If an alternative has substantially the same or greater probative value with lower prejudice, discount the original item's probative value.
  • Exclude the item if its discounted probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice.
  • Throughout, account for the offering party's need for evidentiary richness and narrative integrity. Two items going to the same point does not mean only one can come in.
  • Application to § 922(g)(1): Naming the prior offense generally carries a risk of unfair prejudice. The risk is especially obvious where the prior crime is a gun crime or resembles other pending charges, as here with the joined assault count. Old Chief's offer was an admission, which is good evidence under Rule 801(d)(2)(A). That admission was seemingly conclusive, since the statute cares only that the prior offense falls within the broad class of qualifying felonies.
  • The prosecution's right to choose its evidence: The Court endorsed the general rule from Parr v. United States that a defendant cannot stipulate away the full evidentiary force of the Government's case. Evidence tells a story, sustains jurors' willingness to draw inferences, conveys moral significance, and meets jurors' expectations. A stipulation can leave a gap that makes jurors wonder what is being hidden.
  • Why that rule does not apply to felon status: Status rests on a judgment wholly independent of the charged conduct. The choice is between two abstract propositions, a record of conviction or an admission of the same fact. Congress made distinctions among qualifying felonies irrelevant (quoting Tavares: from "short lobsters" to "the most aggravated murder"). Proving status without its basis leaves no gap in the narrative of the current crime. If the prior offense is admissible for another purpose, Rule 404(b) preserves the Government's opportunity to offer it.
  • Conclusion of the balance: The admission and the legitimately probative part of the record carried equivalent evidentiary value. They differed only in the prejudicial risk present in the record and absent from the admission.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice O'Connor, dissenting, joined by the Chief Justice, Justice Scalia, and Justice Thomas:
  • No unfair prejudice: Evidence that directly proves an element is not unfairly prejudicial merely because it hurts. Rule 404(b) bars propensity use, but here the conviction was offered as direct proof of an element, so neither 404(b) nor 403 bars it.
  • Statutory structure: § 922(g)(1) requires proof of a particular qualifying crime, given the § 921(a)(20) exclusions. Crimes have names; a defendant is never convicted of an unspecified "crime." The Government may prove the specific gun possessed, so it should be able to prove the specific prior crime.
  • Limiting instructions: Rule 105 instructions can cure incremental harm, and jurors are presumed to follow them (Shannon).
  • Vague standard: The majority never says what in the order risked an improper verdict: the assault, the details, or the joinder of related counts.
  • Narrative logic cuts both ways: Jurors may be puzzled by an unnamed crime, or doubt why gun possession was illegal (Staples, Barker).
  • Burden of proof: The Government must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt (Winship, Gaudin); a not-guilty plea puts every element at issue (Mathews); a defendant's tactical concession does not lift that burden (Estelle v. McGuire). The Government therefore needs latitude to choose its proof.
  • Partial jury waiver: Forcing a concession on the Government resembles a partial guilty plea or jury waiver without Government consent, contrary to FRCP 23(a) and Singer v. United States.

Notes

  • Limited to felon status: The Court said its discussion was general because Rule 403 is general, but its holding is limited to cases involving proof of felon status.
  • Abuse-of-discretion review: Evidentiary rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion (United States v. Abel). A defendant does not meet that standard merely by showing an alternative means of proof the prosecution chose not to use.
  • Judge from the time of ruling: Reviewing courts must evaluate the decision as the trial court faced it, without hindsight. Cf. United States v. O'Shea, approving refusal to impose a stipulation where the prior convictions would likely come in on other grounds.
  • Rule 404(b) purposes preserved: If the prior offense is admissible for motive, intent, identity, or another non-propensity purpose, the Government may still seek its admission.
  • Generally no forced stipulation for narrative evidence: The prosecution's choice will generally survive Rule 403 when a defendant seeks to substitute an admission for evidence telling a coherent story of the charged offense.
  • Other alternatives left open: Other means of proof, such as a redacted record of conviction, may also require excluding the offense's name. Any alternative needs a jury instruction. The Government may propose a redacted judgment for the court to weigh against the defendant's admission.
  • Low-prejudice priors: A prior offense may be so remote in time or nature that its prejudicial potential is minimal. An old, minor felony might even prejudice the Government through nullification risk, which the Government must bear.
  • Separable portions of a document: A court may find some sections of a document relevant and others irrelevant and inadmissible.
  • Jury instructions: Old Chief's proposed instruction was ambiguous and would have needed revision. The instruction actually given was erroneous. It told jurors not to treat the prior conviction as evidence of guilt, although status was an element. It also invoked impeachment under Rule 609 though Old Chief never testified. The Court placed fault at least as much on Old Chief.
  • Prosecutorial misconduct argument: Old Chief argued that refusing an adequate stipulation and jury instruction in this narrow context was prosecutorial misconduct, since a prosecutor must pursue just convictions. The Court responded that any ethical obligation depends on the construction of Rule 403, and it had no reason to anticipate related ethical lapses once the Rule's meaning is settled. Separately, in remanding, the Court referred to an issue not passed upon below.
  • Circuit split resolved: The Court rejected the approach of circuits allowing the Government to refuse a stipulation (Burkhart, Smith, Breitkreutz) and the Ninth Circuit's view that a stipulation has no place in Rule 403 balancing. The decision aligned with circuits requiring elimination of the offense's name (Tavares, Poore, Wacker, Jones).
  • Authorities relied on: Advisory Committee Notes to Rules 401, 403, and 404; Michelson; Moccia; Tavares; Abel; Parr (general rule accepted, held inapplicable to status); Saltzburg and McCormick on juror expectations and marginal probative value; Lakeside v. Oregon on adverse juror inferences.
