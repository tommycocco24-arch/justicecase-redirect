Black-Letter Rule

A gender classification survives equal protection scrutiny only if it serves important governmental objectives and is substantially related to achieving them, and this applies equally when the classification burdens men. A statute that lets courts order alimony from husbands but never from wives fails that test, because the State already holds individualized hearings on the parties' finances and so has no need to use sex as a proxy for need or for past discrimination. Even statutes meant to compensate women for past discrimination must be carefully tailored, and the State may not classify by sex when a gender-neutral rule would serve its purposes as well.

Facts

  • Parties: William Orr (appellant) and Lillian Orr (appellee), formerly married.
  • Divorce decree: A 1974 final decree ordered Mr. Orr to pay Mrs. Orr $1,240 per month in alimony. The record indicates the obligation came from a stipulation between the parties that the court incorporated into the decree.
  • Statutes: Ala. Code, Tit. 30, §§ 30-2-51 to 30-2-53 authorize alimony awards to wives out of husbands' estates. The Alabama Supreme Court had held there is no authority in the state to award alimony against a wife in favor of a husband.
  • Contempt proceeding: In 1976 Mrs. Orr sought a contempt judgment for alimony arrears. Mr. Orr's defense was a motion asking the court to declare the statutes unconstitutional because they burden husbands but never wives.
  • No claim for himself: Mr. Orr apparently did not claim he was entitled to alimony. He argued only that he should not have to pay if similarly situated wives could not be ordered to pay.
  • No timeliness or stipulation objection below: Neither Mrs. Orr nor the Alabama courts objected that the constitutional challenge came too late, and Mrs. Orr never raised the stipulation as an alternative ground. Her brief below agreed the sole issue was the constitutionality of the gender classification.

Procedural Posture

The Lee County Circuit Court denied Mr. Orr's motion and entered judgment against him for $5,524 in back alimony and attorney fees. The Alabama Court of Civil Appeals upheld the statutes, the Alabama Supreme Court granted certiorari and then quashed the writ as improvidently granted without opinion, and the U.S. Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Do Alabama statutes that authorize courts to impose alimony obligations on husbands but never on wives violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment?

Holding

Yes. In an opinion by Justice Brennan, the Court held the Alabama alimony statutes unconstitutional because the gender classification is not substantially related to the State's important objectives where individualized hearings already assess each spouse's need, and it reversed and remanded for further proceedings not inconsistent with the opinion.

Reasoning

Part I: Jurisdiction (three threshold questions the Court raised on its own)

  • Standing. Every challenge to an underinclusive statute faces the possibility that the State will extend the benefit to the disfavored class and leave the challenger with nothing. Alabama could cure the defect by allowing awards to husbands or by denying alimony to both spouses. If it chose the second course, Mr. Orr would benefit. The Court cannot know which course the State will take, so denying standing on this ground would mean underinclusive statutes could never be challenged.
  • Burden suffices. Mr. Orr bears a burden he would not bear if he were female. The Court compared a hypothetical law imposing alimony on blacks but not whites, which a black payor could clearly challenge. The Court relied on Stanton v. Stanton and Craig v. Boren (no denial of standing on this ground) and on Linda R.S. v. Richard D. (a parent obligated by statute to pay child support has standing to challenge the statute because a similarly situated parent is not burdened).
  • Additional standing basis (footnote). Mr. Orr alleged that a gender-neutral statute would lower his payments because current law applies gender-related factors, such as maintaining a wife in her accustomed manner and reviewing stipulations for fairness only to the wife. The Court did not resolve these allegations but said they made his standing unassailable.
  • Timeliness. Raising the challenge for the first time at the contempt stage might have been a procedural default under state law. The Alabama courts considered and decided the federal question on the merits, so it is irrelevant when the question was raised (Beecher v. Alabama; Manhattan Life Ins. Co. v. Cohen).
  • Adequate and independent state ground. The stipulation might support a continuing contractual obligation. The Alabama courts did not rest on it, Mrs. Orr never raised it, and the Court of Civil Appeals called the constitutional question the "sole issue." A state court that actually decides the federal question adversely gives this Court jurisdiction, even if the state court could have rested on a state ground (Indiana ex rel. Anderson v. Brand). The Alabama Supreme Court's unexplained quashing of the writ does not change this (Cicenia v. Lagay).
  • Powell's objections rejected (footnote). The Court rejected Justice Powell's proposed Pullman abstention because that doctrine applies only where the state court to be deferred to has not previously examined the case, and here the Alabama courts had already considered it; none of the cases Powell cited approved abstention in that situation. The Court also rejected Powell's suggestion that the parties may have colluded, calling it "disturbing" and noting that no evidence whatever, within or outside the record, supports it, and that Mrs. Orr had no conceivable interest in helping her ex-husband resist her own demand for $5,524 in back alimony.

Part II: Merits

  • Standard. The statutes classify by sex, so they are subject to equal protection scrutiny (Reed v. Reed). Discrimination against men gets the same scrutiny (Craig v. Boren). The test, quoted from Califano v. Webster, has two elements:
  • The classification must serve important governmental objectives.
  • The classification must be substantially related to achieving those objectives.
  • Objective 1: reinforcing the wife's dependent role. Fails element 1. A State preference for families in which the wife is dependent cannot sustain the statutes. Stanton rejected the "old notion" that the man must provide the home, and the female is no longer destined solely for the home.
  • Objective 2: helping needy spouses. Satisfies element 1. The Court conceded that assisting needy spouses is a legitimate and important governmental objective. Whether using sex as a proxy for need is a permissible means of pursuing that objective is a question for element 2, not something the Court approved.
  • Objective 3: compensating women for discrimination during marriage that left them unprepared for the workforce. Satisfies element 1. Reducing the economic disparity caused by the long history of discrimination against women is an important objective (Califano v. Webster).
  • Ordinary approach to element 2. For the need rationale, the Court would usually ask whether sex is an "accurate proxy" for dependency (Craig; Reed). For the compensation rationale, it would ask whether women had in fact been discriminated against in the sphere the statute governs, leaving the sexes not similarly situated (Schlesinger v. Ballard; compare Califano v. Webster and Kahn v. Shevin with Weinberger v. Wiesenfeld). The Court did not need to resolve either question.
  • Element 2 fails anyway because individualized hearings already occur. Even assuming sex is a reliable proxy for need and marriage did discriminate against women, Alabama courts already hold hearings that consider the parties' relative finances. Needy men could be helped along with needy women at little or no extra cost. Hearings can identify which wives were actually disadvantaged and which families defied the stereotype. The gender line is "gratuitous" (Wiesenfeld).
  • No administrative-convenience rationale (footnote). Hearings happen regardless, and Alabama courts review finances even when the parties settle. Even if some effort were saved, a mandatory sex preference adopted to avoid hearings is the arbitrary choice Reed forbids.
  • Perverse results. Compared with a gender-neutral rule placing the obligation on the spouse able to pay, Alabama's rule gives an advantage only to the financially secure wife whose husband is needy. Those wives are the least needy and the least likely to have suffered discrimination in marriage (drawing on Justice Stevens's concurrence in Califano v. Goldfarb). A classification that gives extra benefits only to those the State has no reason to prefer cannot survive.
  • Stereotype risk. Sex-based benefits and burdens risk reinforcing stereotypes about women's "proper place" and need for protection. Compensatory statutes must therefore be carefully tailored. Where a gender-neutral classification serves the State's purposes equally well, the State may not classify by sex. This applies with more force where the choice benefits those who need no special solicitude.
  • Common-law compensation argument rejected (footnote). Mrs. Orr argued the support duty compensated wives for common-law property disabilities. The Court said this at most shows alimony was part of a larger invidious scheme, which would be a reason to invalidate the whole scheme. Alabama had also long ago removed those disabilities by statute (Ala. Const., Art. X, § 209).
  • Motive inference (footnote). A missing substantial relationship may indicate that the asserted objectives were not the statute's real goals.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Blackmun (concurring): Joins the opinion and judgment on two assumptions. The first is that the Court's language about discrimination "in the sphere" of the statute does not make society-wide discrimination always irrelevant. The second is that the language does not cut back on Kahn v. Shevin.
  • Justice Stevens (concurring): Joins the Court's opinion. Whether Mr. Orr has a continuing contractual obligation is an undecided question of Alabama law. The Court correctly refused to decide that issue itself or to direct the Alabama Supreme Court to decide it first, because either course would violate principles of federalism. Even if Mr. Orr's chances on the state-law issue were remote, Stevens does not see how the litigation fails to be an Article III case or controversy.
  • Justice Powell (dissenting): Agrees with Justice Rehnquist that the Court handled the Article III problems too casually. Two unsettled state-law questions could moot the equal protection issue:
  • whether Alabama would bar a challenge first raised in contempt proceedings more than two years after the decree (citing Hughes v. Hughes, decided after this case); and
  • whether the settlement agreement independently binds Mr. Orr.

Under Pullman abstention, the Court should remand to the Alabama Supreme Court to resolve these questions first. He stresses the policy of repose for divorce and alimony decrees. Because neither party raised these obvious issues, he says the Court cannot be sure the parties are fully adverse or are not seeking an advisory opinion. He would remand.

  • Justice Rehnquist, joined by the Chief Justice (dissenting): Mr. Orr lacks Article III standing.
  • As a would-be recipient: He never sought alimony, has the means to pay, and agreed to the amount, so he cannot benefit from a sex-neutral statute (Supervisors v. Stanley).
  • As a burdened payor: His obligation was fixed by contract, which he does not claim is unenforceable. He therefore has not shown that striking the statutes would relieve his burden. The burden of proving causation and redressability is on him (Warth v. Seldin).
  • Precedent: Linda R.S. supports the opposite result, and the Court's race analogy fails if the obligation rests on independent contract.
  • State court rulings: A state court's ruling on the merits cannot create a case or controversy (Doremus v. Board of Education).
  • Disposition: He would dismiss the appeal.

Notes

  • Disposition and remand. Reversed and remanded. On remand the Alabama courts are free to decide state-law questions not yet passed on. That includes whether Mr. Orr's stipulated agreement, or other gender-neutral state law, binds him to keep paying. The Court said it "cannot, and would not, predict" how Alabama would treat the stipulation and noted "somewhat knotty" state-law problems. This was a contempt proceeding, and Alabama judges review stipulations for fairness.
  • Choice of cure left to the State. Alabama may comply by extending alimony to needy husbands or by denying alimony to both spouses. The Court did not choose between them.
  • Timeliness left open (footnote). States, including Alabama in other cases, may hold that contempt proceedings come too late to attack a decree entered without constitutional objection, provided the earlier proceedings gave a fair opportunity to raise the federal right (NAACP v. Alabama). The Court noted Alabama apparently has such a rule (Hughes) and saw no reason to fear that existing alimony obligations would be nullified. State courts may decline tardy challenges but are not required to.
  • Questions the Court did not reach.
  • Whether sex is an accurate proxy for need.
  • Whether women were in fact discriminated against within marriage.
  • Whether a gender-neutral statute would lower Mr. Orr's payments.
  • Relied on (merits):
  • Reed v. Reed: sex classification triggers scrutiny; administrative convenience is no justification.
  • Craig v. Boren: discrimination against men is scrutinized; the "accurate proxy" inquiry.
  • Califano v. Webster: the two-part test; remedying past discrimination is an important objective.
  • Stanton v. Stanton: the breadwinner stereotype cannot justify discrimination.
  • Weinberger v. Wiesenfeld: a gender line is "gratuitous" when neutral treatment serves the same end.
  • Califano v. Goldfarb (Stevens, J., concurring in judgment): the benefited wives are the nondependent ones.
  • Schlesinger v. Ballard: the "not similarly situated" inquiry.
  • Compared, not applied: Kahn v. Shevin and Califano v. Webster were set against Wiesenfeld on the compensation inquiry. Blackmun's concurrence conditions his vote on Kahn remaining intact.
  • Relied on (jurisdiction):
  • Stanton, Craig, and Linda R.S. on standing.
  • Beecher v. Alabama and Manhattan Life Ins. Co. v. Cohen on timeliness.
  • Indiana ex rel. Anderson v. Brand (called "dispositive") and Cicenia v. Lagay on adequate and independent state grounds.
  • Distinguished:
  • Doremus stands only for the rule that a state court cannot confer standing in this Court. The Court called it irrelevant because the Alabama courts never held that the stipulation bound Mr. Orr.
  • Pullman abstention applies only where the state court has not previously examined the case.
  • Overruled: None.
