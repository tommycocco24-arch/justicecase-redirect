Black-Letter Rule

Extrinsic evidence is admissible to explain the meaning of a written instrument whenever it is relevant to prove a meaning to which the language of the instrument is reasonably susceptible. Whether the writing looks plain and unambiguous to the judge on its face does not decide admissibility. The court must preliminarily consider all credible evidence offered to prove the parties' intention. If the language, in light of all the circumstances, is fairly susceptible of either interpretation contended for, extrinsic evidence relevant to prove either meaning is admissible.

Facts

  • Parties: Plaintiff Pacific Gas & Electric (PG&E) hired defendant G.W. Thomas Drayage & Rigging in 1960 to furnish labor and equipment to remove and replace the upper metal cover of PG&E's steam turbine.
  • Indemnity clause: Defendant agreed to work "at [its] own risk and expense" and to "indemnify" PG&E "against all loss, damage, expense and liability resulting from . . . injury to property, arising out of or in any way connected with the performance of this contract."
  • Insurance clause: Defendant agreed to procure at least $50,000 of property-damage liability insurance. PG&E was to be an additional named insured, with a cross-liability clause extending coverage to PG&E's property.
  • Accident: During the work the cover fell and damaged the turbine's exposed rotor. PG&E spent $25,144.51 on repairs and sued for that amount.
  • Theory at trial: PG&E dismissed its negligence count and won on the theory that the indemnity clause covered injury to all property, including PG&E's own.
  • Excluded evidence: Defendant offered admissions by PG&E's agents, its own conduct under similar contracts with PG&E, and other proof that the parties meant the clause to cover only third-party property.
  • Trial court's view: The trial judge called the language "the classic language for a third party indemnity provision" but held that its "plain language" also covered PG&E's property. It then refused all extrinsic evidence that would contradict that reading.

Procedural Posture

The trial court entered judgment for PG&E after excluding defendant's extrinsic evidence on the meaning of the indemnity clause. Defendant appealed from that judgment to the California Supreme Court.

Issue

Did the trial court err by refusing to consider extrinsic evidence of the parties' intended meaning of the indemnity clause on the ground that the clause's language was plain and unambiguous on its face?

Holding

Yes. Extrinsic evidence is admissible whenever it is relevant to prove a meaning to which the contract language is reasonably susceptible, and the indemnity clause was reasonably susceptible of defendant's third-party-only meaning, so the judgment was reversed (Traynor, C.J., for the court, joined by Peters, Mosk, Burke, Sullivan, and Peek, JJ.; McComb, J., dissented).

Reasoning

  • The plain-meaning approach substitutes the judge's linguistic background. A court that interprets a contract by its "plain meaning" decides meaning by the "extrinsic evidence of the judge's own linguistic education and experience" (quoting Corbin). Excluding contrary testimony assumes perfect verbal expression is possible. The court calls that belief "a remnant of a primitive faith in the inherent potency and inherent meaning of words."
  • A four-corners rule fails either way. Confining interpretation to the document because it seems clear to the court would either deny that the parties' intention matters or assume a verbal precision and stability that language lacks.
  • Intention is the source of contractual duties in California. The court rejects the view that obligations arise from the use of "magic words" regardless of intent, citing Hotchkiss v. National City Bank of New York as an example of that view. Under Civ. Code § 1636, a court must give effect to the parties' mutual intention at the time of contracting. Excluding relevant extrinsic evidence could be justified only if meaning could feasibly be determined from the instrument alone.
  • Words lack fixed referents. Quoting Pearson v. State Social Welfare Board and Corbin, the court reasons that meaning varies with verbal context, surrounding circumstances, purposes, and the linguistic experience of users and readers, "not excluding judges." Meaning can only be found by interpretation in light of all the circumstances that reveal the sense in which the writer used the words (quoting the concurrence in Universal Sales Corp. v. California Press Mfg. Co.).
  • Relation to the parol evidence rule. Extrinsic evidence still may not add to, detract from, or vary the terms of a written contract. The terms must be determined first, though, before a court can decide whether evidence is offered for a prohibited purpose. The risk that the parties meant something other than what the judge reads extends beyond trade-usage cases. It exists whenever the parties' understanding may have differed from the judge's.
  • The procedure (two steps).
  • The court gives preliminary consideration to all credible evidence offered to prove intention. This includes the circumstances surrounding the making of the agreement, such as the object, nature, and subject matter of the writing, so the court can place itself in the parties' position at contracting (Civ. Code § 1647; Code Civ. Proc. § 1860).
  • If, after considering that evidence, the language is "fairly susceptible of either one of the two interpretations contended for" (Balfour v. Fresno C. & I. Co.), extrinsic evidence relevant to prove either meaning is admissible.
  • Conditional admission. When an objection comes before the court can assess all the offered evidence, the court may admit the item conditionally. It may reserve its ruling or admit the evidence subject to a motion to strike (Evid. Code § 403).
  • "Ambiguity" language is harmless if properly understood. Prior cases admitting extrinsic evidence because a contract was "ambiguous" are consistent with the rule, so long as one keeps in mind that extrinsic evidence itself may expose the ambiguity by revealing more than one possible meaning.
  • Application. Defendant's evidence was not needed to show that the clause was reasonably susceptible of the third-party-only meaning. It was still relevant and admissible on that question. Because the clause was reasonably susceptible of that meaning, the evidence was also admissible to prove that the clause actually had that meaning.
  • Alternative ground: the clause was ambiguous on its face anyway. The exclusion would be error even under a plain-meaning rule.
  • "Indemnify" carries a double sense in statutes and dictionaries. Civ. Code § 2772 (indemnity against legal consequences of conduct) differs from Civ. Code § 2527 (insurance as indemnity against loss). Black's, Stroud's, and Webster's definitions also vary. The trial court itself recognized the language as typical third-party indemnity wording.
  • "All" is inconclusive. If "indemnify" covers only third-party claims, "all" just means all such claims.
  • "Loss, damage, and expense" is inconclusive. These words commonly appear in third-party clauses so an indemnitee who settles can recover without proving its own liability (Carpenter Paper Co. v. Kellogg; Civ. Code § 2778).
  • "At its own risk and expense" may release PG&E from liability for injury to defendant's property. It does not necessarily make defendant an insurer of PG&E's property.
  • The insurance clause does not show whether coverage was for all injuries or only those caused by defendant's negligence.
  • Guidance for retrial: active negligence instruction. The trial court told the jury PG&E could recover unless all four listed conditions existed. Goldman v. Ecco-Phoenix, Harvey Machine Co. v. Hatzel & Buehler, and Safeway Stores v. Massachusetts Bonding do not require all four. It is enough that the indemnitee's own active negligence is a cause of the harm. The court relies on Markley v. Beagle: a general indemnity clause will not be read to cover the indemnitee's own actively negligent acts.
  • Guidance for retrial: invoices and hearsay.
  • Repair invoices, bills, and receipts are hearsay. They are inadmissible on their own to prove that liability was incurred, that payment was made, or that charges were reasonable.
  • When a party testifies that it incurred or paid a repair liability, the documents may come in to corroborate that testimony. If the charges were paid, the testimony and documents are evidence of reasonableness. Because there was testimony of payment here, admitting the invoices was proper.
  • Reading the line items to prove which repairs were actually made was error. No qualified witness testified that the invoices accurately recorded the work.
  • The expert's opinion on reasonableness rested on that inadmissible hearsay, so defendant's objections should have been sustained. An expert must rely on facts personally observed or on hypotheses supported by the evidence (George v. Bekins Van & Storage Co.).

Concurrences & Dissents

Notes

  • Scope of the rule. The test is reasonable susceptibility. Extrinsic evidence offered to prove a meaning the language is not reasonably susceptible of remains inadmissible (see the conditional-admission footnote). The parol evidence bar on adding to, detracting from, or varying terms stays in place. The court reframes it as a question that comes after interpretation.
  • Two independent grounds. The court rejects the plain-meaning exclusion as a rule. It also holds that this clause was ambiguous on its face. Outlines should record both.
  • Remand-related rulings. The active-negligence and hearsay rulings address questions "that may arise on retrial." They are part of the opinion but are separate from the interpretation holding.
  • Offer of proof. Defendant's offer of proof might ordinarily be too general to preserve error (Evid. Code § 354(a)). No formal offer was needed here because the trial court repeatedly ruled it would exclude all extrinsic evidence (§ 354(b)).
  • Business records. In a footnote, the court says a third-party invoice might come in under Evid. Code § 1271 if a qualified witness testifies to its identity and mode of preparation (California Steel Buildings, Inc. v. Transport Indemnity Co.).
  • Relied on (interpretation): Continental Baking Co. v. Katz, Parsons v. Bristol Development Co., Hulse v. Juillard Fancy Foods Co., Nofziger v. Holman, Coast Bank v. Minderhout, Imbach v. Schultz, and Reid v. Overland Machined Products for the reasonable-susceptibility test. Also relied on: Universal Sales Corp. (including its concurrence) and Lemm v. Stillwater Land & Cattle Co. for surrounding circumstances; Pearson for the instability of word meaning; Balfour for "fairly susceptible"; Civ. Code §§ 1636, 1647; Code Civ. Proc. §§ 1859, 1860, 1861; Corbin and Wigmore.
  • Trade-usage examples cited: Ermolieff v. R.K.O. ("United Kingdom" included Ireland), Higgins v. California Petroleum ("ton" meant long ton), Callahan v. Stanley ("stubble"), Jenny Lind Co. v. Bower ("north" meant magnetic meridian), and Body-Steffner Co. v. Flotill Products (purchase form was an agency contract). The court says the same possibility of a different meaning extends beyond trade usage.
  • Rejected view: Hotchkiss v. National City Bank (and Williston), which treats a contract as an obligation attached by law to the parties' words regardless of personal intent. The court rejects that view for California.
  • Distinguished/clarified: Goldman, Harvey Machine, and Safeway Stores do not support the four-condition jury instruction.
  • Overruled: The opinion does not expressly overrule any case.
