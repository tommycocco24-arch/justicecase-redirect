Black-Letter Rule

When a coal mining lease is fully performed except for the lessee's promised remedial work, the lessor's damages are ordinarily the reasonable cost of performing that work. If the breached provision was merely incidental to the contract's main purpose, and the economic benefit of full performance to the lessor is grossly disproportionate to the cost of performance, recovery is limited to the diminution in value of the premises caused by the nonperformance.

Facts

  • Parties: Willie and Lucille Peevyhouse owned a farm with coal deposits. Garland Coal & Mining Co. leased it.
  • Contract: In November 1954 the plaintiffs leased the premises to defendant for five years for strip mining. Beyond the usual coal-lease covenants, defendant specifically agreed to do restorative and remedial work at the end of the lease.
  • Breach: The parties stipulated that every covenant was performed except the remedial work. Defendant conceded it never did that work.
  • Cost of performance: The work required moving many thousands of cubic yards of dirt. Experts estimated the cost at about $29,000. Plaintiffs sued for $25,000.
  • Diminution in value: Defendant's experts testified, over plaintiffs' objection, that the work would raise the farm's value by only about $300. Plaintiffs offered no value evidence and did not cross-examine those witnesses.
  • Verdict: The jury, told it could consider cost of performance "together with all of the evidence offered on behalf of either party," awarded $5,000. That exceeded the total value of the farm even after the remedial work.

Procedural Posture

The trial court directed a verdict for plaintiffs on liability and left damages to the jury, which returned $5,000. Plaintiffs appealed and defendant cross-appealed, both challenging the damages instruction.

Issue

Where the cost of performing a lessee's incidental remedial obligation vastly exceeds the resulting increase in the land's value, may the lessor recover the full cost of performance?

Holding

No. Where the breached provision was merely incidental to the main purpose and the lessor's economic benefit from full performance is grossly disproportionate to its cost, damages are limited to diminution in value, so the $5,000 judgment was modified and reduced to $300 and, as modified, affirmed (Jackson, J., for a 5-4 court).

Reasoning

  • Question of first impression. The court found no Oklahoma case on point. It distinguished Ardizonne v. Archer, which applied a cost rule to a contract to drill an oil well, because drilling yields geological information and a producing well raises the land's value.
  • Weight of outside authority. Groves v. John Wunder Co. applied the cost rule on similar facts. The court called it the only case it found following that rule where cost greatly exceeded diminution, and noted it was decided by a plurality. Three appellate courts (Sandy Valley, Bigham, Sweeney) applied the value rule in similar circumstances. Plaintiffs argued those cases rested on a tort measure without explanation. The court still found it significant that three of four courts chose the value rule.
  • Artificial facts. No ordinary owner would pay $29,000 for "improvements" adding about $300 in value. The court treated the situation as "basically unreasonable and unrealistic."
  • Characterizing the contract. The court rejected both analogies offered: building and construction (Groves) and grading and excavation (the A.L.R. annotation). The lease's primary purpose was the economical recovery and marketing of coal for the profit of all parties. The remedial provisions were incidental to that object.
  • Choosing the test. Restatement of Contracts § 346(1)(a) turns on "unreasonable economic waste," which its comment ties to destroying a substantially completed structure. No such destruction was at stake here. McCormick, Damages § 168 and Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent turn on whether cost is disproportionate to "the end to be attained." The court labeled this "relative economic benefit" and adopted it, citing Mann v. Clowser as in accord.
  • Statutes. 23 O.S. § 96 bars recovering more for breach than full performance would have yielded. § 97 requires reasonable damages and bars "unconscionable and grossly oppressive damages, contrary to substantial justice." Both apply to contract actions. A cost-of-performance award of about nine times the farm's total value would violate both.
  • Liquidated damages analogy. 15 O.S. §§ 214 and 215 void stipulated damages provisions that operate as penalties, regardless of the parties' agreement. §§ 96 and 97 work the same way here: they cap damages despite what the parties agreed.
  • Disposition. The most liberal view of the record showed $300 in diminution, and plaintiffs argued for no higher figure. The court used its conceded power to modify the judgment (12 O.S. § 952; Busboom v. Smith; Stumpf v. Stumpf).
  • Supplemental opinion on rehearing. Plaintiffs claimed surprise and that evidence of their other lands was excluded. The court found they tried the case solely on a cost theory with notice that defendant sought the value rule. The petition described only 60 acres, so evidence of other land was properly excluded. A party may not change theories on appeal (Knox v. Eason Oil Co.). Offering value evidence would not have waived their objection (Vogel v. Fisher). The court also rejected an amici Contract Clause argument: §§ 96 and 97 predated the 1954 lease by decades, distinguishing Sturges v. Crowninshield, and the decision overruled no prior holding the parties relied on. Rehearing was denied by the same 5-4 split.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Irwin, J., dissenting. The opinion text lists Williams, Blackbird, Irwin, and Berry as dissenting. Irwin wrote the only separate opinion. He argued:
  • The breach was willful and in bad faith. Nothing showed defendant could not perform.
  • Plaintiffs insisted on the restoration clauses and would not have signed without them. Restoration was part of their consideration alongside royalties.
  • The cost of performance was foreseeable at signing. Defendant took the bargain knowing cost might exceed the value to plaintiffs.
  • If the value of performance to plaintiffs counts, so should the benefits defendant received. Weighing either rewrites the contract. He relied on Goble v. Bell Oil & Gas Co., Cities Service Oil Co. v. Geolograph Co., and Great Western Oil & Gas Co. v. Mitchell for enforcing contracts as written.
  • § 96 does not apply.
  • The court should follow Groves, under which a willful breacher gets no benefit of the substantial performance doctrine. Defendant did not even substantially perform.
  • Plaintiffs were entitled to specific performance, so cost of performance is the proper measure. Any other measure shifts plaintiffs' benefits to the breaching party.

Notes

  • Narrow framing. The holding is stated for a coal mining lease fully performed except for remedial work. Cost of performance remains the ordinary measure. The value limit applies only when both conditions are met: (1) the breached provision was merely incidental to the main purpose, and (2) the lessor's economic benefit is grossly disproportionate to the cost.
  • Express carve-out. The rule does not interfere with an owner's right to "do what he will with his own" (Chamberlain v. Parker) or to contract for improvements that lower the property's value. Where that result is contemplated and is a main or principal purpose of the contract, cost of performance would ordinarily be the measure.
  • Restatement distinguished. The court did not narrow § 346. It noted that the Restatement's own explanatory comment makes clear its "economic waste" refers to destroying a substantially completed building or other structure, observed that no such destruction was involved here, and held that "relative economic benefit" (drawn from McCormick and Jacob & Youngs) was a proper consideration in view of the unrealistic facts and the Oklahoma statutes.
  • Statutory grounding. The result rests partly on 23 O.S. §§ 96 and 97, which the court said reflect legislative intent.
  • Willfulness. The dissent's willful-breach argument is not discussed in the majority opinion.
  • Procedural limit. On rehearing the $300 figure stood because plaintiffs tried the case only on a cost theory and failed to prove a different diminution. The court treated their objection as coming too late.
  • Overrules nothing. The court stated its decision overrules no prior Oklahoma holdings.
  • Precedents at a glance:
  • Relied on: Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent; Mann v. Clowser; McCormick § 168; Sandy Valley, Bigham, Sweeney (as persuasive weight); Chamberlain v. Parker.
  • Distinguished: Ardizonne v. Archer; Sturges v. Crowninshield (rehearing).
  • Declined to follow: Groves v. John Wunder Co.
  • Procedural: Busboom v. Smith; Stumpf v. Stumpf; Knox v. Eason Oil Co.; Vogel v. Fisher.
