Black-Letter Rule

Whether a land-use regulation is a taking requiring just compensation turns on an essentially ad hoc, factual inquiry. The court weighs (1) the economic impact of the regulation on the claimant, (2) the extent to which it interferes with distinct investment-backed expectations, and (3) the character of the governmental action. The court measures the interference against the parcel as a whole, and diminution in value, standing alone, does not establish a taking.

Facts

  • Parties: Penn Central owns Grand Central Terminal. UGP Properties was its lessee. The defendants were New York City and its Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • Statute: New York City's 1965 Landmarks Preservation Law lets the Commission designate landmarks and landmark sites. Owners must keep exterior features "in good repair." They must also get Commission approval before altering the exterior or building on the site.
  • Designation: In 1967 the Commission designated the Terminal a landmark and its city tax block a landmark site. Penn Central opposed the designation. It did not seek judicial review.
  • Lease: Penn Central leased the airspace to UGP for an office tower. UGP would pay $1 million a year during construction and at least $3 million a year after.
  • Proposals: The first plan, Breuer I, was a 55-story tower cantilevered above the facade. The second, Breuer II Revised, would have torn down part of the Terminal, including the 42d Street facade, and built a 53-story tower. Both plans apparently complied with zoning.
  • Denials: The Commission denied a certificate of no exterior effect and a certificate of appropriateness for both plans. It called the 55-story tower "nothing more than an aesthetic joke." It also said it had "no fixed rule against making additions."
  • No further steps: Penn Central did not seek judicial review of the denials. It did not submit a smaller plan.
  • TDRs: Landmark designation made the Terminal's unused development rights transferable to at least eight nearby parcels, many of them owned by Penn Central. One or two of those parcels were suitable for office buildings.
  • Concessions: Penn Central accepted that the Terminal can earn a reasonable return in its present state. It also accepted that the TDRs are valuable.

Procedural Posture

The New York trial court granted declaratory and injunctive relief. The Appellate Division reversed, and the New York Court of Appeals affirmed, treating the claim as substantive due process and finding Penn Central could earn a reasonable return. Penn Central appealed, and the Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Does applying New York City's Landmarks Preservation Law to bar construction of an office tower above Grand Central Terminal take Penn Central's property without just compensation in violation of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments?

Holding

No: in an opinion by Justice Brennan, the Court held that the restrictions are substantially related to the general welfare, permit reasonable beneficial use of the landmark site, and give Penn Central opportunities to enhance the Terminal and its other properties, so no taking occurred on this record, and the judgment was affirmed.

Reasoning

  • No set formula. The Takings Clause bars government from forcing some people alone to bear public burdens that should be borne by the public as a whole (Armstrong). The Court has no "set formula" for when that principle requires compensation, so it uses ad hoc, factual inquiries.
  • The factors.
  • Economic impact on the claimant.
  • Interference with distinct investment-backed expectations.
  • Character of the government action. A taking is more readily found for a physical invasion (Causby) than for a public program adjusting the benefits and burdens of economic life to promote the common good.
  • Government must be able to diminish values. Quoting Mahon, the Court said government "hardly could go on" if it had to pay for every diminution in value. Zoning and other use restrictions have been upheld even when they barred the most beneficial use, including:
  • Euclid, which barred industrial use;
  • Gorieb, which required setbacks;
  • Welch, which imposed a height limit;
  • Miller, Hadacheck, and Goldblatt, which ended existing uses.
  • Parcel as a whole. The Court rejected the argument that the airspace is a separate property interest that was fully taken. Takings law "does not divide a single parcel into discrete segments." The Court looks at the character of the action and the interference with rights in the parcel as a whole. Here that was the city tax block designated as the landmark site.
  • Diminution in value is not enough. Diminution in value alone does not establish a taking. The decline was 75% in Euclid and 87½% in Hadacheck.
  • Not reverse spot zoning. Landmark laws do not arbitrarily single out one parcel for worse treatment than its neighbors. New York's law is a comprehensive plan covering over 400 landmarks and 31 historic districts.
  • Arbitrariness argument rejected. Owners have judicial review. Courts can spot arbitrary landmark decisions as well as arbitrary zoning decisions. Penn Central sought no review and did not claim the Commission acted arbitrarily.
  • Unequal burden is not a taking. General-welfare laws often burden some owners more than others, as in Hadacheck, Miller, Goldblatt, and Euclid. Footnote 30 rejects reading those cases as "noxious use" cases. They rest on restrictions reasonably related to a policy expected to produce widespread public benefit.
  • Penn Central is also benefited. Penn Central shares in the citywide benefits of landmark preservation. The Court declined to reject the City Council's judgment that preservation benefits all citizens and structures.
  • *Not a Causby appropriation.* The city is not using the parcel for its own or entrepreneurial purposes. The law only bars anyone from occupying part of the airspace.
  • Severity of impact on the parcel.
  • Present use untouched. The law leaves the Terminal's 65-year use as a railroad terminal with offices and concessions in place. That was Penn Central's "primary expectation."
  • Reasonable return. On this record Penn Central can profit from the Terminal and earn a reasonable return.
  • Airspace not fully barred. The Commission rejected towers over 50 stories, but nothing shows it would bar every structure. Penn Central never proposed a smaller one. Footnote 34 notes counsel conceded the Commission had not suggested it would reject a 20-story tower.
  • TDRs count toward impact. TDRs "may well not have constituted 'just compensation'" if there had been a taking. They still mitigate the financial burden and count in assessing the regulation's impact.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Rehnquist, joined by the Chief Justice and Justice Stevens (dissenting).
  • Property. "Property" means the whole group of rights in the thing (General Motors), and air rights are property.
  • Taken. The city destroyed valuable rights and imposed a "nonconsensual servitude" not borne by neighboring properties.
  • Neither exception applies. Destroying property is not a taking in two situations, and neither fits this case:
  • The nuisance exception (Mugler, Curtin v. Benson) does not apply because the tower complied with zoning and safety law. Penn Central is restricted "because too good a job was done in designing and building it."
  • The "average reciprocity of advantage" that saves zoning (Mahon) is missing. A few scattered buildings bear a multimillion-dollar cost while the benefits go to the whole city.
  • Affirmative duty. Landmark law goes beyond zoning by imposing an affirmative duty to preserve the property at the owner's expense.
  • Reasonable return is not a defense. "It is the character of the invasion, not the amount of damage" that decides whether there is a taking (Cress).
  • Unclear test. Footnote 13 says the majority shifts among "unduly harsh impact," "reasonable return," and "economically viable." It also says the majority leaves the relevant property unit unresolved.
  • Just compensation. Just compensation requires a "full and perfect equivalent" (Monongahela), and that is a judicial question. The record was too thin to decide whether TDRs meet that standard, so the dissent would remand on that question.

Notes

  • Only the taking question decided. The Court decided only whether a taking occurred. It did not decide whether TDRs would be just compensation, and it said they "may well not" be.
  • Limited to the present record. The holding rests on Penn Central's present ability to use the Terminal gainfully (n.36). The city conceded at oral argument that relief may be available if the Terminal later ceases to be "economically viable."
  • Physical control not required. The Court does not accept that a taking requires the government to take physical control of part of a parcel (n.25).
  • Left open: "social increments." The Court had no occasion to decide whether courts may separate the publicly created value of property from its privately created value (n.23).
  • Left open: future proposals. The Court did not decide whether the Commission could deny a smaller addition. No such plan had been submitted.
  • Maintenance costs. The good-repair duty will "presumably always be" factored into the constitutional inquiry (n.21).
  • Relied on. Euclid, Welch, Gorieb, Goldblatt, Miller v. Schoene, Hadacheck, Mugler, Nectow, and Armstrong. For aesthetic and quality-of-life goals: Berman, Belle Terre, Young, and New Orleans v. Dukes.
  • *Treated as the leading case for a regulatory taking: Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon. That statute made mining commercially impracticable, which was nearly the same as destroying the reserved rights. Footnote 27 rejects reading Mahon* to make loss of air rights a taking regardless of the impact on the whole parcel.
  • Distinguished. Causby, Griggs, Portsmouth, and Cress involved government use or invasion of the land for public functions. Discriminatory "reverse spot" zoning arbitrarily singles out a parcel.
  • Overruled. None.
