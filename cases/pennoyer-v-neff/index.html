Black-Letter Rule

A state court can bind a non-resident defendant personally only if the defendant is served with process within the State or voluntarily appears. Substituted service by publication on a non-resident works only in actions in the nature of a proceeding in rem, meaning the defendant's in-state property is brought under the court's control at the start of the action by attachment or an equivalent act, and the judgment then binds only that property. A personal judgment rendered without such jurisdiction is void, does not satisfy due process of law under the Fourteenth Amendment, and cannot support a later execution sale of the defendant's property.

Facts

  • Parties: Neff (plaintiff below) sued Pennoyer (defendant below) to recover possession of a tract of Oregon land of alleged value of $15,000.
  • Neff's title: Neff held the land under a U.S. patent issued in 1866 under the Oregon Donation Law.
  • The earlier suit: Mitchell sued Neff in an Oregon state circuit court on a demand for attorney's services. Mitchell obtained a default judgment in February 1866.
  • Service: Neff was a non-resident of Oregon. He was not personally served and did not appear. Service was by publication under the Oregon Code.
  • No attachment: The land was never attached or brought under the court's control during the suit. Its first connection to the case was the levy of execution after judgment.
  • Pennoyer's title: The land was sold on execution of Mitchell's judgment. Pennoyer claimed under the resulting sheriff's deed.
  • Oregon statute: The Code allowed publication service on absent non-residents with property in the State and allowed attachment in money actions. It also declared that a non-resident with property in the State is subject to jurisdiction "only to the extent of such property at the time the jurisdiction attached."

Procedural Posture

Neff's suit to recover possession was tried in the Circuit Court, which held Mitchell's judgment invalid because of defects in the affidavit supporting the publication order and in the affidavit proving publication. The court did not treat attachment as essential to jurisdiction. Pennoyer brought the case to the Supreme Court as plaintiff in error.

Issue

Is a personal money judgment entered by a state court against a non-resident, based only on publication service with no in-state service, no appearance, and no attachment of property at the outset, valid enough to support a later execution sale of the non-resident's land in that State?

Holding

No. A state court lacks jurisdiction to render a personal judgment against a non-resident who was neither served within the State nor appeared, and publication service supports jurisdiction only where in-state property is brought under the court's control at the commencement of the action; the Mitchell judgment was therefore void and passed no title to Pennoyer. Justice Field wrote for the Court. The judgment was affirmed on this ground, which differs from the lower court's reasoning. The opinion does not state the vote.

Reasoning

  • The lower court's grounds rejected. The majority held that affidavit defects in the publication order can be raised only on appeal or another direct proceeding. They cannot support a collateral attack. An affidavit of publication by the paper's editor satisfied the statute's "printer" requirement, read as "publisher" (Bunce v. Reed; Sharp v. Daugney). The Court affirmed anyway on the jurisdictional ground Neff also pressed below.
  • Two principles of public law. Every State has exclusive jurisdiction and sovereignty over persons and property within its territory. No State can exercise direct jurisdiction over persons or property outside its territory (Story; Wheaton). The States keep these attributes of independent sovereigns except as the Constitution limits them.
  • Indirect effects are permitted. A State's proper exercise of its own power may affect outside persons or property. Examples include ordering a domiciliary to convey land located elsewhere (Penn v. Lord Baltimore; Massie v. Watts) and applying a non-resident's in-state property to a citizen's claim.
  • Property-based jurisdiction is limited. The court's power to inquire into a non-resident's obligations comes only from its jurisdiction over the in-state property. The inquiry extends only as far as needed to dispose of that property. With no property, there is nothing to adjudicate.
  • Timing of seizure matters. Jurisdiction must exist before judgment. A judgment void when rendered stays void. It cannot become valid by later discovery or acquisition of property. Otherwise validity would turn on whether the defendant sold the property before the levy, which would add uncertainty to judicial proceedings.
  • Why seizure suffices for notice. The law assumes property is in the owner's possession, in person or by agent. Seizure therefore tells the owner that the property is in the court's custody and that he must watch for the proceedings. Publication alone does not do this.
  • In rem defined. Strictly, an in rem proceeding runs against the property itself without regard to individual claimants. In the broader sense, it includes actions between parties whose direct object is to reach property, such as attachment, partition, mortgage foreclosure, and lien enforcement.
  • Personal actions. Where the entire object is to determine personal rights and obligations, publication on a non-resident "is ineffectual for any purpose." Process cannot run into another State. Publishing it within the forum creates no greater obligation to appear.
  • Full faith and credit cases. Under M'Elmoyle v. Cohen, D'Arcy v. Ketchum, Lafayette Insurance Co. v. French, and Thompson v. Whitman, a sister State may inquire into the rendering court's jurisdiction. Courts have uniformly refused effect to personal judgments against unserved non-residents beyond the property attached (Bissell v. Briggs; Kilbourn v. Woodworth). Federal courts owe state judgments only the faith and credit a sister State would.
  • Void even at home. The Court rejected older dicta that such judgments, though not binding elsewhere, may be valid in the rendering State. A judgment that is a nullity for want of jurisdiction over the party is entitled to no respect anywhere.
  • Due process. Since the Fourteenth Amendment, these judgments may be attacked directly and their enforcement resisted in the rendering State. In the Court's own words, due process in judicial proceedings requires a tribunal competent by the law of its creation to pass on the subject matter. If the suit involves merely personal liability, the defendant must be brought in by service of process within the State or by voluntary appearance. The Court then quoted Cooley: for any purpose other than subjecting a non-resident's property to valid claims in the State, "due process of law would require appearance or personal service before the defendant could be personally bound by any judgment rendered."
  • Policy. Personal judgments on publication alone, which most defendants would never see, would become "constant instruments of fraud and oppression," entered on claims whose evidence had perished.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Hunt (dissent; no joiners stated). A State controls property within its limits and may subject it to debts owed its citizens, even if the owner is a non-resident who cannot be personally served. Personal notice is not indispensable. The legislature chooses the mode of notice. If the method is reasonably likely and designed in good faith to inform the defendant, and he may defend on appearing, the courts cannot call it a denial of due process (relying on Matter of the Empire City Bank, Happy v. Mosher, Jarvis v. Barrett). Whether property is seized at the start or at the end of the suit is "municipal regulation," not constitutional power. Seizure itself is not notice. Hunt argued that nearly every State and Congress (for the District of Columbia) authorize this process, so the decision unsettles titles held under such statutes for nearly a century. He read Cooper v. Reynolds's contrary remarks as extra-judicial. He distinguished Galpin v. Page and Webster v. Reid on their facts. He stressed that the question of a judgment's conclusiveness in another State was not before the Court.

Notes

  • Scope as stated by the Court. The question decided "respects only the validity of a money judgment rendered in one State, in an action upon a simple contract against the resident of another, without service of process upon him, or his appearance therein."
  • Expressly reserved: status. A State may determine its citizen's status toward a non-resident without personal service, with effect within the State. Divorce is the Court's example.
  • Expressly reserved: consent. A State may require non-residents forming partnerships or associations there, or making contracts enforceable there, to appoint an in-state agent or designate a place for service. On default, it may allow service on a public officer, and resulting judgments may bind both within and outside the State (Vallee v. Dumergue; Lafayette Insurance Co. v. French; Gillespie).
  • Expressly reserved: corporations. A State creating corporations may provide for proceedings against them without personal service on officers or members (Copin v. Adamson).
  • Appellate proceedings excluded. The rule covers courts of first instance. Appellate review may proceed on whatever notice the State provides, as a continuation of the original litigation (Nations v. Johnson).
  • Relied on as binding: Cooper v. Reynolds (quasi in rem judgment reaches only attached property; no attachment ends the case). Field notes he dissented there on other grounds but joined this doctrine, which all judges approved. Also Webster v. Reid (judgments without service or prior attachment are nullities), D'Arcy v. Ketchum, M'Elmoyle v. Cohen, Lafayette Insurance Co. v. French, and Thompson v. Whitman.
  • Relied on as persuasive only: Picquet v. Swan and Boswell's Lessee v. Otis, cited as views of eminent jurists because the language may not have been essential to those decisions.
  • Disapproved: State-court dicta that personal judgments without jurisdiction are binding within the rendering State. No case is expressly overruled.
  • Affirmed on alternative ground: The Court disagreed with both of the lower court's affidavit rulings.
