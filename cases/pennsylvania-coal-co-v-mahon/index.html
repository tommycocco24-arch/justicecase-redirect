Black-Letter Rule

Property may be regulated to a certain extent, but if regulation goes too far it will be recognized as a taking that requires compensation. Whether a regulation goes too far is a question of degree that depends on the particular facts, including the extent of the diminution in value, the extent of the public interest, and whether the law secures an average reciprocity of advantage. Making it commercially impracticable to mine coal has nearly the same constitutional effect as appropriating or destroying it.

Facts

  • Parties: The Mahons, surface owners and plaintiffs below, sued Pennsylvania Coal Co. to stop it from mining under their house in a way that would remove support and cause the surface and house to subside.
  • Deed: In 1878 the Coal Company conveyed the surface under a deed that expressly reserved the right to remove all the coal beneath. The grantee took the premises with the risk and waived all claims for damages from mining out the coal. The plaintiffs claim under that deed.
  • Statute: The Kohler Act (1921) forbids mining anthracite coal in a way that causes subsidence of, among other things, any structure used as a human habitation. It exempts land where the surface owner also owns the coal and the land is more than 150 feet from improved property belonging to someone else.
  • Concession: As applied here, the statute is admitted to destroy previously existing rights of property and contract.
  • Notice: The Coal Company gave timely notice of its intent to mine under the house. That notice was the foundation of the bill.
  • Broader participation: The state Attorney General, the City of Scranton, and other extensive interests took part in the argument below and submitted contentions to the Court.

Procedural Posture

The Court of Common Pleas found the Coal Company would cause the damage if not restrained but denied an injunction, holding the statute unconstitutional as applied. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court agreed the company had federally protected contract and property rights, held the statute a legitimate exercise of the police power, and directed a decree for the plaintiffs; the U.S. Supreme Court granted a writ of error.

Issue

Can a state, through its police power and without paying compensation, forbid a coal company from mining in a way that causes subsidence of a private house when the company expressly reserved the right to mine and the surface owner waived damages?

Holding

No. In an opinion by Justice Holmes, the Court held that the Kohler Act cannot be sustained as an exercise of the police power where the right to mine the coal has been reserved, because the regulation goes so far that it amounts to a taking without compensation, and the decree was reversed.

Reasoning

  • Police power has limits. Government could not go on if every diminution in property value required payment. Some values are held under an implied limitation and must yield to the police power. That limitation must itself have limits, or the contract and due process clauses are gone.
  • Diminution in value. One fact for consideration is the extent of the diminution. When it reaches a certain magnitude, in most if not all cases, the state must use eminent domain and pay compensation. The question depends on the particular facts.
  • Deference with a ceiling. The legislature's judgment gets the greatest weight. Interested parties can still argue the legislature exceeded its constitutional power.
  • Limited public interest (as applied). This was a single private house. Some public interest exists in every private transaction, and some rights may be modified even in such a case. Ordinarily, private affairs do not warrant much of this interference.
  • Damage to one house is not a public nuisance, even if similar damage occurs elsewhere. It is not common or public.
  • The statute's own exception for land where one owner holds both surface and coal shows the public interest it serves is limited.
  • Personal safety does not justify the act because notice could protect it, and notice was given here.
  • Great extent of the taking. The act purports to abolish what Pennsylvania recognizes as an estate in land, a very valuable one, and what the state court called a contract binding on the plaintiffs.
  • Right to coal is the right to mine it. Its value lies in profitable extraction. Making mining commercially impracticable is nearly the same, constitutionally, as appropriating or destroying the coal. The Court assumed the statute does this.
  • General validity reached. Because the state and other interests argued the act's general validity, the Court went further than the single house. It held the act cannot be sustained as a police power measure as it affects mining under streets or cities where the right to mine has been reserved, so that further suits would not be brought in vain.
  • Public should pay for what it wants. The public's rights in a street are those it paid for. If it bought only surface rights without support, there is no more authority to supply the support right free than to take a right of way without paying. The Fifth Amendment presupposes property is wanted for public use and requires compensation, and the Fourteenth Amendment decisions make a similar assumption.
  • Slippery slope. Once the seemingly absolute protection is qualified by the police power, human nature tends to extend the qualification until private property disappears. The Constitution does not allow that.
  • Who bears the loss. The Court assumed an exigency existed that would justify eminent domain. The real question is on whom the loss should fall. Persons or communities who chose to acquire only surface rights took a risk. That the risk became a danger does not entitle them to greater rights than they bought.
  • Shortcut rejected. A strong public desire to improve public conditions does not justify a shorter cut than the constitutional route of paying for the change.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Brandeis, dissenting (no joiners stated in the text):
  • Noxious use. Coal in place is land, and an owner may not use land to create a public nuisance. When changed conditions make a use threaten public welfare, the legislature may prohibit it without compensation. The Kohler Act merely prohibits a noxious use.
  • Restriction is not a taking. The owner keeps possession. The state neither appropriates nor uses the property. If the use stops being noxious, the restriction must be lifted.
  • Appropriate means. Keeping coal in place is an appropriate and ordinarily the only means to prevent subsidence. A restriction does not become inappropriate because it eliminates the only profitable use (Mugler v. Kansas; Powell v. Pennsylvania; Hadacheck v. Los Angeles; Pierce Oil Corp. v. City of Hope), or because the same end could be reached through eminent domain.
  • Denominator. Values are relative. The coal kept in place should be compared with the value of the whole property. Dividing ownership into surface and subsoil cannot increase the owner's rights against the public. For aught that appears, the restricted coal may be negligible compared with the whole.
  • Record and deference. A general police regulation ordinarily is not voided as to a particular property. In any event, the company offered no evidence that the restriction was unreasonable. Courts should defer to the legislature and state high court on whether notice adequately protects public safety.
  • No bargaining away the police power. If public safety is imperiled, neither grant nor contract can prevail against the police power. Private contracts cannot remove rights from state restriction (Hudson County Water Co. v. McCarter).
  • Reciprocity of advantage. Reciprocity matters, and may be essential, when the state confers benefits on property owners, as in drainage or party-wall laws. It has no place when the state protects the public from harm. The only reciprocal advantage in the nuisance cases was living and doing business in a civilized community, and the act gives coal operators that.
  • Public structures. The provisions covering public buildings, streets, and public-service facilities plainly serve a public purpose, and notice would not secure safety there.

Notes

  • Question of degree. The Court said the issue cannot be disposed of by general propositions. It turns on particular facts.
  • Narrow and broad holdings. The as-applied analysis concerned a single private house. The Court then expressly extended its view to mining under streets or cities where the right to mine was reserved, because the act's general validity had been argued.
  • Left open: How far exceptional cases, such as blowing up a house to stop a conflagration, go beyond the general rule, and whether they rest as much on tradition as on principle (Bowditch v. Boston).
  • Assumptions: The Court assumed an exigency existed that would justify the act and that would warrant eminent domain.
  • Distinguished:
  • Plymouth Coal Co. v. Pennsylvania: upheld a required coal pillar along a property line because it protected employees invited into the mine and secured an average reciprocity of advantage.
  • Block v. Hirsh, Marcus Brown Holding Co. v. Feldman, Levy Leasing Co. v. Siegel: wartime housing laws addressing a temporary emergency and providing compensation set by an impartial board. They "went to the verge of the law but fell far short of the present act."
  • Relied on:
  • Rideout v. Knox: some existing rights may be modified even in a private case.
  • Wesson v. Washburn Iron Co.: damage to a private house is not common or public, so not a public nuisance.
  • Commonwealth v. Clearview Coal Co.: "For practical purposes, the right to coal consists in the right to mine it."
  • Hairston v. Danville & Western Ry. Co.: Fourteenth Amendment decisions share the Fifth Amendment's public-use assumption.
  • Spade v. Lynn & Boston R.R. Co.: a person's misfortunes do not justify shifting damages to a neighbor.
  • Overruled: None stated in the opinion.
