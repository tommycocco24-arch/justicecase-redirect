Black-Letter Rule

Under Penal Law § 35.15, deadly physical force in defense of a person is justified only if the defendant actually believed it necessary and a reasonable person in the defendant's situation could have held the same beliefs. The "situation" includes the defendant's relevant knowledge of the assailants, the physical attributes of everyone involved, and the defendant's prior experiences. A prosecutor's charge to a Grand Jury on justification need not match the precision of a trial jury charge. It is adequate if it lets the grand jurors intelligently decide whether the defense should preclude prosecution.

Facts

  • Parties: Bernhard Goetz shot four youths (Canty, Allen, Ramseur, Cabey) on a Manhattan subway train.
  • The encounter: Canty, possibly with Allen beside him, approached Goetz and said "give me five dollars." No youth displayed a weapon. Ramseur and Cabey had screwdrivers inside their coats.
  • The shooting: Goetz drew an unlicensed .38 and fired four rapid shots, hitting Canty, Allen (in the back), and Ramseur and missing Cabey. After surveying the scene, he fired again at Cabey, who was sitting on a bench. That bullet severed Cabey's spinal cord.
  • Goetz's statements: After surrendering, Goetz said he had carried the gun illegally for three years after being mugged in 1981. He said he had twice warded off assailants by displaying it. He was certain none of the youths had a gun but feared being "maimed." He said he intended to "murder [them], to hurt them, to make them suffer as much as possible." Before the last shot he told Cabey, "[y]ou seem to be all right, here's another."
  • Flight: Goetz told the conductor the youths had tried to rob him. He then jumped onto the tracks and fled, surrendering nine days later in New Hampshire.
  • Post-indictment information: A Daily News column reported that Cabey said the other three intended to rob Goetz. A police officer reported that Canty told him at the scene "we were going to rob [Goetz]."

Procedural Posture

A second Grand Jury indicted Goetz on attempted murder, first-degree assault, reckless endangerment, and weapons charges. Criminal Term dismissed all counts except reckless endangerment. It found two grounds: the prosecutor's justification charge wrongly added an objective element, and People v. Pelchat required dismissal because of apparent perjury. A divided Appellate Division affirmed on the justification ground, and the People appealed by leave granted by a dissenting Justice.

Issue

Does Penal Law § 35.15 require that a defendant's belief in the need for deadly force be objectively reasonable, so that a Grand Jury instruction framed around "a reasonable man in the defendant's situation" is proper?

Holding

Yes. Section 35.15 contains both a subjective element and an objective element, so the prosecutor's charge was essentially accurate, and neither it nor the later-surfacing hearsay required dismissal. The Appellate Division's order was reversed and the dismissed counts reinstated. Chief Judge Wachtler wrote for the court, with Judges Meyer, Simons, Kaye, Alexander, Titone and Hancock, Jr., concurring.

Reasoning

  • Statutory structure. Section 35.15 sets two requirements for deadly force, each phrased as "reasonably believes":
  • Triggering condition: the actor reasonably believes the other person is using or about to use deadly physical force, or is committing or attempting kidnapping, forcible rape, forcible sodomy, or robbery.
  • Necessity: the actor reasonably believes deadly force is necessary to avert the threat.
  • Plain meaning. Reading "reasonably believes" to mean "reasonable to him" defies the ordinary meaning of "reasonably" in a statute. The court found that reading also contrary to the Legislature's clear intent to keep an objective element.
  • Statutory history. New York's self-defense statutes from 1829 through 1909 required a "reasonable ground" for the belief. Shorter, Lumsden, Ligouri, and Tomlins applied an objective standard and rejected the defendant's own belief as sufficient. Lumsden held that "mere fear or fancy or remote hearsay information or a delusion pure and simple" would not suffice. The 1937 Law Revision Commission described the standard as that of "a man of ordinary courage" in the defendant's circumstances.
  • Model Penal Code comparison. MPC § 3.04 uses a purely subjective "believes" standard. Under MPC § 3.09, a reckless or negligent mistake can support conviction for a reckless or negligent offense. New York borrowed § 3.04's structure but inserted "reasonably" before "believes." It also chose an all-or-nothing defense over the MPC's graded approach. Had the drafters wanted the subjective test, they could have copied § 3.04.
  • Explaining the wording change. The move from "reasonable ground" to "reasonably believes" reflects the merger of ordinary-force and deadly-force rules into one section. Before 1965, the ordinary-force provisions required an actual unlawful assault. Adding "reasonably" carried the old "reasonable ground" requirement forward and extended it to ordinary force. The drafters' commentary said the deadly-force provisions largely conformed to prior law. The Commission's staff comment on § 35.30 equated "reasonably believes" with having a reasonable ground.
  • "Reasonable" means objective elsewhere. People v. Cantor read "reasonably suspects" in CPL 140.50 to require knowledge sufficient for "an ordinarily prudent and cautious man." People v. Collice already rejected a wholly subjective reading of § 35.15.
  • Policy. A subjective test would let people "set their own standards for the permissible use of force." It would also let a legally competent but delusional defendant commit violence with impunity. The court would not lightly impute that intent to the Legislature.
  • An objective standard still accounts for the defendant. "Circumstances" and "situation" include the assailant's movements, the defendant's knowledge about the assailant, the physical attributes of all persons involved, and the defendant's prior experiences that could reasonably ground the belief.
  • Two-step jury inquiry.
  • Did the defendant believe deadly force was necessary to avert imminent deadly force or an enumerated felony?
  • If the People fail to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant lacked those beliefs, could a reasonable person in the defendant's circumstances have held them?
  • Grand Jury standard. Under Valles and Calbud, a Grand Jury charge need not match a petit jury charge in precision, because the Grand Jury only decides whether to accuse. When the evidence suggests a complete defense, the prosecutor must charge it with enough information for the grand jurors to decide whether it precludes prosecution. The charge here did that, even though it did not explain that "situation" includes Goetz's prior experiences.
  • *The Pelchat ground. In Pelchat*, the sole Grand Jury witness recanted, and the prosecutor knew no legally sufficient evidence remained but let the case proceed. Here Canty and Ramseur recanted nothing. The new material was conflicting hearsay. Criminal Term had no basis to speculate about perjury, especially since it was not in the Grand Jury room. Goetz's own statements and the passengers' testimony independently supported the charges.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Limits. The court stressed that it reached no conclusion on what actually happened or on whether Goetz is blameworthy. Credibility and the reasonableness of Goetz's conduct are for the trial jury. The question for trial is whether the People can prove beyond a reasonable doubt that his reactions were unreasonable and therefore excessive.
  • Left open. The propriety of the order allowing resubmission to the second Grand Jury was not before the court (footnote 1).
  • Statutory details from the footnotes.
  • Subdivision (1) contains exceptions, such as for the initial aggressor.
  • Under § 35.15(2)(a), the actor ordinarily must retreat if he knows he can do so with complete safety to himself and others.
  • Subdivision (2)(b) has no separate retreat clause. Even so, the general necessity requirement of subdivision (1) applies to deadly force used to avert a felony such as robbery.
  • Reckless endangerment count. Criminal Term kept this count based on the Appellate Division's McManus decision, which held justification was no defense to depraved-indifference crimes. The Court of Appeals reversed that decision in People v. McManus (67 NY2d 541) and held justification is a defense to such crimes. Had the charge been defective, that count would have been dismissed too (footnote 2).
  • Relied on:
  • Shorter v People, People v Lumsden, People v Ligouri, and People v Tomlins: objective reasonableness under prior statutes.
  • People v Collice: § 35.15 is not wholly subjective.
  • People v Cantor: "reasonably" imports an objective standard.
  • People v Valles and People v Calbud, Inc.: Grand Jury charge standard.
  • People v McManus: justification generally.
  • Agreeing authority cited:
  • People v Astle and People v Hamel (3d Dept), with Astle declining to follow the First Department's decision in this case.
  • State v Kelly (N.J.) and Weston v State (Alaska), reading "reasonably believes" in post-MPC statutes as objective.
  • Distinguished or explained:
  • People v Miller: its "state of mind of the defendant" language addresses the subjective element. Knowledge of the victim's prior violence also bears on reasonableness, and Miller itself required "reasonable grounds."
  • People v Rodawald: reputation evidence goes to the threshold subjective belief. Rodawald also required "reasonable ground" and upheld refusing an honest-belief-only charge.
  • People v Pelchat: limited to a recanted sole source of evidence known to the prosecutor.
  • Rejected reasoning. The lower courts relied on People v Santiago (1st Dept) and People v Wagman (2d Dept) for a wholly subjective test. The Court of Appeals rejected that reading of § 35.15.
