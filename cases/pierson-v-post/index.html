Black-Letter Rule

Property in a wild animal (ferae naturae) is acquired only by occupancy. Occupancy requires actual corporal possession, or an act that deprives the animal of its natural liberty and brings it within the pursuer's certain control, such as mortally wounding it while continuing pursuit, or trapping or encompassing it with nets so escape is impossible. Mere pursuit, even when capture seems imminent, confers no property right.

Facts

  • Parties: Post hunted a fox with his hounds. Pierson intercepted, killed, and carried off the fox.
  • The animal: The parties admitted the fox was ferae naturae and that property in such animals is acquired by occupancy only.
  • The hunt: Post's claim rested on pursuit with his hounds "in the manner alleged in his declaration." The majority treated the case as "one of mere pursuit." Post had not wounded, trapped, or otherwise seized the fox.
  • Dissent's framing: Post started and hunted the fox on waste and uninhabited ground and was on the point of seizing it. Pierson killed it in view of Post and his dogs, knowing of the chase.

Procedural Posture

Post sued Pierson before a justice of Queen's County and won. Pierson brought the judgment to the Supreme Court on certiorari. Of six exceptions taken below, only one remained, which presented the single question below.

Issue

Does a hunter acquire a property right in a wild animal through pursuit alone, so that he can sue a person who intercepts and kills it?

Holding

No. Pursuit alone vests no property in a wild animal, so Post had no right to the fox and no action against Pierson. The judgment below was reversed in a per curiam opinion delivered by Tompkins, J., with Livingston, J., dissenting.

Reasoning

  • Framing: The parties' admissions reduced the case to one question: what acts amount to occupancy of a wild animal?
  • Ancient authorities: Justinian's Institutes, Fleta, and Bracton hold that pursuit alone vests no property, and that pursuit with wounding also fails unless the animal is actually taken. Under these sources, the judgment below was "obviously erroneous."
  • Puffendorf and Bynkershoek: Both define occupancy as actual corporal possession. Puffendorf states, with hesitation, that another may not fairly intercept a mortally wounded or greatly maimed animal while the wounder is still pursuing it.
  • English cases: These were of little help. They turned on positive statutes, or on disputes between hunters (claiming by occupancy) and landowners (claiming ratione soli).
  • Barbeyrac and Grotius: The court accepted Barbeyrac's point that bodily seizure is not always necessary. Barbeyrac never says pursuit alone is enough, however. Grotius's qualified rule, possession through instruments such as traps and nets that are in our power and enclose the animal so it cannot escape, covers the full extent of Barbeyrac's objection.
  • What counts as occupancy:
  • Mortal wounding by one who does not abandon pursuit. This shows an unequivocal intent to appropriate, deprives the animal of natural liberty, and brings it within certain control.
  • Encompassing and securing the animal with nets and toils, or otherwise intercepting it, so as to deprive it of natural liberty and make escape impossible.
  • Application: Post's case involved mere pursuit. It fell outside every definition of occupancy the court considered.
  • Policy: A narrow occupancy rule promotes certainty and preserves peace and order. Letting first sight, starting, or pursuit support a lawsuit "would prove a fertile source of quarrels and litigation."
  • No legal injury: Pierson's conduct may have been "uncourteous or unkind," but it caused no injury for which the law gives a remedy.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Livingston, J. (dissenting): Would affirm.
  • Sportsmen's custom: The question should have gone to the arbitration of sportsmen, whose custom would have resolved it promptly.
  • Policy on foxes: The fox is a "wild and noxious beast," and killing it benefits the public and farmers. The law should encourage fox hunting, and no one would keep hounds if an intruder could take the prize at the end of the chase.
  • Old authority: Justinian's code is centuries old and did not contemplate fox hunting for sport. "Tempora mutantur," and law may change with the times.
  • Barbeyrac's rule: He adopts Barbeyrac's view as most rational. Property in wild animals may be acquired without bodily touch if the pursuer is within reach or has a reasonable prospect of taking an animal he has shown an intention to convert to his own use.
  • Emperor's rule: Absent local regulation, he would follow an emperor's rule that a beast pursued with large dogs and hounds belongs to the hunter, not the chance occupant.
  • Proposed holding: A pursuit through waste and unoccupied lands that "must inevitably and speedily have terminated in corporal possession" confers a right enforceable against anyone who interferes.

Notes

  • Narrow ground: The court decided a case of "mere pursuit." It identified mortal wounding with continued pursuit, and trapping or encompassing that makes escape impossible, as acts that can amount to occupancy. Neither was present here.
  • Admitted premises: The fox's status as ferae naturae and occupancy as the sole mode of acquisition were conceded. The court did not decide them.
  • Landowner claims: The opinion notes the English ratione soli cases but does not resolve hunter versus landowner disputes.
  • Relied on: Justinian's Institutes, Fleta, Bracton, Puffendorf, Bynkershoek, and Grotius. Barbeyrac is relied on only to the extent his objection to Puffendorf matches Grotius's qualified rule.
  • Distinguished: 11 Mod. 74–130 (also reported at 3 Salk. 9). That action was for maliciously hindering the plaintiff's enjoyment of a private franchise. Holt, C.J., stressed that the ducks were in the plaintiff's decoy pond and so in his possession ratione soli.
  • English reports generally: These were set aside as unhelpful because they rested on statutes or landowner claims.
  • Overruled: No case is overruled.
  • Dissent's caveat: Livingston left correction of any mistake to "a higher tribunal."
