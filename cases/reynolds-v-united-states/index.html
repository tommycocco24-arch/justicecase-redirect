Black-Letter Rule

The Free Exercise Clause bars Congress from legislating over religious belief and opinion. It leaves Congress free to punish actions that violate social duties or subvert good order. Marriage is a civil contract regulated by law, so a government may decide whether monogamy or polygamy is the law of social life, and sincere religious belief is no defense to a knowing violation of a valid bigamy statute.

Facts

  • Statute: In 1862 Congress made bigamy a crime in the Territories (12 Stat. 501), citing the evil consequences thought to flow from plural marriages.
  • Defendant's religion: The accused was a longtime member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints. He proved the church taught that male members had a divinely enjoined duty to practice polygamy when circumstances permitted, on pain of damnation.
  • Second marriage: With permission from church authorities, he was married to a woman named Schofield by Daniel H. Wells, a church official, under church doctrine. He knew his first wife was living and that the second marriage was forbidden by law.
  • Jury instruction: He asked for an acquittal instruction if the jury found he married in pursuance of what he believed was a religious duty. The court refused and charged that religious belief did not excuse him and that the law implies criminal intent.
  • Absent witness: The alleged second wife, Amelia Jane Schofield, had testified at a prior trial for the same offense under another indictment. When an officer sought to serve her, the accused refused to say where she was and said she would not get into trouble "till the subpoena is served upon her." The court admitted her former testimony.
  • Juries: A 15-person grand jury, authorized by territorial statute, returned the indictment. The government's challenges to jurors who were or had been living in polygamy were sustained. The accused's challenge to juror Read, who "believed" he had an unexpressed opinion that would not influence his verdict, was overruled.
  • Charge on consequences: The trial judge told the jury to consider the consequences to the "innocent victims" of polygamy, the women and children.

Procedural Posture

The accused was indicted and convicted of bigamy in the District Court of the third judicial district of Utah Territory, and he brought the case to the Supreme Court as plaintiff in error. The Court affirmed, then on petition for rehearing vacated its affirmance and reversed only to correct the sentence.

Issue

Does the First Amendment's guarantee of free exercise of religion bar conviction under a federal bigamy statute of a person who married a second time because he believed polygamy was his religious duty?

Holding

No. Congress may reach religiously motivated conduct, though not belief, and a person who knowingly commits the prohibited act is guilty regardless of religious belief, so the judgment was affirmed in an opinion by Chief Justice Waite (no vote stated). On rehearing the Court vacated the affirmance and reversed and remanded solely to strike "at hard labor" from the sentence.

Reasoning

  • Meaning of "religion": The Constitution does not define it, so the Court looked to the history surrounding the First Amendment's adoption.
  • Virginia history: Madison's "Memorial and Remonstrance" opposed a bill to support teachers of Christianity. Jefferson's bill for establishing religious freedom passed instead. Its preamble said civil officers may interfere "when principles break out into overt acts against peace and good order." The Court called this "the true distinction" between church and state.
  • Jefferson's Danbury letter: Legislative powers "reach actions only, and not opinions." The Court accepted this "almost as an authoritative declaration" of the Amendment's scope. Congress lacks power over mere opinion. It may reach actions violating social duties or subversive of good order.
  • History of polygamy law: At common law the second marriage was void. England punished polygamy, first in ecclesiastical courts as a matrimonial offense and then under 1 James I, c. 11, with death. The colonies re-enacted that statute. Virginia did so in 1788, after passing its religious freedom act. Every State has since treated polygamy as an offense. The Court found it "impossible to believe" the free exercise guaranty was meant to bar such legislation.
  • Marriage as civil institution (Family Law core): Marriage is "a sacred obligation" by nature but a civil contract "usually regulated by law." Society is built on it, and government must deal with the social relations and duties that spring from it. Relying on Lieber and Kent, the Court said polygamy leads to the patriarchal principle and "stationary despotism." Unless restricted by some form of constitution, every civil government may choose monogamy or polygamy as the law of social life.
  • No religious exemption: Exempting believers would mean nonbelievers are punished while believers go free. It would make religious doctrine superior to law and "permit every citizen to become a law unto himself." The Court illustrated with human sacrifice and a widow's self-immolation.
  • Criminal intent: A person is presumed to intend the necessary consequences of what he knowingly does. He knew he was married, knew his wife was living, and knew the law forbade a second marriage. Ignorance of fact can negate intent. Ignorance of law cannot. His defense was only a belief that the law should not exist.
  • Other rulings:
  • Grand jury: Territorial district courts are not U.S. circuit or district courts, so Rev. Stat. § 808's 16-juror minimum does not apply, and the territorial 15-juror statute governs.
  • Juror Read: A hypothetical opinion does not raise a manifest presumption of partiality. The challenger bears the burden, and the trial court's finding stands unless the error is manifest.
  • Polygamist jurors: They were properly excluded. If not for principal cause, then for favor, and the form of challenge does not matter.
  • Confrontation: A defendant who wrongfully keeps a witness away cannot invoke the Confrontation Clause. The rule rests on the maxim that no one may profit from his own wrong. The facts shifted the burden to him to explain, and he stayed silent. The prior testimony was given in substantially the same cause, with the accused present and able to cross-examine.
  • Charge on consequences: The charge was no appeal to passion. It directed the jury to the character of the crime and to its duty.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Field (concurring in part): Field joins the majority on every point but one. He would hold that no sufficient foundation was laid for admitting Schofield's former testimony, and he reads the authorities the Chief Justice cites as establishing "the exact reverse."

Notes

  • Belief/conduct line: The protection covers belief and opinion. Overt acts against peace and good order may be regulated.
  • Framing of the question: The Court said the inquiry was not Congress's power to make criminal laws for the Territories. It concerned the guilt of one who knowingly violates a properly enacted law while believing it wrong. It did hold the statute within Congress's power as applied to the Territories and places under exclusive federal control.
  • "Unless restricted by some form of constitution": The marriage-regulation power is stated subject to that qualifier.
  • Left open: The Court declined to decide whether a preliminary finding that the defendant procured a witness's absence is wholly unreviewable, as Lord Morley's Case suggested. It held only that the finding has the effect of a jury verdict and stands unless the error is manifest.
  • Distinguished: Regina v. Wagstaff held parents who omitted medical care on religious grounds not guilty of manslaughter, while noting that starving a child would differ. The Court distinguished offenses consisting of a positive act knowingly done.
  • Relied on (territorial courts): American Insurance Co. v. Canter; Benner v. Porter; Clinton v. Englebrecht; Hornbuckle v. Toombs.
  • Relied on (juror bias): Coke, Bacon's Abridgment, and Marshall's rule in Burr's Trial distinguishing "light impressions" from "strong and deep impressions which close the mind." The Court also surveyed the differing state formulations of a disqualifying opinion: positive, decided and substantial, fixed, deliberate and settled.
  • Relied on (forfeiture by wrongdoing): Lord Morley's Case; Harrison's Case; Regina v. Scaife; Drayton v. Wells; Williams v. The State; Greenleaf, Taylor, and Wharton.
  • Relied on (marriage): Kent's Commentaries, both for the common-law voidness of a second marriage and for endorsing Lieber.
  • No precedent overruled.
  • Disposition nuance: The merits were affirmed on every point. The final judgment is a reversal and remand for one purpose, to re-enter the same sentence without hard labor, because the statute authorized imprisonment only. The error was not assigned but appeared on the face of the record.
