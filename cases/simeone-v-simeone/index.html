Black-Letter Rule

Prenuptial agreements are contracts and are judged by the same standards as other contracts. Absent fraud, misrepresentation, or duress, spouses are bound by their terms, and courts will not review whether the bargain was reasonable at signing or at divorce, whether a party understood the rights surrendered, or whether a party had independent counsel. The parties must still make a full and fair (though not exact) disclosure of their financial positions. A recital of full disclosure in the agreement creates a presumption of full disclosure, which a spouse alleging fraud or misrepresentation can rebut only by clear and convincing evidence.

Facts

  • Parties: At the 1975 marriage, wife was a 23-year-old unemployed nurse. Husband was a 39-year-old neurosurgeon earning about $90,000 a year with assets of about $300,000.
  • Signing: On the eve of the wedding, husband's attorney presented the agreement. Wife signed without counsel. Husband's attorney did not advise her about the rights she was giving up.
  • Terms: The agreement capped support at $200 per week on separation or divorce, with a $25,000 maximum. It expressly waived alimony pendente lite and recited that wife "has been informed and understands" that the statutory obligation "might, as a matter of law, exceed the amount provided." It recited that each party found it "fair, just and reasonable."
  • Disclosure: The agreement recited full disclosure and listed husband's assets at about $300,000, including a classic car collection valued at $200,000. Wife claimed the collection was understated by about $183,000. Husband's experts valued it at about $200,000, and the list included cars he only hoped to inherit.
  • Timing dispute: Wife testified she learned of the agreement only on the eve of the wedding. Husband and other witnesses testified to discussions over the prior six months. Husband's counsel testified that terms were revised before the eve of the wedding to increase wife's payments and that she showed no reluctance at signing.
  • Breakdown: The parties separated in 1982. Divorce proceedings began in 1984. Husband paid the full $25,000 between 1982 and 1984. In 1985, wife sought alimony pendente lite.
  • Rights at stake: Only alimony pendente lite was at issue. Alimony and equitable distribution arose under the Divorce Code of 1980, which by its terms does not affect earlier marital agreements (23 P.S. § 103).

Procedural Posture

A master upheld the agreement and denied alimony pendente lite. The Court of Common Pleas of Philadelphia County dismissed exceptions, and the Superior Court affirmed. The Supreme Court granted allowance of appeal to resolve the Superior Court's uncertainty over the plurality opinion in Estate of Geyer.

Issue

Is a prenuptial agreement enforceable, absent fraud, misrepresentation, or duress, where the financially weaker spouse signed without independent counsel and allegedly without understanding the statutory right she waived?

Holding

Yes. A prenuptial agreement is enforceable under ordinary contract principles when there is full and fair financial disclosure and no fraud, misrepresentation, or duress, without inquiry into reasonableness, understanding of rights, or access to counsel. Order affirmed (Flaherty, J., for the Court; Papadakos, J., concurring in the result only; McDermott, J., joined by Larsen, J., dissenting).

Reasoning

  • Geyer's foundation is gone. Geyer and earlier cases rested on an implicit presumption that spouses are unequal and that women cannot understand the contracts they sign. The Court called that presumption invalid, citing women's education, income, and assets and the equal-status principle reflected in Pa. Const. art. 1, § 28. It relied on Chief Justice Nix's Geyer dissent tracing the shift from paternalism to equal treatment.
  • Ordinary contract law applies. Weighing the parties' knowledge and the reasonableness of the bargain departs from contract law. Fraud, misrepresentation, and duress supply adequate remedies.
  • Failure to understand is no defense. Parties are bound whether or not they read or understood the terms (Standard Venetian Blind, Estate of Brant, Bollinger, Montgomery v. Levy). The agreement binds regardless of whether wife fully understood it. Ignorantia non excusat.
  • No independent-counsel requirement. A per se rule would contradict contract principles and paternalistically interfere with freedom of contract.
  • No reasonableness review at inception. Signing shows the parties viewed the deal as reasonable. Reasonableness inquiries undermine the reliability of agreements that parties might not have signed, or married, without expecting strict enforcement. Nearly any agreement can be attacked as unfair.
  • No reasonableness review at divorce. Illness, children, reliance on a spouse, career change, and financial gain or loss are foreseeable. Parties who do not address them assume the risk. Every marriage involves changed circumstances, so a divorce-time test would invite constant challenge.
  • Disclosure duty survives. Following Hillegass, parties stand in a relation of mutual confidence and trust and must disclose financial resources. Nondisclosure supports a claim of material misrepresentation in the inducement. Disclosure must be "full and fair," not exact (Kaufmann Estate). The Court treated this duty as consistent with contract principles.
  • Application, disclosure. The agreement recited full disclosure, so a presumption arose. Ample record support existed for the finding that wife failed to prove an understatement of the car collection by clear and convincing evidence.
  • Application, duress. The master found wife's duress claim not credible. Testimony showed months of prior discussion, revisions in her favor, and enough time to consult counsel (citing Carrier on duress).

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Papadakos, J. (concurring in the result): Adheres to Geyer and would apply its full-and-fair-disclosure and fairness standards, which this record satisfies. Refuses to join the majority's statements on women's equality as unnecessary, unrealistic, and outside the issues presented. Views prenuptial agreements as resembling contracts of adhesion, with the law protecting the subservient party of either sex.
  • McDermott, J., joined by Larsen, J. (dissenting): Would reverse and remand. Frames the issue as the solemnity of marriage, citing the legislative policy that "[t]he family is the basic unit of society" (23 P.S. § 102). Would require (1) full and fair financial disclosure, (2) awareness of statutory rights relinquished, per Geyer, and (3) fairness and equity, per Hillegass. Would let a spouse void an agreement on clear and convincing proof that it was unfair at inception, weighing the Hillegass factors (finances, ages, children, intelligence, standard of living), or became unfair by divorce, as with long-term economic dependence, a spouse's efforts increasing the other's property, unforeseen illness, or child care. Would remand so wife could try to prove that the bare words "alimony pendente lite" did not inform her of the right's nature and that the support terms were unfair.

Notes

  • Discarded: The Geyer approach permitting review of reasonableness and inquiry into whether parties understood the rights they surrendered. The Court also rejected the reasonableness requirement of Geyer "and earlier decisions" and declined to adopt the Superior Court's reading of Geyer.
  • *Retained from Hillegass:* The duty of full and fair financial disclosure, the confidential-relationship rationale, and the presumption of disclosure rebuttable by clear and convincing evidence.
  • Relied on: Gelb Estate (mutual confidence and trust); Kaufmann Estate (disclosure need not be exact); Standard Venetian Blind, Estate of Brant, Bollinger, Montgomery v. Levy (bound by signed terms); Carrier v. William Penn Broadcasting (duress); the Geyer dissents of Nix, C.J., and Flaherty, J.
  • Scope: Only alimony pendente lite was at issue. Divorce Code rights to alimony and equitable distribution did not exist in 1975, and no disclosure of nonexistent rights was required.
  • Defenses left intact: Fraud, misrepresentation, duress, and inadequate financial disclosure.
  • Factual findings: Disclosure and duress were resolved on credibility findings by the master, affirmed below for ample evidentiary support.
  • Majority status: Papadakos, J., concurred only in the result, so the majority's reasoning carried fewer joining justices than its judgment.
