Black-Letter Rule

A court may, in its discretion under Rule 60.02(6), vacate a settlement it approved on behalf of a minor when the minor had a serious injury that was not known or considered when the court approved the settlement. Relief is available without fraud, bad faith, or a mutual mistake when one party knew of the injury and knew that neither the court nor the minor's side knew of it. This holds even if no canon of ethics or legal obligation required that party's counsel to disclose the information.

Facts

  • Parties: David Spaulding, age 20 and a minor, was a passenger in a car driven by John Zimmerman that collided with a car owned by John Ledermann and driven by Florian Ledermann on August 24, 1956. David's father sued the defendants on David's behalf.
  • Plaintiff's doctors: David's family physician, Dr. Cain, diagnosed a crushed chest with multiple rib fractures, a severe concussion, and fractured clavicles. An orthopedist, Dr. Pohl, X-rayed the chest and reported that "the heart and aorta are normal." A neurologist, Dr. Blake, also found no aneurysm.
  • Defense exam: Dr. Hannah, a neurologist who examined David at the defendants' request, reported to Zimmerman's counsel on February 26, 1957, that David had an aneurysm of the aorta. He wrote that it could rupture and cause death, and that he could not say with certainty whether the accident caused it. Before settlement talks began, the report was shared with the Ledermanns' counsel.
  • Settlement: The case was called for trial on March 4, 1957. The next day the parties settled all claims for $6,500. Neither David nor his father knew of the aneurysm. Both believed David was recovering.
  • Court approval: David's counsel petitioned for court approval with affidavits from Drs. Cain and Blake. Nothing in the petition mentioned the aneurysm. The court approved the settlement on May 8, 1957, based on those papers.
  • Discovery of aneurysm: In 1959, during an army reserve physical, Dr. Cain found the aneurysm. He reviewed the post-accident X-rays and saw that they showed the beginning of it. Surgery followed on March 10, 1959.
  • Trial court findings: Defense counsel concealed what they knew, and that fact is undisputed. Defense counsel acted in good faith. The mistake was not mutual. Plaintiff's counsel failed to use the available discovery rules. No discussion of specific injuries took place between opposing counsel before the settlement. For purposes of the motion only, the aneurysm was causally related to the accident.

Procedural Posture

After reaching majority, David sued for additional damages and moved to vacate the earlier settlement. The district court vacated its approval order, the releases, the stipulation and order of dismissal, and the judgment. Defendants appealed.

Issue

Could the trial court, under Rule 60.02(6), vacate its approval of a minor's settlement where defense counsel knew of a serious, possibly accident-related injury that the minor, his counsel, his doctors, and the court did not know about?

Holding

Yes. A court may vacate a minor's settlement for a unilateral mistake where the opposing party knew of an injury that neither the court nor the minor's side knew of, so the settlement did not account for it, and the trial court did not abuse its discretion. Affirmed (opinion by Justice Thomas Gallagher; the opinion does not state the vote; Justice Rogosheske took no part).

Reasoning

  • Settled authority over minors' settlements: The court treated the principles as "well established." A court may vacate a minor's settlement it approved, even without fraud or bad faith, when the minor suffered separate and distinct injuries not known or considered at approval. A release that claims to cover unknown injuries does not prevent this.
  • Unilateral mistake is enough: The mistake need not be mutual. It is enough that one party had more knowledge and knew that neither the court nor the other side had it. The court quoted Keller v. Wolf: there must be "concealment or, at least, knowledge on the part of one party that the other party is laboring under a mistake," and "Equity will prevent one party from taking an unconscionable advantage of another's mistake for the purpose of enriching himself at the other's expense."
  • Application: Neither David, his counsel, nor his doctors knew of the aneurysm. Dr. Hannah's report showed how serious it was, including the risk of death. Counsel for both defendants knew about it and did not tell the court.
  • Ethics point (central for PR): The court said that "no canon of ethics or legal obligation may have required" defense counsel to tell plaintiff, plaintiff's counsel, or the court. It still found it "obvious to them" that the settlement did not cover the aneurysm. That knowledge "opened the way" for the court to use its discretion under Rule 60.02(6). Relief turns on the court's equitable power over minors' settlements. It does not depend on finding that counsel breached a duty.
  • Trial court's rationale: The supreme court quoted the trial court's memorandum at length without separately adopting each point. The trial court reasoned that no duty to disclose existed while the parties were adversaries. Once they agreed to settle, the approval petition became in effect a "joint application to the Court." At that point defendants' silence was a "calculated risk" that the settlement would hold. The trial court said it would likely have denied relief to an adult, leaving him to remedies against his own doctor and lawyer, "except for the character of the concealment in the light of plaintiff's minority."
  • Plaintiff's own nondisclosure rejected: Defendants argued that plaintiff's counsel also withheld information, namely Dr. Blake's letter recommending that the case not be settled for at least a year. The court found that Blake's affidavit, which was filed with the petition, disclosed the concussion and the possible permanent brain injury. All "essential and basic information" on that injury reached the court, so plaintiff was not estopped.
  • Insurance limits rejected: Defendants cited no authority. Minnesota cases hold that insurance limitations play no part in trying personal injury actions, and the court found those principles "equally applicable here." The record also did not show that insurance limits were disclosed to plaintiff's counsel or the court, or that the court considered them.

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Narrow ground: The holding rests on the court's discretionary power over settlements it approves on behalf of minors. The trial court expressly suggested it would likely have denied relief to an adult plaintiff.
  • Standard of review: Abuse of discretion. The supreme court held only that vacating was within the trial court's discretion.
  • Duty to disclose left open: The court did not hold that defense counsel had an ethical or legal duty to disclose. It said no canon or legal obligation "may have required" disclosure and decided the case without resolving that.
  • Expressly left open: "Without determining whether a minor would be thus bound by his counsel's actions," the court declined to decide whether a minor is bound by his own counsel's nondisclosure. It disposed of that argument on the facts.
  • Rule 60.02 timing: The court grounded relief in Rule 60.02(6). As quoted in the opinion, the rule's one-year limit applies to reasons (1) through (3), and reason (6) requires only "a reasonable time." The opinion does not otherwise discuss defendants' timeliness argument.
  • Relied on: Larson v. Stowe, Wilson v. Davidson, and Dasich v. La Rue Min. Co. (vacating minors' settlements for unknown, separate injuries without fraud). Larson v. Stowe also holds that a release covering unknown injuries does not bar relief. Keller v. Wolf supplies the unilateral mistake and unconscionable advantage standard.
  • Relied on by analogy: Jeddeloh v. Hockenhull, Lee v. Osmundson, and Brown v. Murphy Transfer & Storage Co. (insurance limits have no part in trying personal injury actions).
  • Overruled or distinguished: None identified in the opinion.
