Black-Letter Rule

Under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, a parent, including an unwed father who has sired and raised his children, is entitled to a hearing on his fitness before the State removes his children from his custody. A State may not substitute a conclusive presumption of unfitness based on marital status for an individualized determination merely because presuming is cheaper and easier than proving. Granting fitness hearings to married parents and unwed mothers while denying them to unwed fathers violates the Equal Protection Clause.

Facts

  • Parties: Peter Stanley, an unwed father, against the State of Illinois.
  • Relationship: Peter and Joan Stanley lived together intermittently for 18 years and had three children. Only two children were involved in the litigation.
  • Undisputed record: Stanley is the father, lived with the two children all their lives, and supported them. Nothing in the record showed he was a neglectful father.
  • Statute: The Illinois Juvenile Court Act defined "parents" as "the father and mother of a legitimate child, or the survivor of them, or the natural mother of an illegitimate child, and includes any adoptive parent." Unwed fathers were excluded.
  • Two removal routes: In a neglect proceeding, the State had to give notice and a hearing and prove unfitness. In a dependency proceeding, the State only had to show the child had no surviving parent or guardian. Because an unwed father was not a "parent," his children could be taken through the dependency route.
  • Removal: When Joan died, the State brought a dependency proceeding. The children were declared wards of the State and placed with court-appointed guardians. Stanley's fitness was never assessed.

Procedural Posture

In a dependency proceeding brought by the State, the children were declared wards and placed with court-appointed guardians. The Illinois Supreme Court accepted that Stanley's unfitness had not been established but rejected his equal protection claim, holding that proof he and the mother were unmarried was enough; the U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does a State violate the Fourteenth Amendment when it removes an unwed father's children upon the mother's death without a hearing on his fitness, while giving married parents and unwed mothers such a hearing?

Holding

Yes; as a matter of due process, Stanley was entitled to a hearing on his fitness as a parent before his children were taken, and denying him that hearing while extending it to all other parents denied him equal protection, so the judgment of the Illinois Supreme Court is reversed and the case remanded (opinion by Justice White; Justice Douglas joined Parts I and II only; Justices Powell and Rehnquist did not participate).

Reasoning

  • The case is not moot or harmless (Part I). The Court rejected the argument that Stanley could undo the harm through adoption or a petition for "custody and control." A wrong is not permissible just because it might later be undone (cf. Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp.). Delay itself injures the father and the children.
  • Adoption is no remedy. Illinois treats Stanley as a stranger, gives him no adoption priority, and would make him prove he is the most suitable of all applicants. Officials in the case signaled that an unmarried, impecunious father could not expect to win.
  • Custody and control is no remedy. A guardian can be removed without the cause required in a neglect proceeding, cannot leave the jurisdiction without court approval, and may have to report to the court. Legal custody is not parenthood.
  • Due process framework (Part II). Due process does not demand a hearing in every case. The required procedure turns on the nature of the government function and the private interest affected (Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy; Goldberg v. Kelly).
  • The private interest is substantial. A man's interest in children "he has sired and raised" warrants deference and, absent a powerful countervailing interest, protection. The Court drew on family-rights cases: Meyer v. Nebraska, Skinner v. Oklahoma, May v. Anderson, Prince v. Massachusetts, Griswold v. Connecticut (Goldberg, J., concurring), and Kovacs v. Cooper (Frankfurter, J., concurring).
  • Family bonds outside marriage count. Levy v. Louisiana and Glona v. American Guarantee Co. show the law recognizes family relationships not legitimized by marriage. Stanley's interest is "cognizable and substantial."
  • The State's ends are legitimate; its means are not. The Act aims to protect the child's welfare and to "strengthen the minor's family ties whenever possible." Separating children from a fit parent advances none of these goals. If Stanley is fit, the State "spites its own articulated goals."
  • *Analogy to Bell v. Burson.* Georgia could not suspend a license without considering fault, the very factor its own scheme made central. Illinois likewise removes children without considering fitness, the factor its own scheme deems central.
  • *Analogy to Carrington v. Rash.* Even if most members of a class fail a requirement, a State may not impose a conclusive, unrebuttable presumption against all of them when an individualized inquiry is available. Some unwed fathers are fit, as Illinois conceded.
  • Administrative convenience is insufficient. "The Constitution recognizes higher values than speed and efficiency." Procedure by presumption that "forecloses the determinative issues of competence and care" and "disdains present realities in deference to past formalities" cannot stand when the issue is "the dismemberment of his family." See also Reed v. Reed (cited in a footnote).
  • Cost is minimal. In a footnote, the Court observed that uninterested fathers will not demand hearings, and interested ones would eventually get a hearing in a custody or adoption proceeding anyway. Illinois already provides notice by personal service, certified mail, or publication.
  • Equal protection (Part III). Because all Illinois parents are constitutionally entitled to a fitness hearing before removal, denying it to unwed fathers while granting it to other parents is "inescapably contrary to the Equal Protection Clause."
  • Jurisdictional footnote. The Court said it decided the case on the equal protection premise raised below, using analysis "readily available to the state court." It distinguished Picard v. Connor, where an equal protection ground was never presented to the state courts, and said Cardinale v. Louisiana and Hill v. California were observed.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Chief Justice Burger, joined by Justice Blackmun, dissenting.
  • Jurisdiction: Only an equal protection claim was raised and decided below. The Court decided a due process question sua sponte, contrary to 28 U.S.C. § 1257, State Farm Mutual Automobile Ins. Co. v. Duel, and Hill v. California. Using equal protection to import a due process right is "a shorthand condensation of the entire Constitution."
  • No presumption exists: The Act contains no presumption that unwed fathers are unfit, and the State never made the arguments the majority "answered." The juvenile judge indicated Stanley's acknowledged paternity would help him in later adoption or guardianship proceedings.
  • Equal protection merits: Illinois may give full recognition only to father-child relationships formed through marriage or adoption. Marriage is essentially contractual and carries enforceable duties; Stanley "did not seek the burdens when he could have freely assumed them." Unwed mothers are readily identifiable, and unwed fathers often are not. The dissent also asserted that mothers' biological role creates stronger bonds and that unwed mothers are generally more dependable protectors. These generalizations suffice for a classification aimed at child welfare under the State's parens patriae role, even if Stanley is an exception.
  • Stanley's conduct: After the mother died, Stanley placed the children with Mr. and Mrs. Ness and sought neither adoption nor guardianship. At the hearing he asked only that no one else receive legal responsibility, and seemed concerned about losing welfare payments. Counsel also disclosed that the oldest child had earlier been made a ward through a neglect proceeding proven against Stanley.
  • Warning: The majority embarks on "a novel concept of the natural law for unwed fathers that could well have strange boundaries as yet undiscernible."

Notes

  • Scope of the protected interest: The Court described the interest as that "of a man in the children he has sired and raised." The record showed Stanley lived with and supported the children. The opinion does not define what interest a father who never lived with or supported his children would hold, but it does speak to uninterested fathers: in a footnote it observed that unwed fathers who "do not care about the disposition of their children" will not appear to demand hearings, and that those who do not promptly respond to notice cannot complain if their children are declared wards.
  • Not challenged: The State's power and duty to remove children through neglect proceedings. The Court did not question that neglectful parents may be separated from their children.
  • Remand: The Court did not award Stanley custody. It reversed and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion. The Court said Stanley "may" be found unfit.
  • Burden and notice (footnote): Unwed fathers who do not promptly respond to notice "cannot complain if their children are declared wards of the State." Those who respond "retain the burden of proving their fatherhood."
  • Split on grounds: Justice Douglas joined Parts I and II (the due process analysis) but not Part III (equal protection).
  • Precedents relied on: Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy; Goldberg v. Kelly; Kovacs v. Cooper; Meyer v. Nebraska; Skinner v. Oklahoma; May v. Anderson; Prince v. Massachusetts; Griswold v. Connecticut; Levy v. Louisiana; Glona v. American Guarantee Co.; Bell v. Burson; Carrington v. Rash; cf. Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp.; cf. Reed v. Reed; In re Mark T. (Mich. App., cited in a footnote).
  • Precedents distinguished: Picard v. Connor (claim there not raised below). The Court said Cardinale v. Louisiana and Hill v. California were observed.
  • Overruled: None.
