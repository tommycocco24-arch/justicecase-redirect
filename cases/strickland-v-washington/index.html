Black-Letter Rule

A defendant claiming actual ineffective assistance of counsel under the Sixth Amendment must show (1) deficient performance, meaning counsel's representation fell below an objective standard of reasonableness under prevailing professional norms, judged with strong deference and from counsel's perspective at the time, and (2) prejudice, meaning a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different. A reasonable probability is a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome. Prejudice is assessed under the governing legal standard, assuming a decisionmaker who acts lawfully and is reasonably, conscientiously, and impartially applying that standard. For a death sentence, the question is whether there is a reasonable probability the sentencer would have concluded that the balance of aggravating and mitigating circumstances did not warrant death. Failure on either prong defeats the claim.

Facts

  • Crimes and plea: Over 10 days in September 1976, respondent committed three groups of crimes, including three stabbing murders. After his accomplices were arrested, respondent surrendered and voluntarily confessed to the third criminal episode, and the State then appointed an experienced criminal lawyer. Later, against counsel's specific advice, respondent also confessed to the first two murders. Again acting against counsel's advice, respondent waived a jury trial and pleaded guilty to all charges.
  • Plea colloquy: Respondent told the judge he had no significant prior record, was under extreme stress from being unable to support his family, and accepted responsibility. The judge said he respected people who admit responsibility but made no statement about sentencing.
  • Sentencing waiver: Against counsel's advice, respondent waived the advisory jury at sentencing.
  • Counsel's preparation: Counsel, an experienced criminal lawyer, felt a sense of hopelessness after learning of the additional confessions. Counsel spoke with respondent and by phone with respondent's wife and mother, sought no other character witnesses, requested no psychiatric exam, and requested no presentence report.
  • Counsel's strategy: Counsel relied on the plea colloquy for evidence of background and emotional stress. This kept the State from cross-examining respondent or offering its own psychiatric evidence. Counsel also excluded respondent's "rap sheet." At sentencing, counsel argued remorse, acceptance of responsibility, no significant criminal history, and extreme emotional disturbance, knowing the judge's reputation for valuing a defendant who owned up to his crimes.
  • Sentence: The judge found numerous aggravating circumstances for each murder and no (or one comparatively insignificant) mitigating circumstance, and imposed death on each murder count.
  • Collateral evidence: Respondent later offered 14 affidavits from friends, neighbors, and relatives willing to testify, plus psychiatric and psychological reports finding respondent "chronically frustrated and depressed" over finances but not under extreme mental or emotional disturbance.

Procedural Posture

The Florida courts denied collateral relief without an evidentiary hearing, and the federal District Court denied habeas after a hearing, finding no prejudice. The en banc Eleventh Circuit (Unit B of the former Fifth Circuit) announced its own ineffectiveness framework, reversed, and remanded for new factfinding; Florida officials petitioned for certiorari.

Issue

Did counsel's failure to investigate and present more character and psychological evidence at respondent's capital sentencing deny respondent the effective assistance of counsel guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment?

Holding

No; under the two-part test of deficient performance and resulting prejudice, counsel's conduct was reasonable professional judgment, and even if it were not, there was no reasonable probability the omitted evidence would have changed the sentence, so the judgment of the Court of Appeals was reversed (opinion by Justice O'Connor).

Reasoning

  • Purpose of the right: The Sixth Amendment right to counsel exists to protect the right to a fair trial. A lawyer's mere presence is not enough. "[T]he right to counsel is the right to the effective assistance of counsel" (McMann v. Richardson).
  • Benchmark: Any ineffectiveness claim turns on whether counsel's conduct so undermined the adversarial process that the trial cannot be relied on as having produced a just result.
  • Capital sentencing treated like trial: Florida's capital sentencing proceeding is sufficiently like a trial in its adversarial format and decision standards that counsel's duties there need not be distinguished from those at trial (Barclay v. Florida; Bullington v. Missouri).
  • Performance prong:
  • Standard is "reasonably effective assistance," measured by "reasonableness under prevailing professional norms." The Court noted all federal courts of appeals had adopted it.
  • Counsel's basic duties: loyalty and avoiding conflicts of interest; advocating the defendant's cause; consulting on important decisions; keeping the defendant informed; bringing skill and knowledge to make the trial a reliable adversarial test.
  • These duties are not a checklist. ABA Standards for Criminal Justice ("The Defense Function") are "guides" to reasonableness, "but they are only guides."
  • Detailed rules would interfere with counsel's constitutionally protected independence, restrict tactical latitude, and distract from vigorous advocacy. The guarantee's purpose is to ensure a fair trial; improving the quality of legal representation is an important goal but not its purpose.
  • Review must be "highly deferential." Courts must avoid hindsight, reconstruct counsel's circumstances, and indulge a strong presumption that the conduct "might be considered sound trial strategy" (Michel v. Louisiana).
  • The defendant must identify specific acts or omissions; the court asks whether, in light of all the circumstances, they fell outside the wide range of professionally competent assistance.
  • Duty to investigate:
  • Strategic choices made after thorough investigation of law and facts are "virtually unchallengeable."
  • Choices made after less than complete investigation are reasonable to the extent reasonable professional judgment supports the limits on investigation.
  • Counsel must make reasonable investigations or make a reasonable decision that makes particular investigations unnecessary.
  • The defendant's own statements and choices can determine or substantially influence what investigation is reasonable.
  • Prejudice prong:
  • Even unreasonable errors do not justify setting aside a judgment if they had no effect on it (United States v. Morrison).
  • The government cannot prevent attorney errors, and errors are as likely harmless as prejudicial and cannot be categorized in advance, so the defendant must affirmatively prove prejudice.
  • "Some conceivable effect" is too low; almost any error meets it. Respondent's "impaired the presentation of the defense" test is unworkable.
  • "More likely than not altered the outcome" is too high. It borrows from the newly-discovered-evidence standard, which presupposes an otherwise fair proceeding; an ineffectiveness claim alleges the absence of a crucial assurance of reliability, so finality concerns are weaker.
  • The chosen test draws on the materiality standards in United States v. Agurs (undisclosed exculpatory evidence) and United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal (deported witness).
  • The court assumes a lawful decisionmaker reasonably, conscientiously, and impartially applying the governing standards. No entitlement to "the luck of a lawless decisionmaker." Evidence of a particular judge's sentencing practices or actual decision process outside the record is irrelevant to prejudice.
  • The governing legal standard defines the prejudice question. For a conviction: reasonable probability the factfinder would have had a reasonable doubt respecting guilt. For a death sentence: reasonable probability the sentencer (including a reweighing appellate court) would have concluded that the balance of aggravating and mitigating circumstances did not warrant death.
  • The court considers the totality of evidence; a result weakly supported by the record is more likely affected by error than one with overwhelming support.
  • Application, performance: Counsel made a reasonable strategic choice to argue emotional distress and acceptance of responsibility. Nothing in the record shows counsel's hopelessness distorted professional judgment. Limiting character evidence to the plea colloquy kept out contrary psychological evidence and the rap sheet.
  • Application, prejudice: The omitted evidence "would barely have altered the sentencing profile." Given overwhelming aggravating factors, there was no reasonable probability of a different sentence, and the evidence might have hurt by bringing in the rap sheet and contradicting the extreme-disturbance claim. Respondent showed a "double failure."

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Brennan (concurring in part, dissenting in part): Joins the Court's opinion, finding the standards precise enough to separate constitutional violations from lesser derelictions yet flexible enough for case-by-case development. Stresses that the standards should be applied with special care in capital sentencing, where counsel's duty to investigate mitigating evidence "takes on supreme importance." Notes that counsel's incompetence can be so serious it becomes constructive denial of counsel requiring no prejudice showing (citing Cronic). Dissents from the judgment because of his view that the death penalty is always cruel and unusual punishment; would vacate the death sentence and remand.
  • Justice Marshall (dissenting): Joins neither the opinion nor the judgment.
  • Performance: "Reasonableness" is so malleable it either has no grip or invites inconsistent application. It leaves open whether the benchmark is retained or appointed counsel and whether it varies by locale. Many defense tasks (trial preparation, bail, client conferences, timely objections, notices of appeal) could be governed by uniform standards. Refusing to develop them will "stunt the development of constitutional doctrine."
  • Prejudice: Should not be required at all. Prejudice is hard to gauge from a cold record, and evidence of injury may be missing because of counsel's incompetence. The guarantee also secures fundamentally fair procedures, so even a manifestly guilty defendant is entitled to able advocacy. Deficient performance alone should require a new trial (citing Chapman v. California).
  • Presumption: "Strong presumption" and "heavy measure of deference" may impose an unusually weighty burden and covertly legitimate convictions obtained through incompetent lawyering.
  • Prior claims: Rejects the majority's suggestion that its standards rarely change outcomes; lower courts must reassess claims rejected under more tolerant standards.
  • Capital cases: Standards should apply especially stringently. A capital defendant should need only a "significant chance" of a different outcome.
  • Application: Counsel made virtually no investigation of character witnesses. A strategic choice not to present such evidence might be defensible only after assessing it. Failure to investigate at all was unreasonable, especially given counsel's admitted hopelessness. Would grant a new sentencing proceeding.

Notes

  • Scope: Governs claims of "actual ineffectiveness" based on counsel's specific errors. Applies to trials and to capital sentencing proceedings like Florida's.
  • Left open: The Court did not consider counsel's role in an ordinary (non-capital) sentencing, which may require a different approach. It did not decide the general admissibility of the trial judge's testimony at the habeas hearing, though such testimony is irrelevant to prejudice.
  • Presumed-prejudice exceptions (relied on):
  • Actual or constructive denial of counsel and various kinds of state interference with counsel: prejudice presumed (United States v. Cronic).
  • Government interference examples: Geders (bar on overnight consultation), Herring (bar on summation at bench trial), Brooks (defendant must testify first), Ferguson (bar on direct examination of defendant).
  • Actual conflict of interest: a more limited presumption. Prejudice is presumed only if counsel "actively represented conflicting interests" and "an actual conflict of interest adversely affected his lawyer's performance" (Cuyler v. Sullivan). Conflicts breach the duty of loyalty, "perhaps the most basic of counsel's duties."
  • Order of analysis: Courts need not address both prongs or proceed in any order. If a claim is easier to dispose of for lack of prejudice, that course should be followed; the object is not to grade counsel.
  • Habeas and collateral review: The same standards apply on direct appeal, new-trial motions, and federal collateral review. Both prongs are mixed questions of law and fact. A state court's conclusion of effective assistance is not a binding finding of fact under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), though underlying state factual findings get § 2254(d) deference (Townsend v. Sain; Cuyler).
  • Exhaustion: The rule requiring dismissal of mixed petitions is to be strictly enforced but is not jurisdictional (Rose v. Lundy).
  • Effect on prior cases: Differences among lower-court performance formulations are insignificant. Only the strict outcome-determinative prejudice test is heavier than the Court's test, and that difference should matter "only in the rarest case." Prior rejected claims need not be reconsidered to the extent fundamental fairness was already the guiding inquiry.
  • Lower-court standards treated: Agrees with the Eleventh Circuit's description of strategic choices after thorough or limited investigation. Notes that the Eleventh Circuit expressly rejected the prejudice standard from Judge Leventhal's plurality in United States v. Decoster, adopted in Florida's Knight v. State, which requires showing deficient conduct was likely to have affected the outcome. The Court itself rejects a requirement that deficient conduct more likely than not altered the outcome, without expressly tying that rejection to Decoster or Knight. Relies on Decoster for counsel's tactical latitude and the relevance of client conversations.
  • Evidentiary hearing: The prejudice question was resolvable without the District Court hearing evidence; the state courts properly rejected the claim without an evidentiary hearing.
  • PR angle: The opinion lists core duties of defense counsel (loyalty, avoiding conflicts, advocacy, consultation, keeping the client informed, skill and knowledge). It treats ABA standards as guides only and says the constitutional floor exists to ensure a fair trial, not to raise the quality of representation. It warns that intrusive review could dampen defense counsel's ardor, discourage acceptance of assigned cases, and undermine attorney-client trust.
