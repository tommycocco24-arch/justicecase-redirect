Black-Letter Rule

A legal malpractice plaintiff must show (1) an attorney-client relationship, (2) negligence or breach of contract by the attorney, (3) proximate cause of damages, and (4) that but for the attorney's conduct the plaintiff would have succeeded on the underlying claim. An attorney-client relationship can arise, under either a tort or a contract theory, when a person seeks and receives legal advice from a lawyer under circumstances that make it reasonably foreseeable to the lawyer that the person will be injured if the advice is negligently given. A lawyer who gives an opinion on the merits of a medical malpractice claim without the minimal research an ordinarily prudent attorney would do, or without mentioning a soon-to-expire limitations period, may be found negligent.

Facts

  • Underlying injury: John Togstad was treated for an aneurism with a Selverstone clamp on his carotid artery. While the clamp was 50% closed, he lost the ability to speak or move. A resident did not adjust the clamp, and Dr. Blake opened it about an hour later. Togstad was left severely paralyzed on his right side and unable to speak.
  • Competing medical theories: Plaintiffs' expert attributed the injury to inadequate blood flow from the clamp and to negligence by Dr. Blake and the hospital. Dr. Blake and defendants' expert attributed it to blood clots and said a 50% closed clamp does not affect blood flow.
  • The consultation: About 14 months after the hospitalization began, Joan Togstad met with attorney Jerre Miller for 45 minutes to an hour. She described what happened at the hospital, including nurses' statements that raised her suspicions. Miller took notes and asked questions. She brought no records.
  • Mrs. Togstad's version: Miller said he "did not think we had a legal case" but would discuss it with his partner. When she heard nothing, she concluded there was no case. She denied that Miller mentioned lack of expertise, urged her to see another lawyer, or mentioned the two-year limitations period. She did not see another lawyer for a year, relying on Miller's advice.
  • Miller's version: He said only that nothing she related showed a case the firm would be interested in taking, that this was only his opinion, that she should promptly get another opinion, and that the firm was not expert in medical malpractice. He said he consulted attorney Charles Hvass, who he believed saw no liability.
  • No formalities: No fee arrangement was discussed, no medical authorizations were requested, and Mrs. Togstad was not billed.
  • Expert testimony on standard of care: Plaintiffs' expert Green said the minimum before advising on a medical malpractice claim is to get authorizations, review hospital records, and consult a medical expert. Defense expert McNulty testified that declining a case only requires saying so, but that when a lawyer gives a legal opinion on the merits of a medical malpractice claim, community standards require checking hospital records and consulting an expert first. Defense expert Hvass conceded that ordinary care would require telling someone about the two-year limitations period 14 months after the incident.
  • Verdict: By special verdict, the jury found Dr. Blake negligent and a direct cause of the injuries, an attorney-client contractual relationship between Mrs. Togstad and Miller, Miller negligent in rendering advice, that plaintiffs would have won against Dr. Blake but for Miller's negligence, and no negligence by the Togstads. It awarded $610,500 to Mr. Togstad and $39,000 to Mrs. Togstad.

Procedural Posture

After a jury verdict for plaintiffs in Hennepin County District Court, the trial court denied defendants' motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict or, alternatively, a new trial. Miller and his firm appealed.

Issue

Can a lawyer who, in a single unpaid consultation, tells a prospective client she has no case be held liable for legal malpractice on the theory that an attorney-client relationship arose from that advice?

Holding

Yes; under either a tort or a contract analysis, a jury could find an attorney-client relationship where Mrs. Togstad sought and received legal advice from Miller under circumstances making it reasonably foreseeable that she would be injured by negligent advice, and the record supported the findings of negligence, causation, and damages, so the judgment was affirmed (per curiam; no vote is stated).

Reasoning

  • Elements. The court set out the four elements of legal malpractice in a case "of the type involved here," citing Christy v. Saliterman.
  • Attorney-client relationship: precedent. Ryan v. Long found a relationship where the plaintiff called on the defendant for "legal advice" and the defendant "assumed to give him a professional opinion," without saying whether that rested on contract or tort. More recent cases, Ronnigen v. Hertogs and Christy, used contract terms. Ronnigen affirmed a directed verdict for an attorney because the evidence showed no express or implied promise to represent the plaintiff. The trial court here applied the contract approach. A Minnesota Law Review note argued a negligence analysis fits better.
  • Attorney-client relationship: no choice of theory needed. The court found the tort and contract analyses "very similar" on these facts and held the evidence sufficient under either. It assumed Mrs. Togstad's testimony was true, as required on review (Cofran v. Swanman: view evidence and inferences most favorably to the prevailing party).
  • The two tests (footnote).
  • Tort: the lawyer rendered legal advice, "not necessarily at someone's request," under circumstances making it reasonably foreseeable that negligent advice might injure the recipient (citing Palsgraf). Alternatively, a relationship exists whenever a person "seeks and receives legal advice from an attorney in circumstances in which a reasonable person would rely on such advice" (quoting the law review note).
  • Contract: the advice must be rendered at another's request. Where the advice was unpaid, reliance must be shown through promissory estoppel (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90).
  • Key facts supporting the relationship. Mrs. Togstad went for legal advice, was told there was no case, relied on it, and received no qualification urging another opinion or disclosing lack of expertise.
  • Negligence: error of judgment rejected. Defendants invoked Meagher v. Kavli to argue Miller made at most a non-actionable error of judgment. The court said this case involved no mere error of judgment. The claim was that Miller failed to perform the minimal research an ordinarily prudent attorney would do before advising, and Green's and McNulty's testimony supported it.
  • Negligence: limitations period. Defendants sought an instruction that failure to mention the two-year limitations period could not be negligence, arguing under Namchek v. Tulley that a new trial was needed because the jury's theory of negligence was unclear. The court held the instruction was properly refused because Hvass's testimony, combined with Mrs. Togstad's, let a jury find negligence on that ground too.
  • Causation and the limitations period. Defendants argued the limitations period may not have run when Mrs. Togstad saw another lawyer, if it ran from the last treatment date (Swang v. Hauser; Schmidt v. Esser) instead of the single-act date. The court rejected this for two reasons:
  • Defendants assumed at trial the period began on the incident date and could not raise the issue for the first time on appeal (Turner v. Alpha Phi Sorority House; Greer v. Kooiker).
  • Even using the later date, a jury could infer the period had expired from her testimony that she saw a lawyer a year later and from the fact that no suit was brought against Dr. Blake or the hospital. The court called this evidence "weak" but a prima facie showing that defendants had to rebut.
  • Case within a case. Dr. Woods's testimony supported the finding that plaintiffs would have won against Dr. Blake. The jury was entitled to disbelieve the contrary defense experts.
  • Consortium damages. Excessiveness review is for abuse of discretion (Bigham v. J. C. Penney Co.), with no interference unless refusal would be "shocking" and a "plain injustice" (Dawydowycz v. Quady). Consortium includes comfort, companionship, and sexual relationship (Thill v. Modern Erecting Co.). Evidence of Mr. Togstad's impotence and that his injuries precipitated the divorce supported $39,000.
  • No deduction for hypothetical attorney fees. Christy had declined to decide the question but noted the parties likely assumed the hypothetical fee was canceled out by fees spent suing the attorney. Other jurisdictions split. Cases allowing the deduction gave no detailed reasoning. The court adopted the reasoning of cases disallowing it (Duncan v. Lord; Winter v. Brown; Benard v. Walkup), which rely on the plaintiff's expense in suing the attorney.
  • Closing argument. Rule 49 lets counsel comment only on the effect of comparative-negligence percentage answers. Counsel's remark on causation might have violated the rule if it disclosed the effect of the "direct cause" answers. Given the ambiguity, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying a new trial (Patterson v. Donahue).

Concurrences & Dissents

None in the opinion text.

Notes

  • Left open: The court expressly declined to decide whether tort or contract theory governs the attorney-client relationship question.
  • Standard of review drives the result. The court assumed Mrs. Togstad's testimony was true. Miller's contrary account (urging a prompt second opinion, disclaiming expertise) was rejected by the jury and not credited on appeal.
  • Scope of the elements. The four-element test is stated for "a legal malpractice action of the type involved here," where the claimed loss is an underlying medical malpractice claim.
  • Declining a case vs. advising on the merits. McNulty's testimony drew this line: refusing a case requires only saying so, while a legal opinion on the merits of a medical malpractice claim requires checking records and consulting an expert. The jury found Miller gave advice.
  • Limitations causation issue: Decided partly on waiver. The court called the evidence that the period had run "weak."
  • Rule 49: The court did not decide whether counsel's causation remark violated the rule.
  • Relied on: Christy v. Saliterman (elements; attorney-fee dicta); Ryan v. Long (relationship from seeking and receiving legal advice); Cofran v. Swanman (review standard); Palsgraf (foreseeability, in footnote); Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90 (promissory estoppel, in footnote); 63 Minn. L. Rev. 751 (tort formulation); Swang v. Hauser and Schmidt v. Esser (limitations accrual); Turner and Greer (no new issues on appeal); Bigham, Dawydowycz, Thill (damages); Patterson v. Donahue (new-trial discretion); Duncan, Winter, Benard (no fee deduction).
  • Discussed without overruling: Ronnigen v. Hertogs (contract approach; directed verdict for attorney where no promise to represent).
  • Rejected as applied: Meagher v. Kavli (error-of-judgment defense) and Namchek v. Tulley (new trial where jury's negligence theory unclear).
  • Out-of-state authority rejected: McGlone v. Lacey, Sitton v. Clements, Childs v. Comstock (allowing fee deduction without detailed reasoning).
  • Overruled: None.
