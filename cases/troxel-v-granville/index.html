Black-Letter Rule

The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects the fundamental right of parents to make decisions concerning the care, custody, and control of their children. Fit parents are presumed to act in their children's best interests, so when a fit parent's visitation decision is subject to judicial review, the court must accord at least some special weight to the parent's own determination. A nonparental visitation statute of sweeping breadth is unconstitutional as applied where the trial court gives a fit parent's decision no special weight and the order rests on nothing more than a simple disagreement with the parent about the child's best interests. The plurality did not define the precise scope of the parental right in the visitation context.

Facts

  • Statute: Wash. Rev. Code §26.10.160(3) let "[a]ny person" petition for visitation "at any time" and let courts order visitation whenever "visitation may serve the best interest of the child," whether or not circumstances had changed.
  • Parties: Tommie Granville and Brad Troxel never married and had two daughters, Isabelle and Natalie. Jenifer and Gary Troxel are Brad's parents, the girls' paternal grandparents.
  • Background: After the 1991 separation, Brad lived with his parents and regularly brought the girls there for weekend visits. Brad committed suicide in May 1993.
  • The dispute: In October 1993, Granville told the Troxels she wanted to limit their visits to one short visit per month. She never sought to cut off visitation entirely.
  • Competing requests: The Troxels asked for two overnight weekends per month and two weeks each summer. Granville asked for one day per month with no overnight stay, plus holiday participation.
  • Trial court order: One weekend per month, one week in the summer, and four hours on each grandparent's birthday.
  • Trial court reasoning: The judge said it is "normally in the best interest of the children to spend quality time with the grandparent" unless the grandparents' lifestyles would "impact adversely" on the children, and that he had not "been shown it is not in [the] best interest of the children." He based the summer week partly on his own childhood experience.
  • Findings on remand: The Troxels are part of "a large, central, loving family" who can offer "opportunities for the children in the areas of cousins and music," and the children would benefit from time with them if balanced with time with their nuclear family.
  • Fitness: The Troxels never alleged, and no court found, that Granville was unfit.
  • Later events: Granville married Kelly Wynn, who formally adopted the girls.

Procedural Posture

The Washington Superior Court granted visitation, and the Washington Court of Appeals reversed on the statutory ground that nonparents lack standing absent a pending custody action. The Washington Supreme Court found standing under the statute's plain language but affirmed on federal constitutional grounds, holding the statute facially invalid because it required no threshold showing of harm and swept too broadly; the U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari and affirmed.

Issue

Does Wash. Rev. Code §26.10.160(3), as applied to Granville and her family, violate her Fourteenth Amendment due process right to make decisions concerning the care, custody, and control of her daughters?

Holding

Yes. Resting on the "sweeping breadth" of §26.10.160(3) together with the trial court's application of it, the plurality held the statute unconstitutional as applied to Granville and her family. The application featured a combination of factors: no allegation or finding that Granville was unfit; no special weight given to her determination, with the court instead applying a presumption in favor of grandparent visitation; no weight given to her having already offered meaningful visitation; and only slender findings. Taken together, these showed that the order reflected nothing more than a simple disagreement between the court and Granville about her children's best interests, and the order violated her substantive due process right. The judgment of the Washington Supreme Court is affirmed (plurality opinion by Justice O'Connor, joined by the Chief Justice, Justice Ginsburg, and Justice Breyer; Justices Souter and Thomas concurred in the judgment; Justices Stevens, Scalia, and Kennedy dissented).

Reasoning

  • Substantive due process framework: The Due Process Clause "guarantees more than fair process" and gives heightened protection against government interference with certain fundamental rights (Washington v. Glucksberg; Reno v. Flores).
  • The parental right is fundamental: The interest of parents in the care, custody, and control of their children is "perhaps the oldest of the fundamental liberty interests" the Court has recognized. The plurality traces it through Meyer v. Nebraska, Pierce v. Society of Sisters, Prince v. Massachusetts, Stanley v. Illinois, Wisconsin v. Yoder, Quilloin v. Walcott, Parham v. J. R., Santosky v. Kramer, and Glucksberg. Given this precedent, the right "cannot now be doubted."
  • The statute is "breathtakingly broad": It lets any third party subject any parental visitation decision to court review. Once a petition is filed, the parent's decision gets no deference, no presumption of validity, and no weight at all. The judge's view of best interests "necessarily prevails." The Washington Supreme Court declined to read the statute more narrowly.
  • Application: a combination of factors. The plurality held the order unconstitutional based on several factors taken together:
  • No allegation or finding of unfitness. Under Parham, fit parents are presumed to act in their children's best interests. So long as a parent is fit, there is normally no reason for the State to inject itself into the family to question that parent's child-rearing decisions.
  • No special weight; the opposite presumption. The trial court gave Granville's view no special weight. It effectively presumed in favor of grandparent visitation and placed on Granville the burden of disproving that visitation served the children's best interests. That framework "directly contravened" the traditional presumption favoring fit parents.
  • Granville never cut off visitation. She had offered meaningful visitation before any suit was filed. The court gave this no weight and simply split the difference between the parties.
  • Slender findings. The two formal findings (loving extended family; benefit of quality time) and the judge's reliance on his own childhood showed only "a simple disagreement" between the court and the parent.
  • Core principle: Whether an intergenerational relationship would benefit a child is "for the parent to make in the first instance." The Due Process Clause does not let a State infringe the parental right "simply because a state judge believes a 'better' decision could be made."
  • Comparison to other states: The plurality cited statutes that build in parental protection as contrasts: rebuttable presumptions that a parent's refusal is reasonable or that visitation is not in the child's best interest (California, Rhode Island, Utah); requirements that visitation not interfere with the parent-child relationship (Maine, Minnesota, Nebraska, the last by clear and convincing evidence); and requirements that the parent have denied or unreasonably denied visitation (Mississippi, Oregon, Rhode Island). It also cited Hoff v. Berg (N.D. 1999), which struck down a statute presuming grandparent visitation was in the child's best interests.
  • Why decide as-applied without remand: There was no need to guess at how Washington courts would apply the statute because the trial court actually applied it, and the Washington Supreme Court's broad reading covered that application. Remand would add litigation costs that themselves burden the parental right, a point the plurality drew from Justice Kennedy's dissent.
  • Response to the "chattel" charge: Describing third-party interests as a "burden" on the parent-child relationship was meant to flag the constitutional question, and did not imply children are property.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Souter (concurring in the judgment): Would simply affirm the Washington Supreme Court's facial invalidation on its second ground: a statute letting any person at any time obtain visitation under a free-ranging best-interests standard sweeps too broadly. Meyer's right of upbringing "would be a sham" if it did not cover freedom from judicially compelled visitation whenever a judge thinks he could make a "better" decision. A parent's choice of a child's associates is like the choice of a child's teachers in Pierce. Declined to reach the harm question, the scope of the right, or the trial court's specific application, citing the "treacherous field" of substantive due process (Moore v. East Cleveland).
  • Justice Thomas (concurring in the judgment): Noted that no party argued the substantive due process cases were wrongly decided and expressed no view on that question. Agreed Pierce recognizes a fundamental parental right to rear children, including deciding who educates and socializes them. Faulted the other opinions for not stating a standard of review. Would apply strict scrutiny and concluded the State lacks even a legitimate interest in second-guessing a fit parent's visitation decision.
  • Justice Stevens (dissenting): Would have denied certiorari. Agreed with Souter that the plurality's as-applied review is untenable because state appellate courts should review the trial court's application first, and disagreed with the plurality's reading of the trial record as applying a presumption against the parent. Disagreed with Souter that the state court authoritatively construed the statute, noting "best interest of the child" has developed content in Washington law and elsewhere. Would reject both of the Washington court's grounds: the statute has a "plainly legitimate sweep" (many petitioners are former caregivers or close relatives), and no precedent requires a threshold showing of harm. Stressed that parental rights are limited (Lehr v. Robertson, Michael H. v. Gerald D.), that the child has interests of their own in preserving relationships, and that children are not "so much chattel." Would correct the state court's errors and remand.
  • Justice Scalia (dissenting): Believes parents have an unalienable right to direct their children's upbringing, but the Constitution does not authorize judges to enforce that unenumerated right against duly enacted laws. Only Meyer, Pierce, and Yoder rest on such a right; he would not overrule them, since that was not urged, but would not extend them. Warned that recognizing the right means federal judges will craft family law (defining parents, harm, and gradations of third parties), which state legislatures do better and can correct faster. Would reverse.
  • Justice Kennedy (dissenting): Read the Washington Supreme Court to have held that a harm-to-the-child standard is required in every third-party visitation case, and found that rule too broad. History is inconclusive and nearly every state uses a best-interests standard, so freedom from best-interests review in all cases is not "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" (Glucksberg). A fit parent's rights against a stranger may differ from her rights against another parent or a de facto parent, especially where a third party has served as a caregiver over time. Acknowledged that litigation itself can burden the parental right and that best interests may sometimes give insufficient protection. Would vacate and remand so the state court could address the specific order and any facial challenge first.

Notes

  • Narrow, as-applied holding: The plurality held only that §26.10.160(3) was unconstitutional as applied to Granville and her family. It rested on the statute's breadth plus the trial court's specific application.
  • No majority opinion: Four Justices joined the lead opinion. The two concurrences in the judgment took different routes (Souter: facial overbreadth; Thomas: strict scrutiny), and Thomas noted the opinions do not articulate a standard of review.
  • Expressly left open:
  • Whether due process requires all nonparental visitation statutes to require a showing of harm or potential harm to the child before visitation can be granted.
  • The "precise scope of the parental due process right in the visitation context."
  • Whether specific nonparental visitation statutes are invalid per se; the plurality was "hesitant" to so hold given case-by-case adjudication, citing Fairbanks v. McCarter (Md.) and Williams v. Williams (Va.) as state courts reading best-interests statutes to require more.
  • What the plurality did require: A presumption that fit parents act in their children's best interests, and "at least some special weight" for a fit parent's decision when it is reviewed in court.
  • Relevant factors in the as-applied analysis: Parental fitness; whether the court applied a presumption for or against the parent; whether the parent had already offered visitation or cut it off; the substance of the court's findings.
  • Precedents relied on: Meyer, Pierce, Prince, Stanley, Yoder, Quilloin, Parham (source of the fit-parent presumption), Santosky, Glucksberg, and Reno v. Flores. The plurality agreed with Justice Kennedy that protections here should be "elaborated with care."
  • Overruled or distinguished: The plurality overruled no case and distinguished none. It declined to endorse the Washington Supreme Court's harm requirement.
  • Context noted by the plurality: All 50 states have some form of grandparent visitation statute, driven partly by changes in family structure and grandparents' growing child-rearing role.
