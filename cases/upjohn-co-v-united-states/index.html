Black-Letter Rule

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 501, the corporate attorney-client privilege is not limited to the "control group" of senior managers. Declining to lay down a general rule, the Court held that the control group test cannot govern the development of the law in this area. On the facts before it, the Court held that Upjohn employees' communications to corporate counsel were privileged. Those communications were made at the direction of corporate superiors to secure legal advice, concerned matters within the employees' duties, were made by employees aware of the legal purpose, and were kept confidential. The work-product doctrine applies to IRS summonses, and attorney notes and memoranda of witnesses' oral statements cannot be compelled on a mere showing of substantial need and undue hardship.

Facts

  • Discovery of payments: Independent auditors found that an Upjohn foreign subsidiary made payments to or for foreign government officials to secure government business. They told Gerard Thomas, Upjohn's Vice President, Secretary, and General Counsel.
  • Internal investigation: After consulting outside counsel and the Chairman, Upjohn launched an internal investigation of "questionable payments." Counsel drafted a questionnaire, sent over the Chairman's signature to all foreign general and area managers. It identified Thomas as "the company's General Counsel," referred to "possibly illegal" payments, and instructed recipients to treat the matter as "highly confidential." Responses went directly to Thomas.
  • Interviews: Thomas and outside counsel interviewed the questionnaire recipients and some 33 other officers and employees. A worldwide policy statement accompanying the questionnaire made the legal implications clear.
  • Disclosure to regulators: Upjohn voluntarily filed a preliminary report with the SEC on Form 8-K and sent a copy to the IRS. The IRS opened an investigation into the tax consequences and received lists of everyone interviewed or surveyed.
  • Summons: The IRS issued a summons under 26 U.S.C. § 7602 for the investigation files, including the questionnaires and counsel's interview notes and memoranda. Upjohn refused, citing attorney-client privilege and work product.
  • Access to witnesses: The IRS was free to question the employees and had already interviewed about 25 of them.
  • Content of notes: Thomas testified his notes went beyond recording answers and included his views on the importance of responses, how they related to the inquiry, and further questions to pursue.

Procedural Posture

The government petitioned to enforce the summons in the Western District of Michigan, which adopted a Magistrate's recommendation to enforce it. The Sixth Circuit rejected the Magistrate's waiver finding but held the privilege covered only "control group" communications, remanded to identify that group, and stated in a footnote that work product does not apply to § 7602 summonses; the Supreme Court granted certiorari.

Issue

Does the attorney-client privilege protect communications from a corporation's non-control-group employees to corporate counsel during an internal investigation, and does the work-product doctrine apply in IRS summons enforcement proceedings?

Holding

Yes; in an opinion by Justice Rehnquist, the Court held that the employees' communications to counsel were privileged because the control group test frustrates the privilege's purpose, and that the work-product doctrine applies to IRS summonses with heightened protection for attorney notes of oral witness statements, so the judgment was reversed and remanded.

Reasoning

  • Source of law: FRE 501 directs courts to apply common-law privilege principles "in the light of reason and experience." The privilege is the oldest confidential-communications privilege. Its purpose is to encourage full and frank communication so lawyers can give sound advice that serves the public interest in observing law (Trammel, Fisher, Hunt v. Blackburn).
  • Corporations as clients: The Court had assumed the privilege applies to corporations (United States v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co.), and the government did not contest it.
  • Flaw in the control group test: The test, traced to Philadelphia v. Westinghouse Electric Corp., protects only those who act on advice. The privilege also protects giving information to the lawyer so the lawyer can advise. The Court cited ABA Code EC 4-1: a lawyer should be fully informed of all facts, and confidentiality encourages that.
  • Who has the facts: In a corporation, middle- and lower-level employees often hold the relevant information and can embroil the company in legal trouble. Limiting the privilege puts counsel to a "Hobson's choice" (Diversified Industries v. Meredith).
  • Advice flows down too: Legal advice often matters most to non-control-group employees who carry out policy. The test makes it harder to give them full advice (Duplan Corp.).
  • Compliance: Corporations "constantly go to lawyers to find out how to obey the law" amid complex regulation. The narrow test chills compliance efforts.
  • Predictability: An uncertain privilege "is little better than no privilege at all." The control group test's "substantial role" language produced conflicting results (Hogan v. Zletz vs. Congoleum Industries).
  • Application to these facts: The communications were (1) made by employees to counsel acting as such; (2) at the direction of corporate superiors; (3) to secure legal advice; (4) supplying information unavailable from upper management; (5) on matters within the employees' corporate duties; (6) by employees aware they were questioned so the company could get legal advice; and (7) confidential when made and kept confidential.
  • "Zone of silence" answered: The privilege protects communications only. The underlying facts stay discoverable from the employees themselves, so the adversary is no worse off than if the communications never occurred. Government convenience does not overcome the privilege's policies (Jackson, J., concurring in Hickman: no performing "on wits borrowed from the adversary").
  • Government's incentive argument rejected (footnote): Liability risk alone would not ensure deep, quality compliance investigations, and the argument would apply equally to individual clients, where the privilege is settled.
  • Case-by-case approach: Consistent with FRE 501 and its legislative history, the Court declined to draft general rules. It did hold that the control group test cannot govern the law in this area.
  • Work product applies to IRS summonses: The government conceded the point. Tax summonses remain subject to "traditional privileges and limitations" (Euge). Nothing in the statute or history excludes work product, and FRCP 81(a)(3) makes the Civil Rules, including Rule 26(b)(3), applicable to summons enforcement (Donaldson).
  • Heightened protection for oral-statement memoranda: Hickman's necessity exception did not reach attorney notes of witnesses' oral statements, which reveal the attorney's mental processes. Rule 26(b)(3)'s second sentence protects "mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal theories," and the Advisory Committee Notes show this material was in view.
  • Magistrate's error: The Magistrate applied only the ordinary "substantial need" and "undue hardship" standard. Notes revealing communications are privileged here. Notes not revealing communications reveal counsel's mental processes and need a "far stronger showing of necessity and unavailability by other means."

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Chief Justice Burger, concurring in part and concurring in the judgment: Joined Parts I and III and agreed with rejecting the control group test and with the result in Part II. Argued the Court should announce a governing standard because predictability is the privilege's point. Proposed that a communication is privileged at least when an employee or former employee speaks at management's direction with an attorney about conduct or proposed conduct within the scope of employment. The attorney must be authorized by management to inquire and must seek information to (a) evaluate whether the employee's conduct has bound or would bind the corporation, (b) assess the conduct's legal consequences, or (c) formulate legal responses to actions others have taken or may take regarding that conduct. He criticized the majority for acknowledging uncertainty while declining to cure it.

Notes

  • Narrow, case-by-case holding: The Court expressly declined to set a general test for corporate privilege. It rejected the control group test and held these communications privileged on these facts.
  • Left open, former employees: Seven of 86 interviewees had left Upjohn before their interviews. The Court declined to decide whether the privilege covers their communications because the lower courts had not addressed it. The work-product analysis would apply to those notes if the privilege does not.
  • Left open, absolute protection: The Court did not decide whether opinion work product based on oral witness statements is absolutely protected. It noted courts split between absolute protection (In re Grand Jury Proceedings, CA8; In re Grand Jury Investigation, ED Pa.) and special protection discoverable only in a "rare situation" (In re Grand Jury Investigation, CA3; cf. In re Grand Jury Subpoena, CA2).
  • Facts vs. communications: The privilege does not shield underlying facts (Philadelphia v. Westinghouse, 205 F. Supp. 830; Diversified Industries; State ex rel. Dudek).
  • Waiver: The Sixth Circuit rejected the Magistrate's waiver finding; the Supreme Court did not revisit waiver.
  • Rejected: The control group test from Philadelphia v. Westinghouse Electric Corp., 210 F. Supp. 483, as adopted by the Sixth Circuit.
  • Relied on: FRE 501; Trammel v. United States; Fisher v. United States; Hunt v. Blackburn; United States v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co.; ABA Code EC 4-1; Diversified Industries, Inc. v. Meredith; Duplan Corp. v. Deering Milliken; United States v. United States Gypsum Co.; United States v. Gillock; Hickman v. Taylor; United States v. Nobles; United States v. Euge; Donaldson v. United States; FRCP 26(b)(3) and 81(a)(3).
  • Distinguished: The government's reliance on Hickman's necessity language, which the Court read as excluding attorney memoranda of oral witness statements.
  • PR link: The Court grounds the privilege partly in the lawyer's ethical duty to be fully informed and to hold client confidences inviolate (EC 4-1), and in corporate counsel's role in securing legal compliance.
