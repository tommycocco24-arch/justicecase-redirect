Black-Letter Rule

The Sixth Amendment creates a presumption in favor of a criminal defendant's counsel of choice. A trial court may overcome that presumption, and refuse a defendant's waiver of conflict-free counsel, on a showing of an actual conflict or of a serious potential for conflict. Trial courts get substantial latitude in making this call, and the evaluation of each case is left primarily to the trial court's informed judgment.

Facts

  • Parties: Wheat was charged with numerous codefendants in a large marijuana distribution conspiracy. He acted mainly as an intermediary, receiving, storing, and distributing shipments from his home.
  • Iredale's other clients: Attorney Eugene Iredale represented codefendants Gomez-Barajas and Bravo.
  • Gomez-Barajas: Iredale won his acquittal on drug charges overlapping with Wheat's. He then offered to plead guilty to tax evasion and illegal importation. The court had not accepted the plea when Wheat's trial began, so he could still withdraw it and go to trial.
  • Bravo: A lesser player, Bravo pleaded guilty to transporting about 2,400 pounds of marijuana to a residence controlled by Victor Vidal.
  • Request for Iredale: At the end of Bravo's plea proceedings, Iredale told the court Wheat had asked him to try Wheat's case. The hearing on the substitution was held one day before trial.
  • Government's objection: (1) If Gomez-Barajas's plea fell through, Wheat would likely testify against him, and Iredale could not cross-examine Wheat. (2) The Government believed some of Bravo's marijuana reached Wheat and arranged for Bravo to testify against Wheat in exchange for a modified sentencing position. Iredale could not ethically cross-examine Bravo meaningfully.
  • Waivers: Wheat, Gomez-Barajas, and Bravo all agreed to Iredale's representation and to waive future conflict claims. Wheat argued the conflicts were speculative and manufactured to disqualify an effective lawyer.
  • Ruling: The court found an "irreconcilable conflict of interest" that could not be waived and denied the substitution. Wheat went to trial with his original counsel and was convicted.
  • Bravo testified: Per the majority's footnote, Bravo was in fact called at trial to show drug transportation the prosecution hoped to link to Wheat.

Procedural Posture

The District Court denied Wheat's request to substitute Iredale, and Wheat was convicted. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding the District Court did not abuse its discretion, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a circuit split on when a court may override a defendant's waiver of a conflict.

Issue

Does a district court violate a defendant's Sixth Amendment right to counsel of choice by refusing his waiver of conflict-free counsel and denying substitution of an attorney who represented codefendants in the same conspiracy?

Holding

No; the presumption in favor of counsel of choice can be overcome by an actual conflict or a serious potential for conflict, the District Court's refusal here was within its discretion, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed (opinion by Chief Justice Rehnquist).

Reasoning

  • Purpose of the Sixth Amendment: The right to counsel exists to ensure a fair trial (Strickland), and the inquiry focuses on the adversarial process, not the defendant's relationship with his lawyer (Cronic). The Amendment's essential aim is an effective advocate for each defendant. It does not guarantee that a defendant will inexorably get the lawyer he prefers (Morris v. Slappy; Jones v. Barnes).
  • Choice is already limited: A defendant cannot insist on (1) a non-lawyer advocate, (2) a lawyer he cannot afford or who declines, or (3) a lawyer with a previous or ongoing relationship with an opposing party, including the Government.
  • Multiple representation is dangerous: Joint representation is not per se unconstitutional, but a court alerted to possible conflicts must take adequate steps to decide whether separate counsel is warranted (Holloway; Cuyler v. Sullivan). Conflicts can stop a lawyer from challenging evidence harmful to one client or from shifting culpability between clients at sentencing (Holloway).
  • Waiver does not cure the problem: No flat rule that waivers end the inquiry follows from the presumption of counsel of choice. Federal courts have an independent interest in trials conducted within the profession's ethical standards and in proceedings that appear fair to observers. The ABA Model Code (DR 5-105(C)), ABA Model Rules (Rule 1.7), and California's rules all limit multiple representation. The institutional interest in just verdicts is also at stake.
  • Rule 44(c): Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 44(c) requires courts to inquire into joint representation and take appropriate measures to protect each defendant's right to counsel. The Advisory Committee Notes suggest ordering separate representation. This fits the courts' independent duty under Holloway and Glasser.
  • The "whipsaw": Courts face error claims either way. Allow the joint representation and the defendant may later claim ineffective assistance (Burger v. Kemp). Refuse it and he raises a choice-of-counsel claim. Courts of Appeals have entertained ineffective-assistance claims even from defendants who waived, and the Court notes this without passing on it.
  • Actual conflict: Where a court justifiably finds an actual conflict, it may decline a waiver and insist on separate representation (quoting United States v. Dolan (3d Cir.)).
  • Potential conflict and pretrial uncertainty: Courts must rule before trial, when conflicts are hard to predict. Clients rarely tell lawyers everything, witness testimony is unknown, and a small development can shift relationships among defendants. These risks are hard to explain to a lay defendant. A lawyer's willingness to obtain waivers may bear an inverse relation to how carefully he explains them.
  • Result: Courts therefore get substantial latitude to refuse waivers in rare actual-conflict cases and in the more common cases of potential conflict.
  • Manufactured conflicts: The Government may try to manufacture a conflict to remove able defense counsel. Trial courts are aware of this and must weigh it with the other factors.
  • Application: This was not two coequal defendants in a simple case. Iredale proposed to defend three conspirators of varying stature in a complex scheme. The Government planned to call Bravo and might tie his deliveries to Wheat, requiring cross-examination Iredale could not ethically provide. If Gomez-Barajas's plea were rejected, Wheat's probable testimony would put Iredale in an ethical dilemma. The motion came close to trial, and the court relied on experience-based judgment.
  • No single right answer: Other courts might have ruled the opposite way with equal justification. That does not make one result right and the other wrong.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Marshall, joined by Justice Brennan, dissenting: Agrees that the presumption favors counsel of choice and can be overcome only by a substantial potential for a serious conflict. Rejects deference. Whether facts show such a potential is a mixed question of law and fact, which appellate courts traditionally review without deference (Townsend v. Sain; Sumner v. Mata; Cuyler v. Sullivan). Constitutional stakes call for close appellate scrutiny (Bose Corp.). Trial courts should make record findings. Even under the majority's standard, reversal is required. The Gomez-Barajas conflict rested on dubious speculation: the plea was likely to be accepted (and later was), and Wheat had no apparent link to the remaining charges. The Bravo conflict was illusory: Bravo did not know Wheat, his testimony never mentioned Wheat, he was not cross-examined, and this was predictable. The court could have added Iredale and barred him from cross-examining Bravo. Footnotes: his position addresses only cases where all parties made fully informed waivers; the difficulty of prediction does not justify lax review; the prosecutor's eleventh-hour decision to call Bravo suggests a manufactured conflict aimed at a formidable lawyer; timing did not matter because Iredale sought no continuance and the court ruled on the merits.
  • Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Blackmun, dissenting: Criticizes the Court's "paternalistic" view of the right to choose a lawyer. Agrees with Marshall that the Court exaggerated the conflict. Stresses that the waivers were informed and voluntary and that Wheat's existing counsel advised him on the waiver and would have stayed on the defense. The real question was whether Wheat could add counsel of choice. Accepts that district judges deserve wide latitude, but finds a clear abuse of discretion requiring reversal. Deference makes sense because trial judges know more about the defendant's ability to waive (including undue influence by a codefendant), the lawyers' character, the case facts, and the availability of comparable alternative counsel.

Notes

  • Scope of holding: The Court affirms under a deferential standard. It says other courts could reasonably have ruled the other way.
  • Standard: Presumption for counsel of choice, overcome by (1) actual conflict, or (2) a serious potential for conflict. Courts must also consider the possibility that the Government manufactured the conflict.
  • Limited grant: Certiorari was limited to the waiver/substitution issue. The Court did not reach the Ninth Circuit's rulings on the conference held in Wheat's absence or on Wheat's claimed right to a plea bargain.
  • Left open: The Court noted, without passing judgment, that Courts of Appeals entertain ineffective-assistance claims from defendants who waived conflict-free counsel.
  • Circuit split (footnote): Cites differing standards: In re Paradyne Corp. (11th Cir.) (right to counsel does not override societal interests); In re Grand Jury Subpoena Served Upon Doe (2d Cir.) (disqualify where public interest outweighs the right); United States v. Reese (6th Cir.) (override waiver only in "compelling circumstances"); United States v. Flanagan (3d Cir.) (refuse waiver when actual conflict "very likely"), rev'd on other grounds.
  • Distinguished: Faretta v. California (right of self-representation) does not include a right to choose any advocate when the defendant elects counsel.
  • Relied on: Holloway v. Arkansas; Cuyler v. Sullivan; Glasser v. United States; Strickland v. Washington; United States v. Cronic; Morris v. Slappy; Jones v. Barnes; Powell v. Alabama; Gideon v. Wainwright; United States v. Morrison; United States v. Ash; Burger v. Kemp; United States v. Dolan (3d Cir.).
  • Ethics rules invoked: ABA Model Code DR 5-105(C); ABA Model Rules 1.7; California Rules of Professional Conduct 5 and 7. The Court treats these as grounds for the court's independent interest in ethical trials.
  • Overruled: None stated.
