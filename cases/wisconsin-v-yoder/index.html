Black-Letter Rule

When parents' traditional interest in directing the religious upbringing of their children is combined with a free exercise claim, the State must show more than a "reasonable relation to some purpose within the competency of the State" to enforce a generally applicable law against them. Only state interests "of the highest order and those not otherwise served" can overbalance a legitimate free exercise claim. Parental power remains subject to limitation under Prince v. Massachusetts if parental decisions will jeopardize the child's health or safety or have a potential for significant social burdens.

Facts

  • Parties: Respondents Jonas Yoder and Wallace Miller (Old Order Amish) and Adin Yutzy (Conservative Amish Mennonite) lived in Green County, Wisconsin. Petitioner is the State of Wisconsin.
  • The statute: Wis. Stat. § 118.15 required anyone controlling a child between 7 and 16 to cause the child to attend public or private school. Violators faced fines or up to 3 months' imprisonment.
  • The refusal: Respondents' children, ages 14 and 15, had completed the eighth grade in public school. Respondents would not send them to high school. The children were in no private school and no recognized exception.
  • Religious basis: Respondents believed high school attendance would endanger their own salvation and their children's. The State stipulated that the beliefs were sincere.
  • Expert testimony (uncontradicted): High school values (competition, worldly success, self-distinction) conflict with Amish values. Removing adolescents from the community during the formative years would interfere with their preparation for adult baptism. Compulsory high school could cause psychological harm and ultimately destroy the Old Order Amish community. The Amish "learning-by-doing" vocational system was described as "ideal."
  • Amish acceptance of grades 1-8: The Amish accept elementary education in the "three R's" so children can read the Bible, farm, and deal with non-Amish people.
  • Community record: The Green County Amish had never been known to commit crimes, receive public assistance, or be unemployed.
  • Failed compromise: Before trial, respondents proposed a vocational plan modeled on Pennsylvania's Amish vocational school. The State Superintendent rejected it as not "substantially equivalent."
  • Child's testimony: Only Frieda Yoder testified. She said her own religious beliefs were the only reason she stopped attending school. The other children were not called.

Procedural Posture

The Green County Court convicted respondents and fined each $5. It found the law interfered with their sincere religious beliefs but held it a reasonable and constitutional exercise of state power. The Wisconsin Circuit Court affirmed. The Wisconsin Supreme Court reversed under the Free Exercise Clause, and the U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari on the State's petition.

Issue

Do the First and Fourteenth Amendments bar Wisconsin from criminally compelling Amish parents to send their children to formal high school until age 16 when doing so conflicts with the parents' sincere religious beliefs?

Holding

Yes. Because the compulsory-attendance law severely burdened the Amish parents' free exercise of religion and the State failed to show with particularity how its interest in education would be harmed by exempting the Amish after the eighth grade, the State cannot compel respondents to cause their children to attend formal high school to age 16; affirmed (opinion by Chief Justice Burger; Justices Powell and Rehnquist took no part).

Reasoning

  • State power over education is strong but balanced. States may impose reasonable regulations on basic education. Under Pierce v. Society of Sisters, even that paramount interest yielded to parents' right to provide an equivalent private education. The State's interest in universal education is "not totally free from a balancing process" when it impinges on free exercise and parental interests in religious upbringing.
  • The framework. To compel attendance against a free exercise claim, the State must show either (1) its requirement does not deny free exercise, or (2) a state interest "of sufficient magnitude to override" the free exercise interest. Only interests "of the highest order and those not otherwise served" suffice (Sherbert v. Verner).
  • Step 1: The claim must be religious. A way of life based on purely secular considerations gets no protection. Thoreau's isolation at Walden Pond was "philosophical and personal," so it would not qualify. The Amish way of life is a deep religious conviction, shared by an organized group, intimately tied to daily living, and grounded in the Biblical command "be not conformed to this world."
  • Step 2: The burden is severe and inescapable. The law compels Amish parents, under threat of criminal sanction, to act against fundamental tenets (Braunfeld v. Brown). It also poses an objective danger: Amish families must either abandon their beliefs and assimilate or migrate elsewhere. Forced migration of religious minorities was an evil at the heart of the Religion Clauses.
  • Belief/action distinction rejected. Wisconsin argued that only belief is protected. The Court held that religiously grounded conduct can be protected even from generally applicable regulation (Sherbert, Murdock, Cantwell). Here "belief and action cannot be neatly confined in logic-tight compartments."
  • Facial neutrality does not end the inquiry. A neutral law "may, in its application, nonetheless offend the constitutional requirement for governmental neutrality if it unduly burdens the free exercise of religion."
  • Step 3: Searching examination of the State's interests. The Court weighed the State's interests against the impediment that an Amish exemption would cause.
  • Citizenship: The Court accepted that education prepares citizens for political life. But one or two extra years of high school would add little for a community that has functioned as self-sufficient, law-abiding citizens for over 200 years in this country.
  • Self-reliance and "ignorance": The record refutes the "ignorance" charge. The Amish provide continuing vocational education after the eighth grade, and Congress recognized their self-sufficiency through the Social Security tax exemption.
  • Children who leave the faith: "Highly speculative." There is no specific evidence of attrition or that departing youth would become burdens on society, and no showing that one or two more years of school would fix any such problem.
  • History: Compulsory education beyond the eighth grade is recent. Wisconsin itself required only eighth grade until 1933.
  • Child labor link: The age-16 limit partly reflects child labor concerns. Supervised work on the family farm lies "at the periphery" of those concerns and displaces no adult workers.
  • *Parens patriae and Prince. The State relied on Prince to assert the child's right to secondary education and its own parens patriae power. The Court read Prince narrowly. Sherbert* confined it to conduct posing "some substantial threat to public safety, peace or order," and no harm to the child's physical or mental health or to public welfare was shown here.
  • Parental rights as the core. "The primary role of the parents in the upbringing of their children is now established beyond debate as an enduring American tradition." Pierce "stands as a charter of the rights of parents to direct the religious upbringing of their children." The duty to prepare the child for "additional obligations" includes inculcating moral standards, religious beliefs, and citizenship.
  • State would control the child's religious future. If the State could "save" the child from the Amish parents through two more years of school, it would "in large measure influence, if not determine, the religious future of the child." The same argument would reach all church schools short of college.
  • Burden on the Amish was met. The Amish showed sincerity, the link between belief and way of life, the role of that link in community survival, and the hazard of enforcement. They also met the "even more difficult burden" of proving that their alternative education serves the State's own stated interests. Given that showing and the minimal gap between what the State required and what the Amish accepted, the State had to show "with more particularity" how an exemption would harm its interest.
  • Establishment Clause (footnote). The exemption does not sponsor, finance, or actively involve the State in religion (Walz v. Tax Commission). It reflects the "governmental obligation of neutrality in the face of religious differences" (Sherbert).

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Justice Stewart, joined by Justice Brennan (concurring): The case involves only the criminal punishment of parents. Nothing in the record shows the children's beliefs differ from their parents'. Frieda Yoder testified that religion was her only reason for leaving school. The record therefore does not present the child-rights issue in Part II of Justice Douglas's dissent.
  • Justice White, joined by Justices Brennan and Stewart (concurring): Joins because the question is close and the State's interest in two more years (ninth and tenth grades) does not outweigh the sincere Amish practice. The case would be "very different" if the religion forbade all schooling and all compliance with state standards. Pierce does not let parents replace state standards with "idiosyncratic views"; the schools in Pierce met every state standard. The State has a legitimate interest in developing children's talents and preparing them for a life they may later choose, and the record shows many children leave the faith. Still, the State did not show that Amish children leaving after eighth grade would be "intellectually stultified." Any entanglement from administering the exemption is permissible because it is needed to protect free exercise. Analysis would differ if the practice threatened public safety, peace, or order.
  • Justice Douglas (dissenting in part): The Court wrongly treats only parent and State interests as at stake. The parents' motion to dismiss raised the children's religious liberty too, and as in Prince, the child has no other effective forum. A mature child who wants high school may have that wish respected even over parental religious objections. Children are "persons" under the Bill of Rights (Haley, Gault, Winship, Tinker, Barnette). It is "the future of the student, not the future of the parents, that is imperiled." Because Frieda Yoder testified that her views matched her parents', he joins the judgment as to Jonas Yoder. He dissents as to Yutzy and Miller, whose children were never heard, and would reserve the issue for new hearings on remand. He also criticizes the majority's reliance on the Amish "law and order" record as irrelevant to whether something is a religion. He says the Thoreau passage retreats from the broad definitions of religion in United States v. Seeger and Welsh v. United States. He reads the majority as departing from Reynolds v. United States and predicts Reynolds may in time be overruled.

Notes

  • Narrow holding. The Amish made a showing "that probably few other religious groups or sects could make." The Court stressed three centuries of history, uncontested sincerity, and a proven alternative education. It also emphasized that this was not a group that had "recently discovered some 'progressive'" child-rearing method.
  • Limited deviation. The holding covers one or two years past the eighth grade, for children who completed grades 1-8.
  • Judicial restraint. Courts "are not school boards or legislatures" and must "move with great circumspection" when weighing religious exemption claims against educational requirements.
  • State regulation preserved. The ruling does not undermine the general applicability of compulsory-attendance laws. States may still set reasonable standards for Amish vocational education under parental and church guidance, if consistent with the opinion. The Court assumed Wisconsin could reach an accommodation like the Pennsylvania plan.
  • Expressly left open: How to resolve competing interests of parents, children, and the State where the State claims Amish parents are keeping children out of high school "despite their expressed desires to the contrary." The children were not parties, and the State never tried the case on that theory. The Court said such state intrusion would itself raise "grave questions of religious freedom."
  • *The Prince limit.* Parental power linked to free exercise may still be limited where decisions jeopardize the child's health or safety or create significant social burdens. No such harm was shown here.
  • Relied on: Pierce v. Society of Sisters (parental right to direct upbringing and education; read as a "charter" of parental religious-upbringing rights); Meyer v. Nebraska; Sherbert v. Verner (compelling interest balancing; neutral laws can unduly burden; narrowing of Prince); Braunfeld v. Brown (direct criminal compulsion); Murdock v. Pennsylvania and Cantwell v. Connecticut (protected religious conduct); Walz v. Tax Commission (Establishment Clause concerns; "tight rope"); Lemon v. Kurtzman and Tilton v. Richardson (zealous protection of Religion Clause values).
  • Distinguished/narrowed: Prince v. Massachusetts, confined to conduct posing a substantial threat to public safety, peace, or order, and involving child labor evils absent here. The opinion cites Reynolds, Gillette, and Jacobson as examples of permissible regulation of religious conduct. A footnote cites state cases rejecting similar Amish claims (State v. Garber, State v. Hershberger, Commonwealth v. Beiler).
  • Overruled: None.
  • Standing frame: The majority rests on the parents' free exercise right because "it is the parents who are subject to prosecution here." The holding "in no degree depends on the assertion of the religious interest of the child."
