Black-Letter Rule

The right to marry is of fundamental importance. A statutory classification that significantly interferes with the decision to marry cannot be upheld unless it is supported by sufficiently important state interests and is closely tailored to effectuate only those interests. Reasonable regulations that do not significantly interfere with decisions to enter into marriage may still be imposed without rigorous scrutiny.

Facts

  • Statute: Wis. Stat. § 245.10 barred any Wisconsin resident with minor children not in their custody, and under a court order to support them, from marrying in Wisconsin or elsewhere without a court order. The court could grant permission only if the applicant proved compliance with the support obligation and showed the children "are not then and are not likely thereafter to become public charges."
  • Penalties: No license could issue without the order. Marriages entered in violation were void, and obtaining a license in violation was a crime.
  • Redhail's support order: As a minor high school student, Redhail was adjudged the father of a child born out of wedlock and ordered to pay $109 per month. From May 1972 to August 1974, Redhail was unemployed and indigent and paid nothing. The arrearage exceeded $3,700 by December 1974.
  • Public charge: The child had received AFDC benefits since birth and would have remained a public charge even if Redhail had been current on payments.
  • License denial: The Milwaukee County Clerk, Zablocki, denied Redhail's marriage license application solely for lack of a court order. The parties stipulated that Redhail could not have met either statutory prerequisite.
  • Intended marriage: Redhail and the woman Redhail wished to marry were expecting a child and wanted to marry before the birth.

Procedural Posture

Redhail filed a § 1983 class action seeking declaratory and injunctive relief. A three-judge district court applied strict scrutiny, held the statute violated the Equal Protection Clause, and enjoined enforcement; the clerk appealed directly to the Supreme Court, which noted probable jurisdiction.

Issue

Does a state statute that bars noncustodial parents under court-ordered support obligations from marrying, unless they prove compliance and that their children will not become public charges, violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment?

Holding

Yes. Because § 245.10 directly and substantially interferes with the fundamental right to marry, and the means chosen unnecessarily impinge on that right given the State's asserted interests, the classification fails critical examination under the Equal Protection Clause; the judgment of the District Court is affirmed (opinion by Justice Marshall).

Reasoning

  • Framework: Under equal protection, the Court first determines the burden of justification by looking to the nature of the classification and the individual interests affected (Memorial Hospital v. Maricopa County). Because the right to marry is fundamental and the statute significantly interferes with it, "critical examination" of the state interests is required (Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia; San Antonio Independent School Dist. v. Rodriguez).
  • Marriage as fundamental: Loving v. Virginia is the leading case. Loving could have rested on race discrimination alone, but it also held the freedom to marry a fundamental liberty under the Due Process Clause. The Court reads Loving as applying to all individuals, citing Maynard v. Hill ("the most important relation in life"), Meyer v. Nebraska, and Skinner v. Oklahoma.
  • Privacy line of cases: The right to marry is part of the fundamental right of privacy implicit in the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. The Court relies on Griswold v. Connecticut, Carey v. Population Services International (quoting Roe v. Wade), Cleveland Board of Education v. LaFleur, Moore v. East Cleveland, and Smith v. Organization of Foster Families. A footnote adds Boddie v. Connecticut and United States v. Kras as further support for marriage's fundamental status.
  • Logic of placing marriage with other family decisions: It would make little sense to protect decisions about procreation, childbirth, and child rearing but leave unprotected the decision to enter the relationship that founds the family. Redhail's partner had a right to seek an abortion or to bear a child who would face the disabilities of illegitimacy (Roe; Trimble v. Gordon; Weber v. Aetna). A decision to marry and raise the child in a traditional family setting deserves equivalent protection. Because Wisconsin criminalized fornication, a right to procreate must imply some right to enter the only relationship in which the State permits sexual relations.
  • Threshold limit: Not every regulation touching the incidents of or prerequisites for marriage gets rigorous scrutiny. Only regulations that significantly interfere with the decision to marry do (distinguishing Califano v. Jobst).
  • Why this interference is direct and substantial: Some people, like Redhail, can never get the order and are absolutely barred from marrying. Others could comply in theory but will be burdened enough to be coerced into forgoing marriage. Even those who comply suffer a serious intrusion into a fundamental freedom of choice.
  • State interests: The State asserted (1) counseling applicants about their prior support obligations and (2) protecting the welfare of out-of-custody children. The Court accepted both as legitimate and substantial for present purposes but held the means unnecessarily impinge on the right to marry.
  • Counseling rationale fails: The enacted statute provides for no counseling and no automatic permission after counseling. The record contains no evidence that counseling occurs. Counseling could never justify withholding permission once it is done.
  • "Collection device" rationale fails:
  • For applicants unable to pay, the statute just blocks the marriage and delivers no money to the children.
  • For everyone, Wisconsin already has other means at least as effective that do not burden marriage: wage assignments, civil contempt, and criminal penalties. If the State wants noncustodial parents to keep children off public assistance, it can adjust the criteria for setting support amounts.
  • "Preventing new obligations" rationale fails:
  • Grossly underinclusive: the statute limits no new financial commitments except those from the marriage itself.
  • Substantially overinclusive: a new spouse may improve the applicant's finances and ability to pay prior support.
  • Blocking the marriage may simply produce children born out of wedlock, as happened here. The support obligation is the same either way, so the net result is more illegitimate children.

Concurrences & Dissents

  • Chief Justice Burger (concurring, joins the majority): Sees no significant inconsistency with Califano v. Jobst. The Wisconsin statute was an intentional and substantial interference with the right to marry. The Social Security provisions in Jobst were no "attempt to interfere" with the marriage decision and had at most an indirect impact. Joins on that understanding.
  • Justice Stewart (concurring in the judgment): Rejects the equal protection rationale. The Equal Protection Clause addresses invidiously discriminatory classifications, and calling this statute a "classification" is "little short of fantasy." Denies a constitutional "right to marry"; marriage is a privilege defined and limited by state law, and States may absolutely prohibit some marriages (siblings, those under 14, those failing a venereal disease exam, bigamists). Freedom of personal choice in marriage is still a due process liberty, and the statute directly abridges it. Relying on Boddie, Tate v. Short, and Williams v. Illinois, inability to pay cannot justify total deprivation of a protected liberty. As applied to indigents the statute is irrational. The State's interest in the financial soundness of future marriages cannot justify telling people they are too poor to marry. Argues the majority's equal protection analysis is "substantive due process by another name."
  • Justice Powell (concurring in the judgment): The majority sweeps too broadly and gives no principled way to separate "direct and substantial" interference from reasonable regulation, since marriage regulation usually takes the form of prerequisites or barriers. Reads Loving as about a wholly unsupportable racial classification, saying nothing about scrutiny for "supportable" restrictions. Starts from domestic relations as a "virtually exclusive province of the States" (Sosna v. Iowa; Pennoyer v. Neff; Maynard v. Hill) and warns that compelling-interest review would cast doubt on bans on incest and bigamy, blood tests, and fault-based divorce. Applies due process limits (Moore; Boddie's monopolization principle) and equal protection's "fair and substantial relation" test (Reed v. Reed; Craig v. Boren). Disagrees that alternative collection methods foreclose conditioning marriage on support payments: the Constitution does not bar the device against those able but unwilling to pay. The defect is the failure to provide for the truly indigent, compounded by the "unprecedented" public-charge requirement. The new-obligations rationale fails as grossly underinclusive. In a footnote, notes Boddie was an as-applied challenge, but facial invalidation is appropriate here because the statute lacks a severability clause and the legislature provided a stopgap (§ 245.105) if § 245.10 is enjoined. Also flags a serious procedural due process question from the standardless "likely to become public charges" prediction.
  • Justice Stevens (concurring in the judgment): Writes to address tension with Jobst. Classifications based on marital status (tax, Selective Service, Social Security) differ fundamentally from classifications deciding who may lawfully marry. The latter remain open to evenhanded regulation (bans on marrying a child, a close relative, or a person with venereal disease). This statute is different because it makes economic status determine eligibility to marry: among parents who have met their court-ordered obligations, the rich may marry and the poor may not. Calls that discrimination "totally unprecedented." The statute rests on sex-role assumptions (only fathers affected; they never marry employed women), exempts custodial parents facing the same budget strain, and misses the poorest parents, who are unlikely to be under support orders. The public-charge provision is "futile or perverse" in many applications. Rejects both strict scrutiny that foreordains invalidity and a rational basis test satisfied by "occasional and random benefit." Expressly leaves open whether any limit on the right to marry may rest on economic status.
  • Justice Rehnquist (dissenting): Agrees with Powell that marriage does not invariably trigger the strictest scrutiny but rejects Powell's intermediate standard and the Stewart and Powell indigency exceptions. Applies rational basis under both clauses (Dandridge v. Williams; Williamson v. Lee Optical). Jobst controls: a statute is judged by typical cases, not "selected, atypical examples," and the difference between legal impossibility here and practical impossibility in Jobst does not justify heightened review. The State has an exceptionally strong interest in collecting support given limited funds. Even under the concurrences' view, the stipulated record shows indigency only through August 1974, not at the license denial, complaint, or judgment, so Redhail never showed the statute unconstitutional as applied (citing Broadrick v. Oklahoma's standing rule). Would reverse. Leaves open whether a class may get relief when the named representative never had a valid claim.

Notes

  • Ground of decision: The majority rests on equal protection. Redhail urged substantive due process in the alternative, but the Court affirmed on the equal protection ground the District Court used. The District Court had also found a separate basis for strict scrutiny in wealth discrimination; the majority reports that ground without adopting it.
  • Limit on scrutiny: Rigorous scrutiny applies only to regulations that "significantly interfere" with decisions to marry. Reasonable regulations of the incidents of or prerequisites for marriage remain permissible.
  • State interests: The Court accepted the asserted interests as legitimate and substantial "for present purposes." Invalidation turns on the poor fit of the means.
  • Distinguished: Califano v. Jobst (upholding termination of Social Security child benefits upon marriage). Jobst placed no direct legal obstacle to marriage, showed no evidence of significant deterrence, and did not stop Jobst from marrying.
  • Relied on: Loving v. Virginia (leading right-to-marry case), Maynard v. Hill, Meyer v. Nebraska, Skinner v. Oklahoma, Griswold v. Connecticut, Carey v. Population Services International, Roe v. Wade, Cleveland Board of Education v. LaFleur, Moore v. East Cleveland, Smith v. Organization of Foster Families, Whalen v. Roe, Paul v. Davis; Boddie v. Connecticut and United States v. Kras (footnote); Memorial Hospital, Murgia, Rodriguez, Bullock v. Carter (scrutiny framework); Trimble v. Gordon and Weber v. Aetna (illegitimacy).
  • Abstention: Younger and Huffman did not apply because no state proceeding was pending (Wooley v. Maynard). The statute had no ambiguities, and § 1983 plaintiffs need not first go to state court (Wisconsin v. Constantineau; Zwickler v. Koota). Burford abstention was inapplicable because no complex state-law issues were involved, and no doctrine requires abstention just because a federal ruling may overturn state policy.
  • Class action issues: The clerk lacked standing to argue that due process required prejudgment notice to absent defendant-class members because the clerk had no "personal stake in the outcome" (Baker v. Carr): the clerk would be bound regardless of the judgment's effect on absent members, claimed no injury from the defendant-class format, and never opposed the defendant class below despite being invited to. As a result, absent members must assert their own due process rights, through collateral attack or otherwise (Hansberry v. Lee); the Court added that the question may be wholly academic. The clerk did not appeal the findings that the defendant class satisfied Rules 23(a) and (b)(2), the class definition covering all county clerks, or the requirement to send each clerk a copy of the judgment.
  • Mootness: Redhail's later Illinois marriage did not moot the case because it was void under § 245.10's extraterritorial reach, and the class claim remained live (Franks v. Bowman; Sosna v. Iowa). Wisconsin's new § 245.105, enforceable only while § 245.10 is stayed or enjoined, also did not moot the case.
  • Expressly left open: The Court expressed no judgment on the constitutionality of new § 245.105.
